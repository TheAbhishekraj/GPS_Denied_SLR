Abstract— With the rapid advancement of autonomous 
flight technology, there is an increasing demand for higher 
precision and advanced navigation techniques, with permissible 
distance errors often restricted to a few meters. Furthermore, 
the ubiquitous deployment of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles 
(UAVs) necessitates the integration of novel technologies to 
ensure operational continuity under adverse conditions, such as 
environmental signal interference, hostile attacks, or traversal 
through zones of complete signal loss. This study presents a 
methodology to address these challenges, enabling the 
maintenance of coordinates and navigation for UAVs to traverse 
jammed or completely out-of-coverage zones, thereby avoiding 
the need for emergency landings or Return-to-Home (RTH) 
protocols common in current UAV systems. The proposed 
approach leverages the TransGAN model, a framework 
typically employed for data analysis comprising a Generator 
and a Discriminator. In this context, the model processes 
sequential real-world coordinate data. Under normal GNSS 
operation, TransGAN is trained as a high-precision prediction 
model utilizing velocity and coordinate data as inputs. 
Conversely, during GNSS outages or interference, the trained 
TransGAN model is utilized to generate coordinates, thereby 
maintaining navigation capabilities for the UAV.

I.
INTRODUCTION 
Rapid advancements in autonomous systems demand 
highly accurate Positioning, Navigation, and Timing (PNT) 
data, shrinking acceptable error margins to mere meters. 
While Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) are 
ubiquitous, their fundamental acquisition principles remain 
unchanged. In UAV operations, civilian GPS signals—
broadcasted at low power over wide bandwidths without 
dedicated security—are highly susceptible to interference, 
spoofing, and complete signal loss in complex environments. 
Conversely, Inertial Navigation Systems (INS) provide 
autonomous spatial coordinates but suffer from accumulated 
measurement errors leading to positional drift. Integrating INS 
with GPS mitigates these issues [1]–[3], offering enhanced 
accuracy, robust signal tracking, fault tolerance, and anti-
jamming capabilities [4]. Despite this integration, maintaining 
reliability in heavily jammed or GNSS-denied zones 
(common in military contexts) remains a critical challenge for 
UAVs. Current countermeasures, such as fiber-optic tethers, 
are constrained by short operational ranges (< 20 km) and 
payload limits. Consequently, when standard radio-controlled 
UAVs lose GPS, mission continuity is severely compromised. 
To address this limitation, we propose a novel trajectory 
reconstruction method utilizing a Transformer Generative 
Adversarial Network (TransGAN). Trained on real-world 
coordinate sequences acquired via a standard, cost-effective 
NEO-M8N module, the model learns historical movement 
dynamics to generate highly accurate simulated GPS 
coordinates during signal outages or interference. This enables 
UAVs to autonomously maintain navigation without resorting 
to emergency protocols.

outages.

BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORKS
This section provides a brief overview of the fundamental 
concepts regarding positioning technologies and the deep 
learning models utilized to address the problem at hand. Given 
the inherent limitations of traditional navigation systems when 
operating in complex environments, the application of 
advanced neural networks, such as Generative Adversarial 
Networks (GANs), is emerging as a promising approach for 
reconstructing and predicting spatio-temporal data sequences. 
The Global Positioning System (GPS) is structurally 
categorized into three core segments: the Space Segment, the 
Control Segment, and the User Segment [5]. Operating 
through this architecture, GPS determines 3D spatial 
coordinates via trilateration based on a network of satellite 
constellations [6]. Despite providing high accuracy, GPS 
signals are highly susceptible to attenuation, obstruction, or 
intentional jamming. To mitigate these issues, GPS is 
frequently integrated with an Inertial Navigation System

B. TransGAN Architecture
The integration of Transformers into Generative
Adversarial Networks has recently demonstrated robust 
capabilities in modeling complex data, ranging from time-
series synthesis [12] to high-fidelity image super-resolution 
reconstruction [13]. Building upon these foundational 
advancements, the specific TransGAN model [14] utilized in 
this study constructs its adversarial framework entirely upon 
Transformer architectures, moving away from traditional 
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). 
The core mechanism of this model is the Multi-Head Self-
Attention (MHSA) module, which inherently excels at 
capturing global dependencies and selectively prioritizing 
critical temporal correlations within sequential data by 
quantifying vector similarities. To alleviate the quadratic 
computational complexity traditionally associated with 
processing long sequences, TransGAN introduces a grid self-
attention module. This allows the model to efficiently process 
high-dimensional spatio-temporal data without exceeding 
memory constraints. 
Structurally, TransGAN consists of two primary 
components operating in an adversarial loop. The Generator 
is composed of a series of Transformer encoder blocks. It 
receives an initial input—such as random noise or a 
conditioning state—and processes it through multiple self-
attention layers and up-sampling modules to progressively 
synthesize the target data representation. Conversely, the 
Discriminator (or Critic) employs a symmetrical Transformer-
based structure. It ingests either the real data sequences or the 
generated outputs, processes the sequence tokens through its 
attention layers, and concludes with a classification head to 
output a probability score indicating the authenticity of the 
data (REAL or FAKE). By leveraging these pure attention 
mechanisms, TransGAN becomes highly effective for 
modeling complex, dynamic sequential variations, making it 
particularly suitable for continuous UAV coordinate 
generation. 
C. Existing GNSS-Denied Navigation Methods
To maintain navigation continuity during GNSS outages,
various trajectory estimation methods have been developed. 
Traditionally, filtering algorithms have been the primary 
countermeasures. For instance, Qi and Moore [15] utilized a 
direct Kalman Filtering (KF) approach for GPS/INS 
integration, providing a fundamental baseline for error 
estimation. However, traditional KF heavily relies on 
predefined linear mathematical models and often struggles to 
adapt to the highly non-linear error accumulations inherent in 
prolonged signal denial scenarios. 
To overcome the limitations of classical filters, data-
driven machine learning models have been extensively 
explored. Kong et al. [16] leveraged Multi-Layer Perceptron 
(MLP) architectures to model GPS path detection. While 
effective for short-term prediction, standard MLPs often lack 
the capacity to capture complex temporal dependencies over 
extended periods. Addressing this, Wei et al. [17] introduced 
a mixed optimization method combining an Adaptive Kalman 
Filter with a Wavelet Neural Network (WNN) to compensate 
for INS drift. Although WNNs demonstrate improved 
robustness, these methods primarily focus on pointwise error 
compensation rather than full sequence generation. 
While KF [15], MLP [16], and WNN [17] serve as reliable 
baselines for GNSS-denied navigation, they generally lack the 
generative capacity required to fully reconstruct long-term 
spatio-temporal sequences. This limitation directly motivates 
our application of the TransGAN model, which is specifically 
designed to capture global temporal dependencies and 
synthesize continuous trajectory data under extreme outage 
conditions. 
While established temporal and structural models, such as 
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks or Tree-
structured Deep Neural Networks (TDNN), have been widely 
applied for signal processing and short-term trajectory 
forecasting, they present notable limitations in prolonged 
GNSS-denied environments. Specifically, sequential models

III. 
PROPOSED METHOD 
The coordinate generation architecture proposed in this 
study (Figure 1) comprises two core components: the Data 
Processing Block and the Data Generator Block. Following 
pre-processing, the data is fed into the TransGAN model to 
learn the spatio-temporal relationships between coordinate 
points, thereby generating substitute trajectories during GNSS 
signal outages.  
 
Fig. 1. Overview of Coordinate Generation Model 
A. Data Processing Block 
This study utilizes the Pixhawk flight controller to acquire 
and process input data. Real-world trajectory coordinates are 
interpolated from peripheral modules, specifically the GPS 
and IMU. Through the filtering algorithms integrated within 
the Pixhawk, sensor noise is attenuated to produce a highly 
reliable and comprehensive input dataset for the subsequent 
data generation phase.  
B. Data Generator Block 
This block employs the TransGAN architecture to 
effectively exploit long-term dependencies within spatio-
temporal data sequences. The framework encompasses the 
adversarial architecture of the model and its operational 
prediction process during inference. 
1) Model Architecture (Training Mode) 
As illustrated in Figure 2, the Generator processes the pre-
processed data sequence, applying Positional Encoding to 
preserve temporal order before utilizing Transformer 
Encoder blocks to extract dynamic features. Its output 
separates into three independent linear branches to predict 
position variations Δ/0123204 for Longitude (LON), Latitude 
(LAT), and Altitude (ALT) at each time step. Conversely, the 
Critic (Discriminator) evaluates the authenticity of the 
generated Δ/0123204 sequence. By employing a Transformer 
Encoder coupled with sub-networks to assess component-
wise plausibility and detect motion discontinuities, the Critic 
outputs a Real/Fake score to continuously optimize the 
trajectory generation process. 
 
Fig. 2. Proposed TransGAN Training Architecture 
2) Prediction Process (Inference Mode) 
When the Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) enters an 
interference zone or experiences complete GNSS signal loss, 
the Data Generator Block transitions from training to 
inference mode. It is crucial to clarify that during this 
inference phase, the Discriminator (Critic) and its 
REAL/FAKE scoring mechanism are completely deactivated. 
The trajectory reconstruction relies exclusively on the trained 
Generator via a continuous loop-back mechanism, as 
accurately depicted in Figure 3. 
The process initializes from the final validated coordinate 
point of the training set (Preceding Position), utilizing only the 
available INS data corresponding to the duration of the signal 
interruption. The data flow operates step-by-step as follows: 
First, the input INS data passes through a Positional 
Encoding layer to preserve temporal order, and is then 
processed by the Transformer Encoder to synthesize a 
positional variation segment ( Δ/0123204 for LON, LAT, 
ALT) directed toward the target checkpoint. 
In the subsequent loop-back trajectory generation process, 
this generated Δ/0123204 is routed through an Adaptive Step 
Adjustment module. This mechanism dynamically scales the 
output to prevent the trajectory from overshooting due to 
cumulative INS drift. The adjusted variation is then fed into 
the Coordinate Accumulation block, where it is added to the 
Preceding Position to calculate the new absolute coordinates. 
This new coordinate then loops back to serve as the preceding 
position for the next time step. 
Finally, the accumulated position sequence is processed 
through the Savitzky-Golay Filter block for trajectory 
smoothing and denormalization. This final filtering step 
eliminates sharp, physically impossible positional jumps, 
ensuring the reconstructed flight path achieves maximal 
precision and continuity before outputting the final Predicted 
Coordinates.

real-world GPS trajectory acquired in central Hanoi via a 
NEO-M8N module. The 75-minute flight yielded over 3,000

signal anomalies and receiver errors, the raw data underwent 
filtering and smoothing (as detailed in III.A) to preserve 
trajectory integrity. Subsequently, to simulate adverse

C. Computational Complexity and Hardware Feasibility 
To determine the feasibility of deploying the proposed 
TransGAN model on resource-constrained UAV companion 
computers, we evaluated its computational complexity. The 
lightweight design of our Transformer-based architecture 
requires approximately 0.355 million parameters. The 
computational cost is evaluated at 0.0007 Giga-FLOPS 
(GFLOPS), which is significantly lower than large-scale 
vision transformers and well-suited for embedded systems. 
Furthermore, the average inference speed for generating a 
single spatial coordinate update (Δ/0123204) was measured at 
2.69 milliseconds (ms) per step. Given that the typical GPS 
update frequency for UAV navigation is 1 Hz (1000 ms per 
cycle), our model's inference speed is well within the real-time 
operational constraints. These metrics confirm that the 
proposed TransGAN methodology is highly practical and can 
be effectively executed on onboard UAV edge devices, such 
as NVIDIA Jetson Nano or Raspberry Pi, without causing 
latency bottlenecks. 
V. 
CONCLUSION AND FUTURE DEVELOPMENT 
A. Conclusion 
This study proposes a hardware-efficient, TransGAN-
based navigation algorithm that utilizes standard UAV GPS 
modules (e.g., NEO-M8N) to ensure continuous operation in 
GNSS-denied or heavily jammed environments, effectively 
circumventing forced landings and RTH protocols. While the 
current framework achieves acceptable operational accuracy, 
its precision remains constrained by inherent sensor errors, 
environmental factors, and a simplistic INS architecture. 
Future iterations will integrate advanced multi-axis IMUs 
(accelerometers and gyroscopes) to diversify input data and 
mitigate these structural limitations, ultimately targeting high-
precision, robust navigation with error margins restricted to a 
mere few meters. 
B. Future Development 
To advance this research toward practical deployment, 
future work will focus on three primary avenues. First, 
enhancing the hardware and data foundation by integrating 
multi-axis IMU sensors to minimize device errors , alongside 
expanding the training dataset across diverse topographies 
(e.g., urban canyons, forests, and mountainous terrains) to 
improve
