Abstract
Long-endurance unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) missions beyond 72 consecutive flight
hours face a reliability boundary at which thermal gyroscope drift drives the inertial
navigation system (INS) position error into rapid nonlinear divergence at a predictable
threshold tc. This paper presents BAZ II, a simulation-validated multi-agent navigation
system that extends the analytical BAZ (bifurcation-aware zonal navigation) framework.
Its central idea is to treat communication quality as a planning resource and combine it
with multi-agent collaboration, making the navigation cliff a manageable degradation
event rather than a hard operating limit. Four contributions support this idea: a thermal-
hysteresis MEMS gyroscope drift model reproduces the analytical cliff in simulation and
supplies its physical mechanism; a distributed collaborative simultaneous localization and
mapping (SLAM) filter coupled to a stochastic continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) inter-
agent channel sustains GPS-denied localization within the operational accuracy budget;
a 3D Gaussian process RF-aware model predictive controller (MPC) with cognitive radio

1. Introduction
1.1. Background and Motivation
Long-duration unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs)—defined here as platforms capa-
ble of multi-day sorties exceeding 48 consecutive flight hours—have become essential to
autonomous environmental monitoring, persistent area surveillance, and high-altitude
communication relay [1–4]. Unlike rotary-wing platforms where mission times are typically
limited to 2–8 h, long-endurance UAVs must maintain navigation capability across multiple
thermal cycles per day [5,6], tolerate extended Global Positioning System (GPS) outages,
and operate over highly variable communication links—conditions that lie well outside
the design envelope of conventional navigation and control architectures. Throughout
this paper, the acronym BAZ (Bifurcation-Aware Zonal navigation) denotes the precur-
sor analytical framework of Alrashed et al. [7], while BAZ II denotes the simulation-
validated multi-agent extension developed here. The precursor study [7] introduced the
idea that communication quality should be treated as a planning resource and proactively
shaped through trajectory selection. The BAZ study established three theoretical results:
(1) ε-optimality of a sigmoid penalty model predictive control (MPC) algorithm under
continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) communication uncertainty (ε ≤0.12); (2) exponen-
tial stability of a hysteresis-modulated switched architecture (λ ≥0.23); and (3) analytical
derivation of a 72-h bifurcation threshold. MPC is adopted as the unifying control frame-
work here because it is the only standard receding-horizon method that natively combines
the three properties required by long-endurance multi-UAV navigation: (i) hard pointwise
constraints on attitude rate, thrust, and obstacle clearance; (ii) stochastic disturbance models
such as the CTMC channel state, which enter as expectations over the prediction horizon;
and (iii) position-dependent cost terms—specifically, the GP-queried SNR field Jcomm and
the SLAM information cost Jinfo in Equation (1)—that have no closed-form analogue in lin-
ear quadratic regulators or sliding-mode formulations. Therefore, the BAZ II contribution
is not the MPC paradigm itself but the integration of these three terms into a single compu-
tationally tractable cost function on embedded hardware. Beyond the 72-h cliff, thermally
induced gyroscopic drift drives the INS position error through a sharp transition from
bounded linear growth to rapid nonlinear divergence. Accelerometer thermal sensitivity
also exists [8], but is sub-dominant on the multi-day timescale: typical tactical-grade MEMS
accelerometer thermal bias of ∼0.5 mg/◦C produces a Schuler-bounded position error
oscillation that does not accumulate, whereas the integrated gyroscope drift (Equation (5))
grows quadratically and is the rate-determining failure mode beyond ∼48 h. Therefore, the
accelerometer error budget is folded into the INS noise model in Section 3.3 and does not
require a separate bifurcation analysis. BAZ was validated through Monte Carlo simulation
over 2430 missions consisting of 54,686 flight hours [7] and demonstrated a 243% endurance
extension together with sub-9 m circular error probable (CEP) navigation precision even

1.2. Research Gap and Contributions
While the BAZ analytical framework [7] establishes the existence and theoretical
character of the 72-h navigation cliff, several gaps remain before the framework can support
reliable multi-agent deployments at long endurance. A first-principles physical mechanism
for the cliff has not been demonstrated; the analytical CTMC model has not been coupled to
a multi-agent SLAM front-end; the scalar reactive communication penalty offers no spatial
foresight in 3D urban environments; and the original communication cost functional treats
SNR as the sole proxy for link quality, ignoring bandwidth, latency, and jitter. These four
gaps motivate the contributions of the present paper.
The central research question addressed here is the following: by treating communica-
tion quality as a first-class planning resource within a cooperative multi-agent system, can
the analytically predicted 72-h navigation cliff be converted from a hard endurance limit
into a managed degradation event that a mission planner can anticipate, and can this be
achieved within the real-time computational budget of embedded UAV hardware? The
main scientific contribution of the paper is a simulation-based affirmative answer to this
question: a single hierarchical navigation system, BAZ II, in which a physically grounded
cliff model, collaborative SLAM, RF-aware predictive control, and multi-objective commu-
nication cost together extend the effective cliff time while remaining inside the onboard
real-time limit. The four primary contributions listed below are the components that, in
combination, answer this question.
In response to second-round reviewer feedback that an earlier draft presented seven
items at uneven levels of novelty, the contribution list has been consolidated into four
primary contributions, supplemented by two implementation enablers that are explicitly
not claimed as new (Sections 3.5 and 4.1.5). The four primary contributions are summarized
below; the prose is deliberately non-parallel, since the contributions differ in character (a
validated physical mechanism, an estimator, an integrated controller, and a cost-functional
reformulation) rather than being four instances of a single template.
C1. Physical mechanism for the 72-h navigation cliff. The BAZ analytical bifurca-
tion [7] predicted, but did not physically explain, the abrupt growth in INS position error
beyond ∼72 h. A thermal hysteresis MEMS gyroscope drift model is constructed here
from first principles—irreversible micromechanical creep accumulated over successive
diurnal cycles—and is calibrated against published tactical-grade datasheets [9–11]. The
bifurcation mechanism of Equation (7) reproduces the analytical cliff to within 0.8% over

C2. Stochastic communication-aware collaborative SLAM. The single-agent BAZ
formulation cannot fuse landmark observations across multiple agents and offers no mech-

ε-optimality and Lyapunov stability guarantees inherited from BAZ are preserved under the
lumped channel. On a common 30-min visual-inertial benchmark against Swarm-SLAM,
Kimera-Multi, and D2SLAM (Section 3.4), the proposed filter is the only one for which
the RMS localization error remains within the 2 m operational budget under stochastic
CTMC degradation.
C3. 3D RF-aware predictive control with cognitive radio resilience. The scalar reactive
penalty of BAZ steers the vehicle only after an SNR threshold has been crossed. In its place,
Sections 3.5 and 3.6 integrate a 3D Gaussian process (GP) RF map built using an anisotropic
spatiotemporal kernel and queried directly inside the MPC cost Jcomm with a cognitive
radio (CR) frequency-hopping layer that operates against barrage and sweep jammers.
The combination is what is novel here: GP-based predictive routing alone cannot survive
adversarial jamming, and CR alone cannot exploit spatial RF structure. Their integration
restores link availability from 12.4% to 81.7% under barrage jamming and lowers RF map
prediction error by 48.6% relative to an isotropic baseline.
C4. Multi-objective communication cost via the analytic hierarchy process. The orig-
inal BAZ communication cost is scalar in SNR. Section 4.1.3 replaces it with an analytic
hierarchy process (AHP) scalarization that admits bandwidth, latency, and jitter as addi-
tional axes; the pairwise-comparison matrix is provided in Supplementary Material S1.
The reformulated cost is shown via tornado analysis to be insensitive to ±20% weight
perturbations, dispelling the concern that the AHP reduction is a fragile choice of weights,
and yields a 15.1% latency reduction and 12.9% jitter reduction with negligible SNR loss.
The two implementation enablers are deliberately separated from the contribution list,
since neither is novel. Online recursive GP updates (Section 3.5, m = 64 inducing points at
10 Hz) follow the sparse-GP machinery of [12]; the parameter choices are documented in
Supplementary S5. The recursive update is required for tractable execution of C3 on the
Jetson Xavier NX, but the algorithmic structure itself is not new. A structure-exploiting
condensed Riccati QP step (Section 4.1.5) is the textbook block-tridiagonal factorization [13]
that also underlies HPIPM [14] and FORCES [15]; authorship of the recursion itself is not
claimed. What is reported is the integration of this factorization as the real-time iteration
step of the BAZ II MPC, and the resulting solve-time profile that keeps the fully integrated
controller inside the 100 ms Layer 2 budget on embedded hardware.

2. Related Work
2.1. INS Thermal Drift and Multi-UAV SLAM
Tactical-grade MEMS gyroscopes are dominated by thermal sensitivity, with position
error growing quadratically under bias instability [9,33,34]. Temperature drift compensa-
tion using machine learning methods has been explored for MEMS accelerometers [8,35]
and inertial navigation systems [36], while attitude and quaternion representations for
strapdown INS are well-established [37,38]. GPS-denied navigation remains a fundamental
challenge for long-endurance UAVs, addressed through vision-based approaches [39–45],
lightweight simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) for resource-constrained
platforms [46], celestial navigation alternatives [47], autonomous landing [48], and Lie
group-based visual localization [49]. The EuRoC benchmark dataset [50] and Stanford
drone dataset [51] have become standard evaluation testbeds, while Cadena et al. [52]
provide a comprehensive overview of SLAM past, present, and future.
Modern UAV navigation estimators rely on extensions of the Kalman filter, primarily
the extended Kalman filter (EKF) and unscented Kalman filter (UKF), which are the ap-
propriate references for the nonlinear strapdown navigation problem considered in this
paper [52,53]; filter divergence remains a concern in long-horizon operation [53]. GNSS
reliability under degraded conditions has been characterized via Monte Carlo methods [54].
While multi-UAV SLAM systems like Kimera-Multi, Swarm-SLAM, and decentralized
pose graph optimization [21,22,55] have matured, they do not explicitly address sustaining
collaboration during the multi-day nonlinear error regime predicted in BAZ [7]. Vision-
and-language navigation surveys [56] and autonomous landing on maritime vessels [57]
extend the operational envelope further.
2.2. Communication-Aware Path Planning
RF awareness in UAV planning has been explored via SNR-constrained optimization
and radio map-based signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR) planning [24,26,58].
UAV communication for wireless networks has been studied extensively [59–61], includ-
ing energy minimization for rotary-wing UAVs [62], joint communication and trajectory
optimization for IoV applications [63], and time-variant multipath channel models for UAV-
to-ground links [64]. Finite-state Markov channel models underpin the CTMC framework
used in this work [65]. Communication-aware swarm surveillance has been studied for
hierarchical multi-UAV architectures [66]; adaptive control via communication information
optimization [67] and distributed MPC for channel resource allocation [68] represent closely
related contributions. Asynchronous distributed trajectory optimization for UAV swarms
https://doi.org/10.3390/drones10080561

under communication constraints has also been proposed [69]. GP regression is a standard
tool for spatial RF mapping [25], but achieving 10 Hz updates on embedded hardware

GP-guided MPC trajectory planning with these techniques for UAVs remains largely
unexplored. This work brings together GP-based 3D RF mapping, cognitive radio (CR)
anti-jamming, and position-dependent CTMC channel models in a single MPC framework,
thereby shifting communication management from reactive to predictive and directly
extending the CTMC model developed in BAZ [7].
3. Materials and Methods
3.1. System Architecture
BAZ II extends the three-layer hierarchical architecture of BAZ [7] with three new
modules: a collaborative SLAM front-end, a 3D RF-aware predictive controller, and a
72-h cliff monitor (Figure 1). The reference platform is a 23.8 kg hexarotor equipped
with tactical-grade MEMS sensors and partitioned across three operating layers: Layer 1
(Navigation, 100 Hz), Layer 2 (Planning, 10 Hz), and Layer 3 (Mission Control, <1 Hz).
Airframe attitude and rate dynamics follow standard rigid-body formulations [70,71],
with geometric tracking control [72,73] providing attitude consistency across the operat-
ing modes. The handling quality envelope conforms to MIL-STD-1797A [74,75]; the IMU
performance envelope is benchmarked against navigation-grade SBG Systems units [76],
and the platform mass and payload allocation are sized against the published DJI Ma-
trice 400 specification [77]. Detailed specifications and temporal scales are provided in
Section S5.3.1 of Supplementary Material S5.

Simulations are executed with n = 2430 Monte Carlo runs [85–88] over a 120-h win-

random walk, thermal noise, and bifurcation threshold. For reproducibility, the full sim-
ulation environment is specified as follows: the bifurcation simulator is implemented in
MATLAB/Simulink R2023b [89] (the specific Simulink toolboxes used are Aerospace Block-
set, Sensor Fusion and Tracking Toolbox, and Statistics and Machine Learning Toolbox);
the ODE integrator is the variable-step Dormand–Prince method (ode45) with relative
tolerance 10−6 and absolute tolerance 10−9; random number generation uses MATLAB’s
default Mersenne Twister stream with deterministic per-run seeds (the explicit seed list
and the run script that consumes it are provided in Supplementary Material S3 to enable
byte-for-byte replay); ground-truth temperature profiles are generated by the closed-form
expression of Equation (6). The physical parameters {µBI, σBI, σrw, κT, κH, α, β∗} are listed
in Section 3.3 together with their published manufacturer sources [9–11,78]. Represen-
tative MATLAB run scripts that regenerate the principal results of Sections 3.3–3.5 are
provided as Supplementary Material S3, and the complete parameter table appears in
Section S5.1 of Supplementary Material S5. The choice of MATLAB/Simulink is moti-
vated by the availability of validated MEMS gyroscope drift, sensor fusion, and CTMC
channel models in standard toolboxes; this allowed the present study to focus on the bi-
furcation analysis and multi-agent fusion logic rather than re-implementing low-level INS

3.4. Distributed Collaborative SLAM with Information-Weighted Fusion
To arrest INS divergence in GPS-denied conditions, BAZ II implements distributed
collaborative SLAM in which agents share landmark observations through an information-
weighted fusion filter. The fusion logic is explicitly coupled to the stochastic CTMC
inter-agent channel, so that inter-agent position aiding is robust to intermittent link outages
rather than being silently corrupted by them. The full information filter update, event-
triggering logic, and ergodicity-based stability proofs are provided in Section S5.3.2 of
Supplementary Material S5. The multi-agent extension directly addresses the scalability
limitation identified in Section 5.4 of BAZ [7], where the single-agent formulation could
not account for inter-agent communication deconfliction.

3.4.2. Communication-Degraded Fusion via CTMC
The inter-agent communication link quality is governed by a two-state CTMC
(Good/Bad) [65]—a justified simplification of the three-state (Nominal/Degraded/Denied)
model of BAZ [7]—applied here to the inter-agent link. The reduction is justified by lumpa-
bility [90,91]: the Nominal and Degraded states of the BAZ model share the same generator
structure with respect to their aggregate transition rate into the Denied state, satisfying the
ordinary lumpability condition. The steady-state good-channel availability of the lumped
two-state model (πgood ≈78.9%) matches the aggregate Nominal and Degraded availabil-
ity of the BAZ three-state model to within 2.1% under the link parameters of this scenario.

3.4.3. Event-Triggered Communication
To reduce bandwidth usage on the degraded link (approximately 4.8 kB/s compact
keyframe [21]), agents broadcast observations only when the position displacement since
the last transmission exceeds a threshold

exchange in decentralized SLAM [94], which is critical when operating on degraded links.

Detailed sensitivity analyses characterizing the GPS-denied localization error as a

3.6. Cognitive Radio Anti-Jamming
To mitigate adversarial jamming, BAZ II incorporates a cognitive radio (CR) layer that
performs real-time spectrum sensing and adaptive frequency hopping. The hopping policy
that maximizes link reliability πgood is derived from history-dependent sensing information;
the full control-theoretic derivation and SINR restoration guarantees appear in Section S5.3
of Supplementary Material S5. In a five-agent fleet, simultaneous spectrum sensing by
all agents would risk hidden-node collisions and duplicate channel selections. The CR
layer prevents both through a time-division sensing schedule: each agent is assigned a non-
overlapping 5 ms sensing slot within a 25 ms frame (one slot per agent at C = 4 channels
plus one guard slot), so that only one UAV senses a given channel at any given instant.
Channel selections are broadcast on a dedicated low-power control channel (<1 kbps
overhead) before each hopping decision, ruling out two agents selecting the same frequency
simultaneously. The 25 ms sensing frame fits comfortably within the 100 ms Layer 2 budget;
the ≈15 ms net sensing latency per agent inflates the mean restoration time by less than
4.4% relative to the single-agent baseline reported in Table 8.

3.6.3. Anti-Jamming Simulation Results
Table 8 reports SINR performance under three scenarios over a 60-minute mission: no
jamming (baseline), barrage jamming on the primary channel (all power on one frequency),
and sweep jamming cycling across all four channels at 2 Hz. Under barrage jamming,
activating the CR layer raises mean SINR from −3.2 dB to +18.4 dB (+21.6 dB recovery) and
restores link time from 12.4% to 81.7%—within 2.4 percentage points of the no-jamming
predictive MPC baseline—with a mean restoration latency of 340 ms. Sweep jamming,

window cannot resolve channels faster than the hopping rate, reducing link time to 76.3%.

channel state in the good-channel region for the majority of the mission.

4.1.1. Computational Budget Management
Real-time execution of collaborative SLAM, 3D RF map queries, and MPC optimization
on embedded hardware (NVIDIA Jetson Xavier NX, 21 Tera Operations Per Second (TOPS),
21 W thermal design power (TDP)) requires careful resource allocation. A normalized
computational budget is defined as follows:
ctotal = cSLAM + cRF + cMPC + coverhead ≤cmax = 1.0.
(17)
The system adaptively switches between two processing modes:
•
Full mode, with full-resolution SLAM (cSLAM = 0.38), dense RF queries (cRF = 0.22),
full MPC (cMPC = 0.18), overhead 0.07, and total 0.85.
•
Sparse mode, with sparse SLAM (cSLAM = 0.18), cached RF (cRF = 0.12), simplified
MPC (cMPC = 0.10), overhead 0.06, and total 0.46.
The orchestrations for the operation of these computation modes are managed through
the Layer 3 Strategic Multi-Mode Controller [7]. This controller dynamically controls the
computational accuracy so that real-time determinism is maintained as ctotal approaches
the maximum possible value. Figure 10a illustrates how the compute budget was allocated
under each operational regime. In addition to achieving an intended 33% reduction in
central processing unit (CPU) usage in Sparse mode, it resulted in a 0.71 m increase in
SLAM localization errors.

4.1.5. Structure-Exploiting Condensed Riccati MPC Solver (Implementation Enabler,
Not a Contribution)
To meet the 10 Hz real-time requirement of the integrated BAZ II controller on the
Jetson Xavier NX, the BAZ IPOPT QP step is replaced by a standard structure-exploiting
condensed Riccati factorization drawn from the established sparse-MPC literature [13–15].
Authorship of the Riccati recursion itself is not claimed: the block-tridiagonal factorization
is a textbook construction [13] and is also the underlying kernel used by the open-source
HPIPM [14] and FORCES [15] solvers. What is reported here is the integration of this
factorization as the real-time iteration (RTI) step of the BAZ II MPC, together with the
embedded-hardware solve-time profile (Table 12) needed to verify that the four primary
contributions C1–C4 fit within the 100 ms Layer 2 budget. Complexity is reduced from
O(N3
p) to O(Np); the implementation details, RTI linearization scheme, and complex-
ity analysis appear in Supplementary Material S5. Table 12 and Figure 12 summarize
the performance improvements. Table 12 presents solve-time statistics collected over
10,000 consecutive QP solves during the representative mission scenario on the Jetson Xavier
NX. The larger sample size provides reliable characterization of tail probability: the empirical
99th-percentile estimate requires at minimum 1000 samples, and the Gumbel-fitted worst-case
at 10−4 probability is also reported. IPOPT benchmarks include both cold-started (BAZ con-
dition) and warm-started variants (initialized from the previous solution); warm-starting
is standard practice at 10 Hz and reduces IPOPT solve time substantially. The proposed
Riccati solver at Np = 50 achieves a mean solve time of 3.1 ms, which is 62% lower than the
BAZ IPOPT baseline at the same horizon, and a worst-case of 5.8 ms, well within the 100 ms
Layer 2 real-time budget at 10 Hz. This restores the full predictive horizon of BAZ while
simultaneously incorporating the additional BAZ II cost terms, removing the Np = 20
compromise imposed by the added GP and SLAM computational load.

5. Discussion
5.1. Significance of the 72-h Navigation Cliff and FDIR
The computational verification of tc = 72.9 ± 2.4 h corroborates the BAZ analytical
result (tc = 72.3 h) nearly exactly. The identified physical mechanism—irreversible accu-
mulation of thermal hysteresis micromechanical creep—is the causal explanation that was
absent from the original CTMC characterization.
Beyond confirming the threshold, the mechanism changes how it can be used op-
erationally. This 72-h cliff monitor is a proactive fault detection, isolation, and recovery
(FDIR) mechanism that is invoked before INS divergence and spans the physical (thermal)
to cyber (communication) domains. Works on LSTM-based real-time planning [95] and
cost-effectiveness of autonomous aerial systems [96] give complementary context for the
operational deployment of such an approach.
5.2. Collaborative SLAM as a tc Extension Mechanism
The localization improvement observed for GPS-denied agents illustrates what multi-
agent collaboration can offer to long-endurance operations. Its connection to the navigation
cliff is direct: by providing external position-aiding that arrests INS estimate divergence,
collaborative SLAM turns tc from a hard failure threshold into a recoverable degrada-
tion boundary. The modest drop from full communication (1.07 m) to CTMC-degraded
communication (2.06 m) confirms that the information-weighted fusion inherits the same
robustness to intermittent link outages that was proved for the scalar CTMC model in
BAZ [7].
The sensitivity analyses make this scaling concrete. The fleet size sensitivity curve
(Figure 5a) quantifies this scaling: the power-law exponent γ = 0.84 shows near-linear
improvement, and N ≥4 suffices to meet the 2 m error budget under both full and CTMC-
degraded communication. The CTMC channel quality heatmap (Figure 5b) identifies the
robust operating zone (πgood > 0.65, RMSE < 2 m), providing channel-quality thresholds
for mission planning. The synthesis figure (Section 5.3, Figure 12) directly quantifies the
cliff extension: for N = 5 and πgood = 0.80, teff
c
extends beyond the 96 h simulation window,
and even the minimum useful configuration (N = 2, πgood = 0.55) yields a 13.5% extension
to 82.1 ± 3.8 h [5,97].
5.3. Navigation Cliff Extension as a Function of Channel Quality and Fleet Size
An operational question left open by the individual contribution results is the follow-
ing: by how much does collaborative SLAM extend the effective navigation cliff tc, and how
does this extension scale with link quality and fleet size? Figure 12 answers this directly.

5.4. Predictive Versus Reactive Communication Management
The 1.9 percentage point gain in link availability achieved by the GP-based predictive
MPC reflects the compounding benefit described in Section 3.5. Unlike the reactive scalar
CTMC model inherited from BAZ, the GP posterior gives the controller spatially resolved
foresight, allowing it to steer the vehicle away from high-uncertainty shadowed regions
before the instantaneous SNR drops below threshold. This look-ahead capability is critical
for protecting the high-SNR windows that collaborative SLAM needs for reliable informa-
tion exchange—a “communication safety margin” that the original single-agent reactive
formulation could not provide by design.
However, this advantage is bounded by the fidelity of the underlying RF map. The
predictive gain is largest when the GP posterior is well-conditioned over the planning
horizon and shrinks as the vehicle enters previously unsampled terrain, which is precisely
where the online recursive update of Section 3.5 becomes important. Therefore, the pre-
dictive and reactive modes are complementary rather than mutually exclusive: predictive
routing shapes the nominal trajectory, while the reactive penalty remains as a fallback when
the map is locally uncertain.
5.5. Anisotropic GP Mapping and Cognitive Radio Resilience
The 48.6% reduction in RF map prediction error obtained by the ARD, spatiotem-
poral and online GP variant relative to the isotropic baseline shows that directional SNR
structure contributes substantially to mapping uncertainty in urban environments. The
fitted vertical length scale (ℓz = 45 m) is much shorter than its horizontal counterpart
(ℓx = 180 m), reflecting the altitude-dependent LoS probability of the path-loss model
(Supplementary Material S5, Section S3.3); an isotropic kernel collapses both scales into a
single correlation dimension and incurs the corresponding penalty. The hyperparameter
sensitivity heatmap (Section 3.5.3, Figure 8) corroborates this interpretation: the isotropic
ridge lies +1.4 dB above the anisotropic optimum, while the optimum itself is robust to
±30% perturbations of (ℓx, ℓz) (RMSPE rises by only +0.8 dB). The 10 Hz online recur-
sive update is most consequential for extended multi-day deployments, where a statically
trained GP would fail to produce accurate maps as the UAV enters previously unsampled
terrain [32].
Cognitive-radio performance shows a clear asymmetry between barrage and sweep
jamming. Barrage jamming on a single channel is readily mitigated: the 5 ms sensing
window is long enough to discriminate the jammed channel from the three clear alternatives,
and the mean restoration latency of 340 ms is dominated by the CTMC update from a
degraded state estimate to a clear-channel prediction. A detailed JNR sensitivity analysis
appears in Section 3.6.4 and Figure 9: link time exceeds 76% up to JNR = 18 dB for barrage
jamming and ≈14 dB for sweep jamming. Sweep jamming at 2 Hz cycles faster than the
sensing window can resolve, restricting the hopping strategy to a reactive rather than a
predictive mode. Extending the GP-based hopping policy of Equation (16) with temporal
predictions of sweep patterns is a natural direction for future research.
5.6. Multi-Objective Communication Cost and Solver Tractability
The Pareto analysis confirms that the proposed AHP-weighted Jcomm formulation
provides a more operationally balanced link quality metric than the SNR-only BAZ base-
line, reducing latency and jitter at negligible SNR cost. A detailed discussion of the
multi-objective cost structure and AHP weight sensitivity (Figure 12) is provided in
Supplementary Material S5.

The practical value of this reformulation is that latency and jitter, rather than SNR
alone, are the quantities that govern the usability of the inter-agent link for collaborative
SLAM and control. Treating them as explicit cost axes allows the planner to trade a small
SNR margin for a measurable improvement in the metrics that matter operationally. The
tornado analysis further shows that this tradeoff is stable under ±20% perturbation of the
AHP weights, so the result does not depend on a fragile choice of relative priorities.
5.7. Computational Tractability
The adaptive sparse/full processing mode allows the integrated framework to fit
within the computational budget of current embedded hardware. The condensed Riccati
solver (Section 4.1.5) reduces worst-case bounded solve times, enabling the combined
BAZ II contributions without compromising the MPC horizon length. Detailed empirical
complexity scaling results (Figure 12) and real-time computation metrics across processing
modes are available in Supplementary Material S5.
A normalized heatmap of all five integration configurations against six performance
metrics confirms that the Full Integration configuration achieves the highest aggregate
score (82.4%), with the BAZ II contributions acting synergistically rather than competing
across the performance space (Supplementary Material S4, Figure S1). This indicates that
the integration does not merely sum the individual components but lets them share com-
putational headroom, which is what keeps the combined controller inside the embedded
real-time budget.
5.8. Limitations
Several limitations bound the scope of the present results, and are stated here explicitly
so that the conclusions can be read in the correct light. They define the regime in which the
reported numbers are meaningful and the regime in which they are not.
First, every result reported in this paper is obtained in simulation. No hardware-in-the-
loop or field data are presented, and the thermal hysteresis drift model, CTMC channel, and
jamming models are all idealizations calibrated against published datasheets rather than
measurements from the authors’ own platform. Therefore, the quantitative figures should
be read as evidence of internal consistency and relative improvement, not as guarantees of
field performance.
Second, the Monte Carlo ensembles emphasize mean behavior, and the simulated
outcomes are comparatively consistent across seeds. Rare divergent or failed runs, for
instance cases in which a loss of SLAM loop closure coincides with a prolonged bad-channel
CTMC sojourn, are under-represented at the seed counts used here. Characterizing this tail
behavior rather than only the mean is one motivation for the larger seed budgets identified
in the axes for future work provided below.
Third, the SLAM benchmark of Section 3.4 is conducted over 30-min visual inertial
scenarios, whereas the cliff itself is a multi-day phenomenon. Therefore, the comparison
with Swarm-SLAM, Kimera-Multi, and D2SLAM is measured at the benchmark horizon,
but is extrapolated at multi-day timescales rather than being measured. In addition, those
systems are not designed for the stochastic CTMC degradation regime, so the compari-
son characterizes their behavior under a regime outside their design intent rather than
providing a like-for-like ranking.
Finally, the computational feasibility claim is established for a single embedded plat-
form (the NVIDIA Jetson Xavier NX) and a fleet of five agents. Scaling to larger swarms or
to different hardware, along with the decentralized consensus and compression that this
would require, is left to future work.

6. Conclusions
This paper presents BAZ II as a simulation-validated multi-agent extension of the BAZ
analytical framework. The unifying idea of the paper is that the 72-h navigation cliff need
not be treated as a fixed endurance limit; when communication quality is promoted to a first-
class planning resource and combined with multi-agent collaboration, the cliff can instead
be reframed as a degradation event that mission planners can anticipate and manage.
Four ideas give this reframing substance. A physically grounded thermal-hysteresis
drift model explains why the cliff exists, rather than merely predicting that it does. A
communication-aware collaborative SLAM filter lets agents aid one another’s localization
through an explicitly stochastic channel. An RF-aware predictive controller supported by a
cognitive radio layer gives the fleet spatial and spectral foresight in place of purely reactive
recovery. Finally, a multi-objective communication cost replaces a single signal-strength
proxy with the link qualities that actually govern cooperative navigation. The contribution
of the paper is not any one of these components in isolation but their integration into a single
hierarchical system that remains within the real-time budget of embedded UAV hardware.
The central claim that collaboration and proactive communication management can
extend the effective navigation cliff is supported consistently across the simulation study;
however, it remains a simulation result. Translating it into an operational guarantee will
require the hardware-in-the-loop and long-endurance field testing set out in Section 5.8
together with multi-day SLAM characterization, explicit modeling of accelerometer thermal
bias, and scaling to larger and heterogeneous fleets. Pursued along these axes, BAZ II is best
understood as a design baseline for long-endurance GPS-denied multi-UAV navigation
rather than a finished operational solution. The associated key performance indicator
and optimization methodology builds upon the authors’ prior work in aerospace systems
engineering [98,99].
