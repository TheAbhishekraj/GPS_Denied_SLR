a b s t r a c t
Reliable self-localization of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) in dense urban environments remains a major 
challenge due to the frequent unavailability or degradation of Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) and 
other radio signals. This paper presents a robust and cost-eﬀective method for UAV self-localization by using 
vision and millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar data in GNSS-denied environments. The approach generates an 
initial dense point cloud through depth estimation and semantic segmentation, which is then geometrically 
reﬁned using sparse mmWave radar point cloud. A semantic-guided clustering method is applied to the mmWave 
radar point cloud to remove noise and extract key structural elements such as walls, which are later fused with 
vision-based depth information. For positioning, image matching algorithm provides coarse localization, followed 
by ﬁne registration that leverages geometric features of windows to enhance precision. Experimental results 
demonstrate that the proposed method can achieve self-localization accuracy within 0.4 m, while maintaining 
low system complexity and deployment cost, oﬀering a practical solution for UAV self-localization in GNSS-
denied urban scenarios.
1.  Introduction
Driven by advancements in autonomous ﬂight and sensing technolo-
gies, the unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have found widespread use in 
diverse applications such as surveying [1], power line inspection [2], au-
tonomous delivery [3], regional monitoring [4], and emergency search 
and rescue [5], playing an increasingly important role in both indus-
trial and civilian domains. Accurate navigation and positioning capabil-
ities have become a fundamental prerequisite for the reliable execution 
of drone operations. Currently, most drone systems rely on a combina-
tion of Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) and Inertial Naviga-
tion Systems (INS), where GNSS corrects accumulated drift of inertial 
sensors to enable high-precision self-localization. Nevertheless, in envi-
ronments with weak or absent GNSS signals, including urban canyons, 
indoor areas, and complex terrains, the performance of the integrated 
navigation system declines markedly, critically aﬀecting the drone’s au-
tonomy [6–8]. Therefore, achieving robust self-localization and naviga-
tion in GNSS-denied environments remains a signiﬁcant challenge with 
growing research interest.

ronments, reliable self-localization is a fundamental requirement for

1.1. Related work
Various positioning technologies have been developed and applied 
to achieve accurate drone self-localization. Among them, vision-based 
methods are widely adopted due to their high accuracy, low cost, and 
ease of implementation. These methods typically rely on monocular 
or stereo cameras to capture images of the environment, from which

salient visual features (e.g., Oriented FAST and Rotated BRIEF [9], Scale-
Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) [10], or learned descriptors [11]) 
are extracted and matched across frames. By analyzing the motion of 
these features over time or against a pre-built map, the system estimates 
the camera’s relative pose using Structure-from-Motion (SfM), Visual 
Odometry (VO), or Visual SLAM techniques [12]. Furthermore, deep 
learning-based approaches have been introduced to enhance robustness 
under dynamic conditions by learning more discriminative and invari-
ant representations. Qiu et al. [13] proposed a framework that models 
the self-localization process as a combination of image retrieval, fea-
ture matching, and geometric transformation (e.g., Perspective-n-Point 
(PnP)), enhanced by resolution adaptation and feature quality selection 
modules to improve projection consistency and matching accuracy. Liu 
et al. [14] introduced a multisource feature learning framework that 
align aerial images with georeferenced satellite images using Siamese 
networks and spatial attention, enabling robust localization even under 
heterogeneous imaging conditions. Visual-based localization methods 
have shown impressive performance in environments with good visibil-
ity. However, their performance degrades signiﬁcantly in environments 
with poor lighting or low-texture surfaces, where the perception capa-
bility of visual sensors is severely limited. Moreover, the reliance on 
complex feature extraction and matching pipelines often results in high 
computational complexity and increased energy consumption, which re-
stricts their deployment in real-time UAV applications. These limitations 
have motivated the exploration of alternative sensing modalities that 
can provide more robust geometric information.
Owing to high range accuracy and direct acquisition of geometric 
information, positioning methods based on Light Detection and Rang-
ing (LiDAR) technology is the much-sought techniques in this area. 
Qian et al. [15] employed LiDAR and monocular cameras to construct 
textured 3D point cloud maps, leveraging LiDAR odometry to pro-
vide prior pose and depth information, which was combined with vi-
sual matching to optimize pose estimation and enable robust, real-time 
localization. Petráˇcek et al. [16] utilized readily available 3D point 
clouds and 2D LiDAR to build local maps, integrating onboard Inertial
Measurement Unit (IMU) data to achieve accurate positioning in dark 
areas of large historical sites. Xu et al. [17] proposed an asynchronous 
LiDAR–camera fusion framework to estimate the absolute pose and po-
sition of drones in unknown outdoor environments. Despite their eﬀec-
tiveness, LiDAR systems are often constrained by high power demands, 
substantial weight, and sensitivity to adverse weather conditions, which 
limit their broader deployment in drone applications. Given the limi-
tations in power, weight, and environmental robustness of LiDAR sys-
tems, lightweight radio-based techniques such as UWB are increasingly 
explored as eﬃcient alternatives for UAV localization.
Among such alternatives, ultrasonic [18] and ultra-wideband (UWB) 
[19] positioning technologies share similar operating principles, both 
relying on pre-deployed auxiliary nodes for localization. While ultra-
sonic systems can achieve centimeter-level accuracy, their low update 
rate and susceptibility to occlusion will result in the instability of the po-
sitioning accuracy [20]. UWB systems leverage systems to large band-
width and nanosecond pulses to deliver high temporal resolution and 
precise positioning [21,22], but their performance in complex environ-
ments usually requires assistance from other sensors such as GPS, IMU, 
or vision to maintain robustness. Such dependence reﬂects a broader 
challenge in UAV self-localization, where most existing solutions con-
tinue to rely heavily on external infrastructure.
Even with signiﬁcant progress in improving the accuracy of self-
localization systems for UAVs, most existing solutions continue to de-
pend heavily on external infrastructure, which leads to high deploy-
ment and maintenance costs. In addition, mainstream perception meth-
ods such as LiDAR, INS, and vision present inherent limitations, making 
it diﬃcult to maintain stable performance in complex and dynamic en-
vironments.
1.2. Motivation
The increasing adoption of real-world 3D models has rendered them 
a valuable resource for enhancing the robustness of self-localization, 
as they provide rich geometric structures and semantic information

for prior map support [23,24]. Meanwhile, millimeter-wave (mmWave) 
radar has been introduced to further extend environmental perception 
capabilities [25], oﬀering strong penetration capabilities and reliable 
performance under poor lighting and adverse weather conditions, allow-
ing the capture of structural information even in visually degraded en-
vironments [26,27]. Nevertheless, the generated point cloud tends to be 
sparse and insuﬃciently detailed, limiting its suitability for high-quality 
3D reconstruction on its own. Advances in visual perception techniques, 
particularly in depth estimation and semantic segmentation [28–30], 
have enabled the extraction of spatial and semantic information from 
single images, laying a robust foundation for multimodal fusion. Depth 
prediction models exhibit strong generalization ability and can generate 
dense depth maps from unlabeled data, while segmentation models such 
as Mask2Former demonstrate excellent transferability across datasets 
[31], facilitating the automatic identiﬁcation of semantic regions and 
categories without manual annotation. The combination of these visual 
modalities forms a solid basis for recovering 3D structure from monoc-
ular imagery. The fusion of visual and radar sensing technologies com-
bines the strong generalization capability of visual segmentation models 
with the robustness of radar against adverse environmental conditions 
such as poor lighting and weather, thereby supporting 3D scene recon-
struction and precise self-localization across diverse environments.
Despite the complementary strengths of mmWave radar and monoc-
ular cameras, existing research primarily focuses on sensor-level
fusion for object detection or depth completion, with few studies ad-
dressing their joint potential for large-scale 3D reconstruction and self-
localization. Most existing 3D mapping and localization methods, es-
pecially for large-scale scenarios, still rely on LiDAR sensors. Unlike 
these LiDAR-based approaches, where dense and geometrically uni-
form point clouds facilitate direct geometric processing, the combina-
tion of mmWave radar and monocular cameras introduces signiﬁcant 
heterogeneity at the data level. Speciﬁcally, mmWave radar provides 
sparse and noisy 3D point measurements, lacking appearance informa-
tion, while monocular images contain rich texture and semantic cues 
but without direct depth perception. These two modalities diﬀer fun-
damentally in terms of sensing mechanism, spatial resolution, and data 
representation, forming a typical heterogeneous sensing problem.
Furthermore, the prior real-scene 3D model used for localization is 
typically represented as a dense point cloud or textured mesh, adding 
another modality gap between local observations and the global map. 
Bridging this multi-level heterogeneity, from raw sensor data fusion to 
observation-to-map matching, remains a key technical challenge for en-
abling accurate and robust 3D reconstruction and self-localization in 
GNSS-denied environments. The sparse and noisy nature of radar point 
clouds poses signiﬁcant challenges in structural feature extraction and 
precise map matching, which are essential for accurate self-localization 
within real-scene 3D model.
1.3. Contribution
This paper proposes a novel approach for 3D mapping and precise 
self-localization of UAVs in GNSS-denied environments based on the fu-
sion of mmWave radar and visual information. A dense point cloud is 
generated from monocular images via depth inference and semantic seg-
mentation, while geometric measurements from mmWave radar are in-
tegrated to improve structural completeness and spatial accuracy with-
out relying on costly equipment or extensive labeled datasets. The re-
sulting local point cloud is then matched with high-precision real-world 
3D maps to facilitate robust UAV self-localization in complex urban en-
vironments with window structures serving as geometric anchors for 
ﬁne matching after initial image-based alignment.

2.1.  Setup overview
Fig. 1 illustrates an overview of the UAV self-localization system in 
GNSS-denied environment. In GNSS-denied environments, a stationary 
unmanned platform is equipped with a mmWave radar and a monocu-
lar camera, enabling 3D mapping of surrounding structures as well as 
self-localization within a prior 3D model. This sensor conﬁguration ex-
ploits the complementary advantages of mmWave radar and monocular 
vision, combining robustness of radar in adverse weather and low visi-
bility with camera-based texture and color perception, facilitating both 
structural reconstruction and pose estimation relative to the environ-
ment. In particular, the localization approach beneﬁts from wide ﬁeld-
of-view depth sensing of radar combined with visual feature extraction, 
enhancing robustness in complex environments1.
The structural landmarks utilized in our method are planar wall sur-
faces and corner regions, which satisfy the requirements of being ubiq-
uitous, structurally distinct, and extractable from sparse radar point 
clouds. These landmarks are represented as simpliﬁed geometric primi-
tives (planes and edges), facilitating eﬃcient matching against the prior 
real-scene 3D model of the environment. Due to the non-unique nature 
of these geometric features, accurate localization requires either a coarse 
prior pose to resolve ambiguities. We will demonstrate that even under 
sparse radar data conditions, the extracted structural features are suﬃ-
cient to enable accurate self-localization within the 3D real-scene model. 
Fig. 2 outlines the overall processing pipeline followed throughout this 
paper.

Real-scene 3D models are accurate digital reconstructions generated 
from multi-angle drone imagery using photogrammetry and 3D recon-
struction techniques, oﬀering centimeter-level precision, complete spa-

Considering the large volume and redundancy of complete real-

UAV positioning algorithms, this paper segments the original model to

Fig. 3. During the segmentation process, the area is divided using a ﬁxed

“place name + annotation + slice number”. After segmenting the 3D 
data, corresponding 2D images are generated using the same naming 
convention as the point cloud dataset, ensuring a one-to-one correspon-
dence that facilitates subsequent data processing and analysis.
3.  Proposed method
This section details the proposed self-localization algorithm for UAV 
spatial localization, as depicted in Fig. 2. The process starts with pre-
processing images and radar point clouds, with detection results from 
both sources as input. Visual 3D points are then geometrically reﬁned 
using mmWave radar to achieve eﬀective data fusion. Subsequently, op-
tical image matching is performed using the SIFT algorithm with the 
prepared 2D image dataset, retrieving the corresponding real-scene 3D 
model segments for coarse localization. Finally, pose estimation is con-
ducted by aligning with the real-scene point cloud model, resulting in 
accurate self-localization.
3.1.  Processing of multi-modal data and preliminary point cloud generation
In outdoor 3D perception and positioning tasks for unmanned sys-
tems, single sensor approaches often face trade-oﬀs among accuracy, ro-
bustness, and structural completeness. This paper proposes a mmWave-
based strategy that integrates vision to exploit complementary strengths 
in geometry and stability, enabling more reliable environmental percep-
tion.

3.1.1.  Image information extraction
This paper utilizes existing deep learning models to extract two key 
types of information from images: depth and semantic category data. 
With advances in CNN- and Transformer-based approaches, semantic 
segmentation and depth estimation have evolved into end-to-end, data-
driven tasks, signiﬁcantly improving model expressiveness and robust-
ness.
Semantic segmentation aims to assign a category label to each pixel 
for ﬁne-grained scene understanding. Mask2Former leverages the pow-
erful capabilities of the Transformer architecture to build a uniﬁed and 
eﬃcient model that adapts to various segmentation tasks via mask-
based learning, achieving strong generalization and fully automatic im-
age segmentation without user prompts. As shown in Fig. 4, we adopt 
the publicly available Mask2Former model trained on the COCO dataset 
to obtain building-related semantic masks and corresponding category
labels.
Monocular depth estimation predicts per-pixel depth from a single 
image, supporting the recovery of the overall 3D structure. Depth Any-
thing V2 oﬀers robust monocular depth inference with strong zero-shot 
transferability, generating dense and continuous depth maps from a sin-
gle image for 3D reconstruction in complex environments. An initial 
dense point cloud is generated by integrating semantic segmentation 
masks with monocular depth information. To address frequent depth 
discontinuities at object boundaries, point cloud normal vectors are uti-
lized to identify and eliminate edge anomalies, enhancing geometric 
consistency and supporting more reliable downstream processing, as de-
picted in Fig. 5.
3.1.2.  Radar information extraction
The mmWave radar provides accurate ranging and strong resistance 
to interference under diverse weather conditions, but its sparse point 
clouds with limited structural features hinder reliable point cloud clus-
tering in complex environments.
We propose a semantic-guided clustering method that projects radar 
point clouds onto the image plane and retains only the points falling 
within building regions identiﬁed by semantic segmentation. A density 
constraint is then applied to remove isolated noise. For any query point

3.2.2.  Establishing geometric correspondence
We project radar points onto the image plane and back-project the 
depth map to obtain 3D points in the camera coordinate system, which

3.3.  Image matching
Although the fused point cloud generated from joint reconstruction 
using images and mmWave radar contains rich environmental geometry, 
image-based matching remains preferable during coarse alignment. The 
dense feature content of images in texture-rich areas facilitates rapid 
and precise self-localization. In contrast to the potentially noisy and 
unstable fused point cloud, their direct correspondence with the real-
scene 3D model improves alignment reliability. Consequently, image-
based coarse matching not only improves eﬃciency and accuracy but 
also oﬀers a reliable initial guess for subsequent ﬁne registration and 
pose optimization.
The SIFT algorithm is a robust local feature matching method that ef-
fectively handles variations in scale, rotation, illumination, image blur, 
and viewpoint. Unlike pixel-based methods, the SIFT algorithm main-
tains stable feature extraction even with degraded image quality or 
signiﬁcant scene changes. Optical images are matched against a pre-
constructed image database using SIFT to retrieve candidate views. 
Based on the image matching results, corresponding real-scene 3D 
model data is extracted from the point cloud dataset to estimate the 
approximate location within the large-scale model, as illustrated in
Fig. 6.

This paper extracts texture keypoints based on the Hue-Saturation–
Intensity (HIS) color space. Following the RGB-to-HSI conversion, the 
hue and saturation channels describe color type and intensity, allowing 
for the identiﬁcation of regions with strong color characteristics,such 
as glass. To ensure adaptability to point density and window size, the 
neighborhood radius is set as 𝑟= 5𝛿𝑝, with 𝛿𝑝 denoting the average point 
spacing. For each point, the mean hue diﬀerence and average saturation 
within its neighborhood are combined to obtain a color gradient, which 
highlights edges and reﬂective features. After normalization, points with 
gradient values exceeding a preset threshold are selected as texture key-
points.
The density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DB-
SCAN) is ﬁrst applied to the identiﬁed keypoints, generating a initial set 
of candidate window regions. A secondary clustering strategy is then 
employed to further reﬁne the segmentation of window areas. Clusters 
with abnormal bounding box dimensions are removed. Subsequently, 
cluster merging is guided by three types of similarity metrics: (1) Ge-
ometric size similarity, where the aspect ratios of bounding boxes are 
compared to determine the consistency in window dimensions; (2) Pro-
jected contour similarity, in which cluster point clouds are projected 
onto their principal planes via Principal Component Analysis (PCA). The 
aspect ratios of the resulting 2D contour acts as an indicator of shape 
consistency, helping to mitigate the eﬀects of minor viewpoint devia-
tions or partial occlusions; (3) Color similarity, where color features are 
converted into the Lab color space and normalized histograms are com-
puted to measure diﬀerences in appearance, capturing consistency in 
window frames or glass. The three similarity metrics are fused with pre-
deﬁned weights to deﬁne the distance measure for agglomerative hier-
archical clustering, producing window groupings with consistent struc-
tural and visual features. Each resulting group comprises at least two 
window clusters.

4.2.  Radar data processing
The mmWave radar is a robust sensing system capable of reliably 
detecting target positions and velocities under various challenging con-
ditions. The raw radar data collected during the experiment undergo 
a processing pipeline including ADC calibration, range FFT, Doppler 
FFT, constant false alarm rate (CFAR) detection, and direction of ar-
rival (DoA) estimation, producing point clouds with 3D spatial coordi-
nates (x, y, z) as well as velocity and reﬂectivity attributes. The spatial 
points capture the geometric proﬁle, structural characteristics, and mo-
tion state of the detected targets. Additionally, Doppler information is 
exploited to estimate the motion of detected points, facilitating the ex-
traction of static target point clouds.

5.  Conclusion
This paper addresses the problem of self-localization for UAVs under 
GNSS-denied conditions by proposing a 3D mapping and localization 
method that integrates mmWave radar with visual sensing. An initial 
dense point cloud is constructed through image-based depth estimation 
and semantic segmentation, while geometric errors are corrected using 
mmWave radar point clouds, enhancing overall mapping accuracy. To 
enhance structural reliability, a semantic-guided clustering and struc-
tural extraction method is introduced to ﬁlter radar noise and identify 
key architectural features such as wall surfaces. During positioning, the 
spatial distribution of window cavities is leveraged as auxiliary con-
straints to achieve precise alignment between local point clouds and the 
real-scene 3D model. Experimental results demonstrate that the pro-
posed method delivers sub-meter self-localization accuracy in typical 
building environments, while maintaining low system complexity and 
deployment cost, thereby oﬀering a promising solution for UAV self-
localization in complex urban scenarios.
Nevertheless, the study has several limitations. First, the experi-
ments were conducted in a localized campus environment, which may 
restrict generalization to larger and more diverse urban areas. Second, 
the system has not yet been deployed on UAVs, and real-time embedded 
implementation remains challenging due to payload, power, and pro-
cessing constraints. Third, the current framework is tailored for outdoor 
façades and does not yet address indoor environments, where multipath 
eﬀects and weaker radar returns require specialized treatment. Future 
work will therefore focus on extending the framework to larger-scale 
and more diverse urban areas, as well as exploring adaptability to diﬀer-
ent architectural styles. Additional eﬀorts will be devoted to improving 
detection inside buildings through radar denoising and SLAM-based in-
tegration, developing lightweight real-time algorithms suitable for UAV 
platforms, and incorporating advanced fusion models such as transform-
ers or graph neural networks to further enhance accuracy. These direc-
tions will broaden the scalability and robustness of the system, making 
it a more practical solution for UAV self-localization in complex and 
dynamic urban environments.
