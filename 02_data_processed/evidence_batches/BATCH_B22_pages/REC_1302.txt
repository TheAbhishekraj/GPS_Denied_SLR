Abstract
Achieving scalable swarm autonomy in Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS)-denied
and communication-constrained environments remains an open challenge at the intersec-
tion of robotics, distributed optimization, and reinforcement learning. Existing unmanned
aerial vehicle (UAV) autonomy frameworks typically decouple navigation, perception, and
distributed learning, while assuming centralized coordination or reliable global positioning.
This paper introduces a unified federated deep reinforcement learning architecture that
enables GNSS-independent multi-UAV autonomy through the principled integration of
multi-modal perception, decentralized policy optimization, energy-aware control, and
edge-compliant inference. The proposed framework formulates joint navigation and dy-
namic target tracking as a partially observable Markov decision process optimized via
Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) over structured motion primitives. A communication-
efficient federated learning mechanism enables distributed policy convergence under
non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) agent experiences without sharing
raw data, establishing a scalable alternative to centralized training. To address sim-to-real
discrepancies, the architecture incorporates domain randomization, structured sensor noise
modeling, and curriculum-based training to promote robust zero-shot deployment. Multi-
agent simulation experiments evaluate the swarm-level and federated-learning behavior of
the proposed framework, while single-UAV field deployment evidence using a DJI Matrice
100 platform supports the feasibility of the onboard sensing, perception, and edge-inference
pipeline under realistic outdoor conditions. The evaluation demonstrates stable decen-
tralized convergence, improved energy efficiency relative to centralized baselines, robust
target-tracking performance under GNSS-denied conditions, and real-time edge-compliant
inference. The results establish that federated reinforcement learning can serve as a viable
systems-level foundation for resilient, energy-aware, and scalable aerial swarm intelligence,
advancing the state of the art in distributed autonomous robotics.

1. Introduction
1.1. Problem Context
The increasing operational demand for autonomous unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs)
has made the development of advanced autonomous navigation systems independent
of Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) both critical and urgent [1]. In modern op-
erational environments, such as urban canyons with dense high-rise buildings, indoor
infrastructures (e.g., industrial facilities, warehouses, tunnels), forested or densely vege-
tated areas, and regions affected by GNSS jamming or spoofing, satellite-based positioning
is often degraded, unreliable, or completely unavailable [2,3]. These constraints pose sig-
nificant challenges for localization, navigation, obstacle avoidance, flight stability, and
multi-agent coordination [4,5].
At the same time, the missions assigned to UAV systems are becoming increasingly
complex and demanding. An example could be in search-and-rescue operations, UAVs
must autonomously navigate unknown or partially destroyed environments while de-
tecting and tracking human targets in real time [6]. In surveillance and reconnaissance
scenarios, maintaining stable tracking under dynamic conditions and limited visibil-
ity is essential [7]. During disaster response missions, UAVs are required to operate in
infrastructure-compromised environments with unstable communication links [6,8]. In
tactical operations, resilience to hostile interference and the ability to function without
remote control are crucial [8]. In addition to GNSS disruption, UAV systems may also be
exposed to communication-level threats, including cyber-attacks and network intrusions.
Recent studies have explored intrusion detection mechanisms in UAV communication
networks using deep learning approaches, as well as system-level strategies for improving
robustness and data reliability in autonomous UAV operations [9,10].
These evolving operational requirements highlight the necessity for decentralized,
intelligent, and resilient autonomy frameworks capable of ensuring reliable performance
in environments where GNSS availability cannot be assumed [11,12].
1.2. Core Challenges
Autonomous UAV operation in GNSS-denied environments faces a set of fundamental
technical challenges that greatly complicate reliable deployment. First, GNSS unavailability
removes access to absolute positioning, forcing the system to depend solely on onboard
sensing and relative localization techniques [3,5]. Without satellite-based corrections, nav-
igation must be maintained through vision, inertial sensing, or learned policies, which
increases vulnerability to drift and cumulative estimation errors [4].
A second major challenge is partial observability. Real-world environments are dy-
namic, cluttered, and often unpredictable. Obstacles may be occluded, lighting conditions
may vary, and sensor measurements can be noisy or incomplete. UAV agents must there-
fore operate under uncertainty, making sequential decisions without full knowledge of the
global state of the environment, a condition formally modeled as a partially observable
Markov decision process (POMDP) [13,14].
Operational energy constraints further restrict autonomy. UAV platforms are inher-
ently limited by battery capacity, and advanced perception and learning algorithms increase
computational load. Balancing navigation performance, tracking accuracy, and energy effi-
ciency becomes essential to prevent premature mission termination and ensure sustained
operation [15,16].
Furthermore, bandwidth limitations pose significant constraints on communication
between UAVs or between agents and ground stations. In disaster zones or contested
environments, connectivity is often intermittent or degraded. Because UAVs cannot reli-
ably depend on continuous communication for centralized coordination, there is a critical

Finally, enabling multi-agent coordination without centralized control introduces

1.3. Research Gap & Core Contributions
Despite significant progress in UAV autonomy, critical limitations remain within
the existing body of work. A substantial portion of prior research treats navigation and
dynamic target tracking as isolated tasks rather than addressing them jointly [7,15]. More-
over, many deep reinforcement learning (DRL) frameworks rely on centralized training
architectures that assume stable communication and full observability [15,17]. While cen-
tralized approaches simplify convergence, they are fundamentally ill-suited for bandwidth-
constrained or contested environments where persistent connectivity cannot be guaranteed.
Furthermore, existing tracking systems often implicitly assume GNSS-based stabilization,
and while visual-inertial odometry offers an alternative, it is prone to drift and high compu-
tational costs on embedded platforms [4,5]. Finally, a large body of work remains validated
exclusively in simulation, lacking systematic methodologies to address the sensor noise,
environmental variability, and hardware limitations inherent to the physical world [21].
Existing studies typically address these components in isolation, either federated learning
without full perception-control integration [22,23], edge inference without collaborative
learning [18], or reinforcement learning without bandwidth-aware synchronization [15].
To the best of our knowledge, no existing architecture fully unifies reinforcement
learning, real-time edge deployment, and multi-modal perception into a single, GNSS-
denied swarm autonomy framework. To bridge this critical systems-level gap, this work
proposes a unified, scalable, and federated-decentralized DRL framework designed to
enable robust UAV autonomy in GNSS-denied environments. Specifically, this paper makes
the following primary contributions:
Unified Navigation and Tracking: We introduce a single combined control system
that jointly optimizes obstacle-aware navigation and dynamic human target tracking,
eliminating reliance on centralized coordination.
GNSS-Independent Multi-Modal Perception: The framework ensures robust envi-
ronmental awareness by fusing RGB, depth, and inertial (IMU) measurements, allowing
UAVs to operate reliably in environments affected by signal obstruction, jamming, or
spoofing [3,5].
Communication-Aware Federated Optimization: To support scalable multi-agent
learning without transmitting heavy raw data, we implement a federated reinforce-
ment learning mechanism inspired by communication-efficient decentralized learning
paradigms [24]. Agents collaboratively improve a shared Proximal Policy Optimiza-
tion (PPO)-based policy [25] over discrete motion primitives by exchanging compressed
model updates.

2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Related Work
2.1.1. Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) for UAV Navigation
DRL has emerged as a dominant paradigm for autonomous UAV navigation in com-
plex and partially observable environments. Policy-gradient methods, particularly Proximal
Policy Optimization (PPO), have gained widespread adoption due to their training sta-
bility and robustness under stochastic dynamics [25]. PPO-based navigation frameworks
have demonstrated effective obstacle avoidance and goal-directed control in indoor and
cluttered environments [15]. In this context, ongoing research efforts within our group
have also investigated learning-based navigation strategies and perception-driven control
mechanisms for UAV autonomy, contributing incremental advances in mission efficiency
and environmental adaptability [26,27].
Recent advancements incorporate attention mechanisms and transformer-based archi-
tectures to enhance long-range spatial reasoning and temporal consistency in sequential
decision-making [28]. Additionally, multi-sensor fusion strategies combining vision and
depth or LiDAR inputs have been shown to improve environmental awareness and naviga-
tion robustness [4].
Despite these developments, most DRL-based UAV navigation systems rely on central-
ized training pipelines and, to do so, assume stable communication infrastructures [15,29].
Furthermore, many frameworks evaluate performance using static goal-reaching tasks
rather than dynamic target tracking scenarios, limiting applicability in real-world missions
requiring joint navigation and pursuit under uncertainty.
2.1.2. Vision-Based UAV Tracking
Vision-based tracking is fundamental for surveillance, search-and-rescue, and disaster
response operations [6]. Modern approaches integrate object detection with online tracking
algorithms such as DeepSort [30] or CenterTrack [31], enabling frame-to-frame identity
association and trajectory estimation.
Thermal-enhanced tracking has demonstrated improved robustness in low-visibility
or forested environments [32,33], while occlusion-aware re-identification techniques aim
to mitigate identity switching in cluttered scenes. Multi-modal fusion approaches further
improve detection reliability under illumination variability and partial occlusions.

2.1.3. GNSS-Free Localization
GNSS-independent localization has been extensively addressed through SLAM and
Visual-Inertial Odometry (VIO) systems [4,5]. Vision-based navigation using deep semantic
cues has also been explored for real-time autonomous UAV inspection tasks, demonstrating
the relevance of perception-driven navigation in structured operational environments [34].
These techniques estimate UAV pose through continuous integration of visual and inertial
measurements, enabling operation in indoor and urban canyon environments [3].
While accurate in short-term estimation, SLAM and VIO methods remain susceptible
to drift accumulation, particularly in large-scale or feature-deprived environments. Loop
closure techniques alleviate this issue but increase algorithmic and computational complex-
ity. Moreover, deploying full SLAM pipelines on edge hardware introduces latency and
memory constraints that may conflict with real-time control requirements [18].
Consequently, purely geometric localization approaches may be insufficient for scal-
able swarm autonomy, motivating the integration of learning-based decision policies
capable of compensating for localization uncertainty.
2.1.4. Federated & Decentralized Learning in UAV Swarms
Scalable swarm autonomy requires distributed intelligence mechanisms capable of
operating under bandwidth constraints and intermittent connectivity [8]. Federated learn-
ing has emerged as a communication-efficient paradigm for collaborative model training
without centralized data aggregation [17]. Extensions to federated meta-learning enable
adaptation across heterogeneous agents [11].
In the context of UAV systems, decentralized reinforcement learning and energy-aware
DRL formulations have been proposed to balance mission performance and battery con-
straints [13]. Consensus-based coordination strategies further support distributed control
under limited communication [19,35].
Nevertheless, existing studies often remain confined to simulation environments and
do not fully integrate federated reinforcement learning with perception-driven control
pipelines deployed on edge hardware. Real-world validation of federated DRL for GNSS-
denied UAV navigation and tracking remains limited, particularly in scenarios combining
multi-modal perception, decentralized optimization, and sim-to-real transfer.

At the front-end, a multi-modal perception module processes raw sensory inputs from
RGB, thermal, depth, and inertial sensors. This module extracts semantic and geometric rep-
resentations of the environment, including human target detections and obstacle structures.
The resulting features are forwarded to a tracking and localization unit, which estimates
the relative target position and constructs a structured representation of environmental
constraints suitable for decision-making under partial observability.
The processed state representation is subsequently provided to the reinforcement learn-
ing (RL) control module, which generates motion commands through a policy optimized
using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). The policy outputs discrete flight primitives
that govern low-level control, enabling obstacle-aware navigation and dynamic human
target tracking within GNSS-denied environments.
To support collaborative learning across multiple UAV agents, a federated update
layer aggregates locally trained policy parameters using a communication-efficient strategy.
Model aggregation follows a federated averaging (FedAvg) scheme [17], allowing decen-
tralized policy synchronization without sharing raw sensory data. Instead of transmitting
local experience buffers, agents exchange compressed model parameters, preserving band-
width efficiency and enabling scalable adaptation under intermittent connectivity and
heterogeneous data distributions.
Finally, the entire pipeline is deployed on embedded edge hardware platforms to
ensure real-time inference and control within strict latency constraints. This tight inte-
gration of perception, distributed optimization, and edge deployment enables scalable
swarm autonomy while maintaining GNSS independence, communication efficiency, and
operational robustness.

2.2.2. Multi-Modal Perception Module
At the front-end, the perception module processes synchronized RGB, thermal, depth,
and inertial measurements to generate robust environmental representations. Human target
detection is performed using a YOLOv8-based architecture [36], optionally employing a
lightweight YOLOv8-nano backbone for embedded deployment [37]. The model achieves
real-time inference while maintaining detection robustness under partial occlusion.
Scene-level semantic understanding is provided by a DeepLabv3+ segmentation net-
work [38], extracting structural information including walkable regions and static obstacles.
The segmentation output is transformed into an occupancy-aware spatial representation
suitable for downstream control [39].
To enhance robustness, RGB and thermal detections are fused through confidence-
weighted integration. In scenarios of visual degradation, thermal-driven localization
operates as a fallback mechanism, mitigating detection dropout.
Detected targets are projected into a relative coordinate frame using geometric trans-
formations and depth estimation. A Kalman filter smooths these relative position estimates
and mitigates sensor noise. During temporary occlusions, triggered when detection confi-
dence drops below a predefined threshold, the filter seamlessly transitions the system into
a prediction-based tracking mode, preserving trajectory continuity.

2.3.2. Energy-Aware Route Optimization
Autonomous UAV navigation in GNSS-denied environments requires not only accu-
rate tracking and obstacle avoidance but also energy-efficient trajectory planning. Given the
inherent battery limitations of aerial platforms, route optimization must balance mission
objectives with resource constraints. To address this, we define a composite cost function
that captures path deviation, energy expenditure, and obstacle-related risk. The total route
cost is formulated as
C = wd ∗D + we ∗E + wo ∗O
where the weighting coefficients wd, we, wo are positive scalars that regulate the trade-off
among navigation accuracy, energy efficiency, and safety.
To ensure stable tracking performance, the path deviation metric D measures the
geometric divergence between the executed UAV trajectory and the desired pursuit path
relative to the tracked target, often represented as the shortest path. This term is critical for
maintaining trajectory smoothness and preventing excessive lateral oscillations. Addition-
ally, the estimated energy consumption E is modeled as a function of velocity magnitude,
acceleration effort, and control actuation frequency. Since propulsion energy dominates

[p.11]

UAV power usage, this specific cost term penalizes aggressive maneuvers and prolonged
high-speed flight. Similar energy-aware deep reinforcement learning formulations have
been explored in decentralized UAV control contexts [12], highlighting the importance
of incorporating battery constraints directly into the optimization objective. Finally, to
enforce safety, the obstacle risk metric O encodes proximity-based penalties derived from
depth sensing. Higher costs are dynamically assigned to trajectories approaching static or

In semi-structured or partially known environments, the cost-based formulation can

Balancing energy consumption and mission performance introduces an inherent trade-

or increased tracking loss. Conversely, minimizing deviation without energy constraints
results in aggressive maneuvers and reduced mission endurance.

that the marginal gain in tracking precision does not outweigh the marginal cost in energy
expenditure. Such multi-objective optimization within reinforcement learning aligns with
established policy optimization frameworks [14], while maintaining practical feasibility for
embedded UAV deployment.

2.4.1. Federated & Decentralized Reinforcement Learning
To enable scalable swarm autonomy under communication and resource constraints,
we extend the reinforcement learning framework to a federated and decentralized setting.
The objective is to allow multiple UAV agents to collaboratively improve a shared policy
while preserving local autonomy and minimizing communication overhead.

Local On-Device Learning Under Edge Constraints
Each UAV agent maintains a local copy of the policy network parameterized by θi.
Policy optimization is performed onboard using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [25],
based solely on locally collected trajectories.
Agents perform on-device policy updates by collecting experience tuples (st, at, rt,
st+1), representing the current state, action taken, reward achieved and next state accord-
ingly, which are stored in a bounded experience buffer. After accumulating a predefined
number of transitions, local PPO updates are executed using mini-batch stochastic gradient
descent. This on-device training paradigm eliminates the need for centralized experience
aggregation and preserves data locality.
To accommodate memory limitations on embedded edge hardware, the experience
replay buffer is implemented as a sliding window with fixed capacity. Older trajectories
are discarded to maintain bounded storage requirements and prevent stale gradient contri-
butions. Trajectory normalization and advantage estimation are performed locally prior
to aggregation.
Given the computational limitations of Jetson-class devices, model architectures are
optimized for parameter efficiency and inference latency. Local training frequency is
regulated to avoid interference with real-time control loops, ensuring that policy updates
do not compromise flight stability.

2.4.2. Sim-to-Real Transfer Strategy
Bridging the gap between simulation and real-world deployment remains a central
challenge in learning-based robotics systems. Policies trained exclusively in simulation
often suffer performance degradation when exposed to sensor noise, environmental vari-
ability, and unmodeled dynamics [48]. To mitigate this sim-to-real discrepancy, we employ
a structured transfer strategy combining domain randomization, noise injection, curriculum
learning, and systematic transfer evaluation.
Robustness-Oriented Training Strategies
Domain randomization is applied during simulation training to expose the reinforce-
ment learning policy to a wide distribution of environmental variations. By intentionally
perturbing visual, physical, and sensor parameters, the learned policy becomes robust to
real-world uncertainty [49,50]. The following factors are randomized during training:
•
Lighting conditions: variation in intensity, direction, and color temperature.
•
Textures: randomized ground patterns, building facades, and obstacle surfaces.
•
Occlusions: partial visibility of the target and intermittent obstruction events.
•
Wind simulation: stochastic lateral and vertical disturbances affecting UAV dynamics.
•
Sensor perturbations: camera blur, exposure shifts, and depth sensor noise.
This stochastic training environment forces the policy to rely on invariant features
rather than simulation-specific artifacts, improving generalization during deployment.
Beyond environmental randomization, explicit sensor-level noise modeling is incorporated
to better approximate real hardware behavior.
•
RGB corruption: Gaussian noise, motion blur, and brightness jitter are injected into
camera streams.
•
Thermal noise: additive noise and contrast variability simulate real thermal sensor
behavior under changing ambient conditions.
•
IMU drift modeling: bias drift and stochastic noise are applied to inertial measure-
ments to emulate cumulative orientation errors.
•
Depth dropout simulation: random pixel dropout and range truncation simulate
structured-light and stereo sensor imperfections.
These perturbations prevent overfitting to idealized sensor outputs and promote
robustness against real-world measurement uncertainty.

To stabilize training under high environmental variability, a curriculum learning strat-
egy is adopted. Rather than exposing the policy to maximum difficulty from the outset,
environmental complexity increases progressively. Training begins in simplified environ-
ments with static obstacles and predictable target motion. Subsequently, the difficulty is
increased by introducing dynamic obstacles, stronger wind disturbances, partial occlu-
sions, and reduced sensor reliability. This staged progression improves policy convergence
stability and mitigates catastrophic learning failures. From an optimization perspective,
curriculum learning reduces gradient variance during early PPO updates [25], enabling
smoother policy improvement before confronting high-complexity scenarios.
Transfer Evaluation
To assess sim-to-real robustness, we conduct structured transfer evaluation across
multiple real-world scenarios. Two primary metrics are analyzed:
Performance Degradation Analysis: The relative drop in tracking accuracy, naviga-
tion deviation, and energy efficiency between simulation and real-world deployment is
quantified. This provides a direct measure of sim-to-real generalization capacity [51].
Zero-Shot Deployment Results: The trained policy is deployed in real-world environ-
ments without additional fine-tuning to evaluate zero-shot performance. Stability, tracking
continuity, and collision avoidance are measured over multiple flight trials. Empirically,
policies trained under domain randomization exhibit significantly reduced performance
degradation compared to baseline simulation-trained models [52].

3.3. Ablation Studies
To systematically evaluate the contribution of each architectural component, we con-
ducted a series of controlled ablation experiments. In each configuration, a single module
was removed or modified while maintaining identical training conditions, hyperparame-
ters, and evaluation protocols. Performance was assessed using tracking accuracy (IoU),
navigation deviation, energy consumption, robustness score, and convergence speed. All
reported values represent the mean over five independent trials.
Effect of Thermal Fusion
To quantify the contribution of thermal sensing, the thermal modality was removed
and the perception module operated solely on RGB and depth inputs. The absence of
thermal fusion resulted in a reduction in mean IoU from 0.83 to 0.76 and a decrease in
robustness under low-visibility and occlusion scenarios. Tracking continuity was par-
ticularly affected in forested and low-contrast environments. These results indicate that
multi-modal fusion significantly enhances perception reliability and target discrimination
under challenging environmental conditions.
Effect of Federated Learning
To evaluate the federated layer, agents were trained independently without parameter
synchronization. While individual tracking performance remained comparable (IoU = 0.81),
convergence required substantially more training steps, and swarm-level behavioral consis-
tency decreased. Navigation deviation increased from 1.2 m to 1.5 m, and robustness under
heterogeneous environmental exposure declined. These findings demonstrate that feder-
ated averaging improves distributed convergence stability and mitigates policy divergence
under non-IID experiences.
Effect of Domain Randomization
Domain randomization and structured sensor noise injection were removed during
training to assess sim-to-real transfer robustness. When deployed in real-world GNSS-
denied scenarios, policies trained without environmental variability exhibited significant
degradation due to overfitting to the idealized simulation dynamics. IoU decreased to 0.72,
navigation deviation increased to 1.8 m, and energy consumption rose due to unstable
corrective maneuvers. The results confirm that environmental stochasticity during training
is critical for robust zero-shot deployment.

4. Discussion
The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed federated and GNSS-
independent UAV autonomy framework achieves a balanced integration of perception,
control, distributed learning, and edge deployment. This section discusses the principal
strengths of the system as well as its current limitations and practical considerations.
4.1. Advantages
Scalability: The federated reinforcement learning architecture enables scalable
multi-agent training without centralized data aggregation. By exchanging compressed
model parameters rather than raw experience data, the system reduces communica-
tion overhead while preserving collaborative policy improvement. This design supports
swarm-level deployment under bandwidth-constrained conditions and heterogeneous
environmental exposure.
GNSS Independence: A key strength of the framework is its complete independence
from satellite-based localization. Through multi-modal perception, relative target estima-
tion, and reinforcement learning-based control, the UAV maintains stable navigation and
tracking under GNSS dropout and signal-denied scenarios. This capability is particularly
relevant for urban canyons, indoor infrastructures, and adversarial environments.
Energy-Aware Autonomy: The integration of energy-aware cost modeling into the
reward function ensures balanced mission execution. Unlike naive tracking policies that
prioritize short-term precision, the proposed formulation explicitly accounts for energy
consumption and obstacle risk. This results in smoother trajectories, improved mission
endurance, and safer maneuvering behavior.
Edge Deployability: The architecture is explicitly designed for deployment on em-
bedded edge hardware. Model compression, discrete action primitives, and bounded
https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics15143064
[p.23]
Electronics 2026, 15, 3064

inference latency (<120 ms) ensure real-time control without reliance on external com-
putation resources. This edge-compliant design strengthens operational autonomy in
communication-limited settings.
Field Deployment Feasibility: Unlike purely simulation-only studies, the proposed
framework is complemented by single-UAV field deployment evidence using a DJI Matrice
100 platform. This supports the practical feasibility of the onboard sensing, perception,
and edge-computing pipeline under semi-structured outdoor conditions. Full physical
swarm-scale validation remains outside the scope of the present study and is identified as
future work.
4.2. Limitations
Extreme Lighting Conditions: Although thermal fusion improves robustness, extreme
lighting transitions, such as direct sunlight glare or complete darkness, can still degrade
RGB-based detection reliability.
Wind Disturbances: While moderate wind disturbances are simulated during training,
sudden high-magnitude gusts introduce nonlinear aerodynamic effects that are not fully
captured by the current dynamic model. This may lead to temporary tracking instability or
increased energy expenditure under severe environmental conditions.
Edge Training Constraints: On-device training is limited by computational and mem-
ory constraints of embedded platforms. Although local PPO updates are feasible, large-
scale model architectures or frequent update cycles may introduce thermal throttling or
latency spikes.
Federated Synchronization Delay: Asynchronous federated aggregation improves robust-
ness to connectivity interruptions; however, prolonged synchronization delays may increase
policy divergence among agents operating under highly heterogeneous environments.
Physical Swarm-Scale Validation: A limitation of the present study is that physical
field deployment evidence is limited to single-UAV trials. Accordingly, the real-world
deployment component supports platform-level feasibility, onboard sensing, and edge-
inference validation, while swarm-level coordination, federated aggregation, and scalability
are evaluated in simulation. Full physical validation with multiple UAVs will require
synchronized multi-UAV flight logs, inter-agent communication measurements, and real-
world federated aggregation analysis.
Overall, the proposed framework demonstrates that integrating multi-modal percep-
tion, energy-aware reinforcement learning, federated optimization, and edge deployment
within a unified architecture enables robust GNSS-independent UAV autonomy. While
certain environmental and hardware limitations persist, the results indicate strong potential
for scalable and resilient deployment in real-world operational scenarios.

frameworks prioritizing robustness, scalability, and communication efficiency.
The framework also holds direct relevance for defense and security applications, where
GNSS signals may be degraded, jammed, or spoofed. GNSS-independent navigation com-
bined with onboard perception enhances operational resilience in contested environments.
Furthermore, communication-efficient federated learning enables coordinated multi-agent
adaptation without transmitting raw sensory data, an important property for secure and
privacy-sensitive operations conducted under adversarial conditions.
In the context of disaster-response robotics, the proposed architecture supports reliable
deployment in environments where infrastructure damage obstructs satellite signals and
communication reliability is compromised. Multi-modal perception improves robustness
in visually degraded settings, while energy-aware control extends operational endurance,
an essential requirement for prolonged search-and-rescue missions. The ability to perform
zero-shot deployment following simulation training further reduces preparation time in
time-critical emergency scenarios.
The work additionally contributes to research on federated reinforcement learning
under heterogeneous and non-IID data distributions. Because each UAV experiences
environment-specific trajectories, gradient updates are inherently biased by local obser-
vations. The empirical findings suggest that bounded synchronization intervals and
communication-efficient aggregation mitigate divergence while preserving distributed
learning benefits. This supports the applicability of federated learning beyond static super-
vised tasks, extending it to sequential decision-making problems characterized by dynamic,
agent-specific state distributions.
Finally, the proposed system exemplifies the broader convergence of edge AI and
distributed intelligence. By executing perception, policy inference, and partial policy
optimization directly on embedded hardware, the framework reduces dependence on
cloud infrastructure and centralized computation. This architectural shift reflects a grow-
ing trend toward localized intelligence augmented by intermittent collaborative learning.
The tight integration of edge-based inference with federated optimization illustrates how
distributed autonomous systems can achieve both real-time responsiveness and scalable
collective adaptation.
Overall, the theoretical and practical implications of this work highlight the feasibility
of resilient, swarm-ready UAV autonomy grounded in federated reinforcement learning
and edge-compliant system design. The presented framework thus contributes not only a
functional architecture but also a systems-level paradigm for distributed aerial intelligence
in complex and constrained environments.
4.4. Future Research Directions
While the proposed framework demonstrates strong performance in GNSS-denied and
communication-constrained environments, several promising research directions emerge
to extend both its theoretical foundations and practical scalability. The convergence of
federated reinforcement learning, edge AI, and swarm robotics opens a broad design space
that remains only partially explored.
One important direction concerns the integration of hierarchical multi-agent reinforce-
ment learning architectures. A hierarchical formulation could decompose decision-making
into strategic and tactical layers, where high-level policies coordinate swarm-wide objec-
tives and mission planning, while low-level controllers handle reactive navigation and

target tracking. Such decomposition may improve scalability in large swarms by reducing
coordination complexity and facilitating modular convergence across distributed agents.
Advancements in transformer-based spatial reasoning also represent a compelling
extension. Although the current framework relies primarily on convolutional perception
pipelines, attention-based models could enhance long-range spatial dependency modeling
and temporal coherence. Transformer architectures may improve performance in cluttered
urban environments, multi-target tracking scenarios, and complex dynamic settings where
contextual reasoning over extended spatial regions is required.
Meta-reinforcement learning offers another promising pathway toward improved
adaptability. By learning environment-invariant priors during simulation training, UAV
agents could achieve rapid online adaptation to previously unseen environments without
extensive retraining. Such zero-shot or few-shot adaptation capabilities would be par-
ticularly valuable in disaster-response missions, where operational conditions may vary
unpredictably and immediate deployment is essential.
Beyond collaborative policy optimization, future swarm systems may incorporate
decentralized task allocation mechanisms. Auction-based coordination schemes, consensus-
driven role assignments, or market-inspired allocation strategies could complement fed-
erated learning by enabling dynamic mission reconfiguration. Integrating task allocation
with distributed reinforcement learning would allow swarms to balance coverage, pursuit,
and surveillance objectives more effectively.
As UAV autonomy systems expand into safety-critical domains, explainability be-
comes increasingly important. Explainable reinforcement learning techniques could pro-
vide interpretable policy representations, enabling post-mission auditing, operator trust
enhancement, and regulatory compliance. Transparent decision-making models may also
facilitate debugging and validation in complex multi-agent scenarios.
Formal verification of learned UAV policies remains an open and challenging problem.
Establishing safety guarantees under bounded uncertainty, proving collision avoidance
properties, and ensuring stability under GNSS-denied conditions are critical steps to-
ward certifiable autonomous flight systems. Bridging the gap between data-driven policy
optimization and formal safety analysis will be essential for large-scale deployment in
regulated environments.
From an experimental perspective, hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) validation represents
an intermediate step between pure simulation and full deployment. Real-time interac-
tion between physical flight controllers and high-fidelity simulation environments could
provide safer and more scalable testing of distributed learning policies before large-scale
field trials.
Future work will also include same-hardware latency benchmarking against alter-
native baseline perception and control pipelines. This would better contextualize the
reported Jetson Xavier NX inference latency and determine whether the observed real-time
performance reflects architectural efficiency or hardware-level constraints. Furthermore,
future work will include controlled side-by-side flight experiments to directly compare
policies trained with and without energy-aware reward shaping, as well as to analyze the
correlation between reward-based energy proxies and actual battery consumption (Wh).
Future work will include physical swarm-scale validation with multiple UAV agents,
including synchronized multi-UAV flight trajectories, inter-agent communication mea-
surements, real-world federated aggregation logs, and quantitative analysis of policy
divergence under non-IID field conditions. Such experiments are necessary to fully assess
the scalability limits of federated reinforcement learning in real-world operational contexts.
Collectively, these research directions indicate that the development of distributed
UAV intelligence is transitioning from isolated algorithmic advances toward integrated,

ing theoretical rigor with systems-level engineering, enabling the deployment of resilient
swarm intelligence in complex and dynamic environments.
5. Conclusions
This work presented a unified decentralized–federated deep reinforcement learning
architecture for GNSS-independent UAV autonomy. The proposed framework integrates
multi-modal perception, energy-aware control, distributed policy optimization, and edge-
compliant deployment within a cohesive and scalable system design.
By combining RGB, thermal, depth, and IMU sensing with PPO-based control and
communication-efficient federated aggregation, the system enables robust navigation and
human target tracking without reliance on satellite positioning. The energy-aware reward
formulation further ensures balanced mission execution, promoting smoother trajectories
and improved endurance under real-world operational constraints.
Multi-agent simulation experiments and single-UAV field deployment evidence
demonstrated the feasibility of the proposed framework. The simulation results support the
swarm-level and federated-learning aspects of the method, while the field deployment evi-
dence supports the practical feasibility of onboard perception, sensing, and edge-compliant
inference under realistic outdoor conditions. Comparative analysis and ablation studies
confirmed that performance gains arise from the principled integration of perception fusion,
decentralized learning, domain randomization, and energy-aware optimization.
Overall, the results indicate that scalable swarm-ready UAV autonomy is a promis-
ing direction through the convergence of federated reinforcement learning and edge AI,
with full physical swarm validation remaining an important future step. The presented
framework provides a practical and extensible foundation for resilient, distributed aerial
intelligence in communication-constrained and GNSS-denied environments.
