Abstract—Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) have become
indispensable in search and rescue (SAR) missions by enabling
rapid aerial reconnaissance in unstructured and GPS denied
environments that are inaccessible to ground teams. Conventional
autonomous navigation systems typically depend on heavy sensor
suites such as LiDAR or reliable GPS signals, which are often
impractical for lightweight and low-cost rescue drones operating
in cluttered indoor spaces, including partially collapsed buildings
in disaster zones. To address this limitation, this paper introduces
a cost-effective, fully autonomous UAV navigation framework
that leverages Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) to replace
brittle rule-based decision logic with adaptive policy learning.
The framework integrates real-time human detection using a
monocular RGB camera and YOLOv8n, and evaluates two DRL
strategies for autonomous indoor maneuvering toward detected
humans, specifically Deep Q-Network (DQN) and Proximal Policy
Optimization (PPO). Simulation results indicate that rule-based
navigation shows low generalization to dynamic and unstructured
conditions. Although DQN shows limited reliability, the PPO
based agent achieves higher navigation success rates and more
stable decision policies. The proposed system is further validated
through Sim to Real transfer using a DJI Tello drone on
indoor flight tasks, demonstrating practical deployment without
expensive sensing hardware. The results confirm the feasibility
of lightweight, low-cost, vision based autonomous UAV agents
capable of adaptive indoor navigation toward detected humans.

I. INTRODUCTION
The increasing frequency and severity of natural disasters
ranging from earthquakes to urban structural collapses have
underscored the urgent need for rapid, autonomous search
and rescue (SAR) technologies. In the immediate aftermath
of such events, the golden hour is critical for victim survival,
yet ground access is often severed by debris or hazardous
conditions. Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) offer a promis-
ing solution, capable of traversing three dimensional spaces
to locate victims [1]–[4]. However, the unique constraints
of disaster zones render many standard commercial drone
technologies ineffective.
The primary operational challenge in these scenarios is the
denial of Global Positioning System (GPS) signals. Whether
due to urban canyons, dense canopy cover, or signal jamming
in conflict zones, reliance on satellite positioning is often
impossible. Consequently, UAVs must depend on onboard
sensing to navigate. While larger autonomous platforms utilize
heavy sensor suites such as LIDAR or high fidelity depth
cameras to map their environment, these solutions impose
severe weight and power penalties [5]. Small, UAVs essential
for maneuvering through tight, collapsed structures cannot
carry the heavy computational payload required to process
complex 3D point clouds in real-time [6]. This creates a critical
bottleneck, the need for a navigation system that is computa-
tionally efficient, lightweight, and capable of operating using
only monocular vision.
Historically, UAV navigation has relied on classical control
theory. [7]–[9] Proportional Integral Derivative (PID) con-
trollers are the industry standard for flight stability but lack the
cognitive ability to make high level path planning decisions in
unknown environments. To bridge this gap, methods such as
Artificial Potential Fields (APF) have been widely explored.
APF functions by assigning repulsive forces to obstacles and
attractive forces to targets, mathematically guiding the robot
through a safe path [10]. While effective in static simulations,

APF and similar geometric approaches often suffer from the
’local minima’ problem, where a drone gets trapped behind
an obstacle, unable to calculate a path around it without
human intervention [11]. Furthermore, rigid heuristics often
fail to account for the stochastic nature of dynamic obstacles,
necessitating a more adaptive approach [12].
Recognizing these limitations, we initially explored a rule-
based vision approach. By integrating YOLOv8n [13] for real-
time object detection, we developed a logic driven controller
where the UAV adjusted its yaw and pitch based on the cen-
troid offset of a detected human. While this proved effective
in controlled, empty environments, the ’if then’ nature of
the algorithm failed to generalize. In complex scenarios with
multiple obstacles or erratic target movement, the rigid rules
often led to oscillating flight paths or collisions, highlighting
the necessity for a system that could learn to adapt rather than
simply follow instructions.
This necessity has driven the shift toward Deep Reinforce-
ment Learning (RL). Unlike traditional control, which requires
explicit programming of flight dynamics, RL enables a UAV
to learn optimal navigation policies through trial and error.
By interacting with an environment and receiving probabilistic
rewards or penalties, an RL agent develops an intuitive under-
standing of flight dynamics and obstacle avoidance [14]. This
capability is particularly significant for rescue operations, as an
RL trained agent can generalize its training from simulation to
real world environments that it has never seen before a process
known as sim-to-real transfer exhibiting robust behavior even
when sensor data is noisy [15], [16].
In this work, we propose a vision based autonomous navi-
gation system designed for lightweight UAVs in GPS denied
environments. Unlike conventional approaches that rely on
fiducial markers (e.g., ArUco tags) for localization or navi-
gate to pre defined coordinates while simply avoiding static
obstacles, our framework leverages semantic visual feedback
to actively search for and approach dynamic human subjects.
Our focus is on a low-cost platform that detects human victims
and navigates toward them autonomously. We investigate the
transition from rule-based control to learning based control,
specifically comparing two prominent algorithms, Deep Q
Networks (DQN) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
[17]. We analyze the distinct behavioral characteristics of
these algorithms in continuous control tasks and validate the
superior approach on a DJI Tello drone. By utilizing a custom
Gymnasium environment and a shaped reward mechanism,
our system demonstrates the feasibility of deploying low-cost,
vision based autonomous agents in search and rescue missions.
The primary contributions of this research are fourfold.
First, we propose an integrated vision-based navigation
framework using YOLOv8n for real-time human de-
tection on resource-constrained hardware. Second, we
provide a comparative analysis between DQN and PPO
algorithms, establishing the superior stability of PPO for
continuous indoor navigation. Third, we develop a custom
Gymnasium-based simulation environment with a shaped
reward function to accelerate policy convergence. Finally,
we validate the system through sim-to-real transfer on a
DJI Tello drone, achieving autonomous human-following
behavior using RGB camera and off-board processing.
II. RELATED WORK
The problem of autonomous UAV navigation in complex
environments has been approached through various method-
ologies, ranging from classical control theory to modern
learning based systems. This section reviews existing literature
on rule-based navigation, real-time object detection, and the
growing application of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)
in aerial robotics.
A. Classical and Rule-Based Navigation Strategies
Early UAV autonomy relied on deterministic algorithms like
PID controllers for stability and Artificial Potential Fields
(APF) for path planning [5], [10]. While mathematically
simple, APF uses repulsive and attractive forces that often trap
drones in local minima, such as U-shaped obstacles, causing
them to stall [11].
Furthermore, these rule-based methods are brittle in unstruc-
tured, GPS-denied environments. They assume reliable state
estimation and struggle with the stochastic nature of shifting
debris or dynamic obstacles found in disaster zones
[18]. Because ”if-then” heuristics cannot account for such
high-level unpredictability, traditional approaches lack the
adaptability necessary for effective autonomous search and
rescue, necessitating more robust, non-deterministic solutions.
B. Real Time Object Detection for Lightweight UAVs
To navigate without external positioning, small rescue
drones require highly efficient visual perception. While
transformer-based models like Mamba offer high accuracy
[19], their computational weight hinders real-time inference
on edge devices like the Raspberry Pi or DJI Tello.
Consequently, CNNs, specifically the YOLO (You Only
Look Once) family, are the standard for resource-constrained
detection [1]. Unlike slow multi-stage detectors (e.g., Faster R-
CNN), YOLO’s single-stage architecture provides an optimal
trade-off between inference speed (FPS) and accuracy (mAP)
[20], [21]. This work utilizes YOLOv8n, a superior lightweight
model that enables the high-frequency control loops necessary
for responsive, high-speed autonomous navigation and human
tracking.
C. Deep Reinforcement Learning in UAVs
To overcome the rigidity of classical control and utilize
visual inputs effectively, researchers have turned to Deep
Reinforcement Learning (DRL). Unlike supervised learning,
which relies on labeled datasets, DRL enables an agent to learn
optimal behaviors through trial and error interactions with an
environment [18], [22].
Fundamentally, the UAV navigation problem is modeled
as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), defined by the tuple
(S, A, R, γ). At each timestep t, the UAV (agent) observes a
state St from the environment (e.g., camera feed), executes

an action At (e.g., flight command), and receives a reward
Rt indicating the quality of that action. The goal is to learn
a policy π that maximizes the expected cumulative reward
P γtRt over time. This paradigm allows the UAV to discover
complex navigation strategies that are difficult to manually
encode [23].
Within this framework, two primary families of algorithms
have been applied to UAVs:
1) Value-Based Methods (DQN): Early literature often uti-
lized Deep Q Networks (DQN). DQN approximates the ’Q
value’ the expected return of taking a specific action in a
specific state. While effective in discrete domains like video
games, DQN is inherently limited in robotic control. It requires
discretizing the action space (e.g., ’move left’ vs. ’move
right’), which often leads to jerky, ’bang bang’ flight patterns
that destabilize the UAV and waste energy [24], [25].
2) Policy Gradient Methods (PPO): To address the need for
smooth, continuous control, research has shifted toward Policy
Gradient methods. Specifically, Proximal Policy Optimization
(PPO) has become the standard for aerial robotics. PPO
optimizes the policy πθ directly, rather than estimating values,
allowing it to output continuous flight commands. While PPO
is capable of continuous control, in this study, we utilize it
for its robust policy optimization stability over DQN, even
within a discretized action space necessitated by the drone’s
SDK. Its ’clipped’ objective function prevents drastic policy
updates that could cause the drone to crash during training
[26]. As highlighted in recent surveys [14], PPO provides a
more consistent convergence profile for continuous navigation
tasks compared to value based counterparts.
D. Sim-to-Real Transfer Challenges
A major hurdle in DRL is the ’reality gap’ the discrepancy
between the simulation where the agent is trained and the
physical world where it operates. Simulation is indispensable
in this context, as training agents directly on physical hardware
would result in frequent collisions and costly mechanical
damage during the initial trial and error exploration phase.
However, transferring these policies remains challenging.
Recent studies emphasize the importance of domain random-
ization and robust reward shaping to facilitate this transfer. As
discussed in [15], agents trained solely on perfect simulation
data often fail when exposed to real world lighting variations
or aerodynamic disturbances. By employing a robust PPO
algorithm and simplifying the input state to relative bounding
box coordinates (as opposed to raw pixel data), our approach
aims to minimize this gap, leveraging the principles of domain
randomization to bridge the disparity between synthetic and
real world visual data [27], [28].
III. METHODOLOGY
This section details the development of the autonomous
navigation framework, progressing from a preliminary rule-
based visual servoing approach to a robust Deep Reinforce-
ment Learning (DRL) agent. The system architecture aims
to enable a lightweight UAV to locate and approach human
subjects in GPS denied environments using only monocular
vision.
A. Phase I: Rule-Based Visual Servoing
To establish a baseline for navigation performance, we
first implemented a classical visual servoing algorithm. This
method relies on geometric relationships between the detected
target and the UAV’s camera frame.
The perception module utilizes YOLOv8n to extract the
bounding box of the detected human, defined by coordinates
(x1, y1) and (x2, y2). The centroid of the bounding box
(Cx, Cy) is calculated as:

To overcome the rigid nature of rule-based control, we

3) State Space: The observation state St is a continuous
vector normalized to [−1, 1], representing the relative position
of the target and a proxy for distance:
St = [dx, dy, sizebbox]
(2)
where dx, dy are the horizontal and vertical deviations from
the frame center, and sizebbox is the normalized area of the
bounding box. As established in [27], using bounding box
parameters rather than raw pixel data significantly reduces the
’reality gap’ during sim-to-real transfer.
4) Action Space: To ensure compatibility with the discrete
command structure of the DJI Tello SDK, we defined a discrete
action space consisting of 5 atomic actions:
A = {Forward, Backward, Left, Right, Hover}
(3)
5) Reward Function Engineering: A critical challenge in
RL is designing a reward signal that ensures effective con-
vergence. Purely sparse rewards (e.g., +1 only for reaching
the target) often lead to the ’temporal credit assignment’
problem, where the agent fails to associate early actions with
the eventual outcome due to a lack of immediate feedback
[14]. Conversely, relying solely on shaped rewards (continuous
feedback) can sometimes lead to local optima where the
agent exploits the shaping function without achieving the main
objective.
To address this, a hybrid reward structure that combines the
goal oriented nature of sparse rewards with the dense guidance
of reward shaping was used.
6) Reward Function : The total reward Rtotal is calculated
as:
Rtotal = Rstep + Rprogress + Ralign + Rterm
(4)
The reward function is balanced as follows:
• Rstep (-0.2): Serves as a time-pressure mechanism to
encourage efficient trajectories.
• Rprogress (15 × ∆D): Provides a dense gradient based
on the change in sizebbox to accelerate depth navigation.
• Ralign (+30): Rewards visual centering (|dx, dy| < 0.6)
to ensure target stability.
• Rterm (+50): A large sparse reward granted only when
reaching the target (sizebbox > 0.8).
• Guidance (Shaped) Components: To accelerate learning,
we provide continuous feedback through Rprogress (re-
warding reduction in distance) and Ralign (rewarding
visual centering). This creates a gradient for the agent to
follow, preventing aimless exploration in the early stages
of training.
• Goal (Sparse) Components: To ensure the agent priori-
tizes the actual objective, we implement a large Terminal
Reward (Rterm
= +50) that is only granted upon
successfully reaching the target distance (< 1.0m). Addi-
tionally, a Step Penalty (Rstep = −0.2) serves as a ’time
pressure’ mechanism to encourage efficient trajectories.
This hybrid approach effectively mitigates the sparsity prob-
lem while ensuring the optimal policy aligns with the true
objective of the rescue mission.
C. Algorithm Selection: PPO vs. DQN
We compared Deep Q Networks (DQN) and Proximal
Policy Optimization (PPO). While DQN is effective for dis-
crete spaces, its value based updates often resulted in jerky,
oscillating flight paths in our tests (30% success rate). PPO, a
policy gradient method, optimizes the policy directly using a
clipped objective function [26]:

IV. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
A. Comparative Performance Analysis
To ensure statistical rigor, all models were evaluated over
500 episodes in a 5×5 grid environment with a 30-step limit.
Statistical significance was verified using 95% confidence
intervals (CI95).
Table II summarizes the results. The Rule-Based approach,
while computationally lightweight, achieved a moderate suc-
cess rate of 47.6% ± 4.4%. It performed well in static scenarios
but failed significantly when the target moved laterally, often
losing the target due to the rigid ’stop and turn’ logic.
DQN showed limited capability for this continuous control
task. The discretization of the action space resulted in ’bang
bang’ control behavior, where the drone would oscillate vio-
lently between left and right commands to maintain the center.
This instability led to a low success rate of 40.4% ± 4.3% and
frequent crashes.
PPO emerged as the superior method, achieving a suc-
cess rate of 61.2% ± 4.3%. The policy gradient approach
allowed for smoother, continuous adjustments to the drone’s
trajectory. Unlike the rule-based agent, the PPO agent learned
to anticipate target movement, adjusting its heading while
simultaneously moving forward, resulting in more efficient
paths. These findings align with similar comparative studies
in aerial robotics [29].

We validated the trained PPO agent on a physical DJI
Tello drone. Figure 4 illustrates the system in operation. The

C. Latency and Computational Constraints
A significant challenge in this implementation is the pro-
cessing architecture. As the DJI Tello lacks sufficient onboard
compute power for deep learning, we adopted an off board
processing model. The UAV generates a dedicated IEEE
802.11n Wi Fi Access Point, to which the ground station
laptop (Apple MacBook M1) connects directly. Video frames
and control commands are transmitted via UDP packets over
this local network link.
We observed an average round trip latency of approximately
150ms-200ms. While acceptable for walking speed human
targets, this latency could be critical in high-speed rescue
scenarios. The breakdown of this latency is as follows:
• Video Transmission: 60-80ms
• YOLOv8 Inference: 15-20ms
• PPO Policy Inference: < 5ms
• Command Transmission: 30-50ms
Future iterations must prioritize Edge Computing solutions
(e.g., NVIDIA Jetson) to eliminate this transmission latency.
V. CONCLUSION
This study demonstrates a cost-effective, autonomous nav-
igation framework for lightweight UAVs operating in GPS-
denied indoor environments. By integrating YOLOv8n for
real-time human detection with a Proximal Policy Optimiza-
tion (PPO) agent, the framework addresses the rigidity of rule-
based logic and the instability of discrete Deep Q-Networks
(DQN). The PPO-based agent achieved a success rate of
61.2% ± 4.3% over 500 evaluation episodes in simulation,
outperforming both rule-based and value-based baselines with
