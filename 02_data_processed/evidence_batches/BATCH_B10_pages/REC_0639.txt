 A Vision-Based Approach to Autonomous Landing 
of an eVTOL Aircraft in GPS-Denied Environments

Abstract—This research paper describes an autonomous 
system developed by a team of undergraduate engineering 
students at Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University – Prescott 
with support from Wisk Aero. Autonomous systems enable an 
electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) aircraft to 
perform the fundamental duties of a pilot – aviate, navigate, and 
communicate - without human intervention. A prototype 
eVTOL system, implemented as a modular payload attached to 
the bottom of a commercially available drone, was developed 
and tested to operate from the final approach through the 
landing phase of flight. The prototype vision-based system 
identifies a landing zone, and calculates relative position and 
orientation position without reliance on Global Positioning 
System (GPS) capabilities, which is potentially vulnerable to 
interruption and disruption. In addition, the system utilizes a 
machine learning approach to identify if any obstructions 
impact the safety of landing the aircraft. Through the 
development of the prototype, the findings demonstrated the 
viability of a computer vision-based landing system to be used 
on an eVTOL aircraft for the purpose of autonomous advanced 
air mobility (AAM) solutions.  
Keywords—Autonomous Landing, eVTOL, Advanced Air 
Mobility, Computer Vision, Machine Learning, Relative Position, 
GPS-Denied, Obstructions, Orientation  
I. INTRODUCTION 
The rapid advancement of autonomous systems has paved 
the way for autonomous electric vertical takeoff and landing 
(eVTOL) aircraft, with the ability to provide efficient and 
flexible transportation solutions. Wisk Aero, a company in the 
field of advanced air mobility (AAM), is actively developing 
transportation solutions to address the growing need for 
efficient and sustainable urban air transportation with the 
ultimate goal of introducing air taxis into the urban 
transportation ecosystem. Recent developments in eVTOLs 
have shown that the future of AAM solutions will involve 
current cutting-edge technologies such as autonomous flight 
and electric or hybrid propulsion systems. The system 
described in this paper aims to replicate the fundamental 
duties of a pilot through computer vision and machine learning 
techniques. 
The system consisted of a commercially available 
quadcopter drone with a sensor package onboard, containing 
the necessary components to simulate the responsibilities of a 
pilot during the landing phase of flight. An optical navigation 
system was explored, similar to [1], for autonomous landing. 
For testing, the drone was piloted remotely and transmitted 
live video from two onboard optical sensors to an external 
display. As has been previously researched [2], the use of 
optical sensors allows for the detection of reliable delineators, 
as well as a simple physical configuration of cameras to 
efficiently transmit optical data. 
The system was capable of performing four primary tasks, 
which are crucial for ensuring a safe and efficient landing. 
First, the landing zone was detected with the use of fiducial 
markers which were recognized by a computer vision 
application. Second, machine learning techniques were 
employed to detect any potential obstructions that could 
impact the safety of the landing. Third, the system calculated 
the orientation of the drone in relation to the landing zone, and 
determined its position in relation to the landing pad, similar 
to [2]. Due to the possibility of interruption and disruption of 
GPS, it was not used by the system to determine its position, 
however GPS was used for verification of the position 
determined by the system. Fourth, the system generated log 
files that captured data related to the landing process, 
providing a valuable resource for analysis, monitoring, and 
future improvements. 
Through the development and testing of the prototype 
system, this research validates the viability of a vision-based 
landing system for eVTOL aircraft. This vision-based 
approach enhances the system's autonomy and reduces the 
reliance on external positioning systems that may be subject 
to failures. The successful implementations of autonomous 
systems opens up new possibilities for AAM solutions, where 
eVTOL aircraft can operate safely and autonomously, 
contributing to the future of urban air mobility (UAM) and 
beyond. 
II. ANALYSIS 
The vision-based system described by this paper performs 
the basic duties of a pilot by using a computer vision 
application that interacts with a predefined environment used 
to lay the foundation for an autonomous system. The system 
performs four tasks, as previously mentioned. This section 
provides an overview of the system’s architecture and the 
design decisions of the system. It covers the use of fiducial 
markers used to detect the landing zone, the architecture and 
design decisions of the chosen machine learning model, the 
trade-offs and design of the relative position algorithms, 
Perspective-N-Point (PnP), and lastly, the physical system 
design.

A. Fiducials 
To identify the landing zone and obtain a camera pose 
estimation during the landing phase of a UAV, binary 
square fiducial markers are utilized. The type of fiducial 
markers experimented with are ArUco markers (Fig. 1), 
identified and marked with a computer vision application 
[3]. An ArUco marker consists of a black border and inner 
binary matrix, enabling easy identification and detection 
even from significant distances. Similar research for using 
fiducial markers for optical localization during landing of 
eVTOLs [4] suggest that ArUco markers can be used in 
simulated and realistic landing scenarios. When either of the 
two optical sensors from the system obtained a line of sight 
of the ArUco markers, each individual marker was detected 
and highlighted. 
 
Fig. 1. ArUco Marker 
 
For the purposes of testing in a scaled landing site 
environment (Fig. 2), four fiducial markers were positioned 
on a flat surface. When four ArUco markers were detected, 
an outline was placed around the landing zone, which is 
labeled as LZ. For robustness, additional test cases allowed 
for the landing zone to be detected if two ArUco markers 
opposite of each other were detected. Since there is more 
than one ArUco marker on the field, each corner of the 
landing zone can be known through each ArUco marker’s 
unique identifier. As the system intakes and processes video 
frames, and detects if there is a landing zone present, it can 
use the ArUco markers to determine its position and 
orientation relative to the landing zone. Details regarding 
the system’s use of Marker Pose Estimation is described in 
Section B, and details regarding the limitations of the 
landing zone detection can be found in Section D of Results. 
 
 
Fig. 2. Landing Zone Detection 
 
 
B. Relative Position and Orientation 
To adhere to the mission requirement of being operable in 
GPS-denied environments, the system implemented a vision-
based solution for determining the position and orientation of 
the drone relative to the landing pad. The system contains two 
cameras, one facing downward at a 90° angle and one facing 
forward at a 45° angle. This design decision was made as the 
initial descent for the drone is to approach the landing pad at 
a 45° decline until directly above the pad, and then to descend 
vertically onto the landing zone.  
Research suggests [5] the speed and accuracy of the PnP 
algorithm is the optimal choice for calculating the relative 
position of the aircraft for this experiment. PnP works by 
taking 2D coordinates in the image plane, and their 
corresponding 3D world coordinates to estimate the pose of 
the object in a 3D frame centered on the camera. By mapping 
the real-world coordinates of the landing pad to the pixel 
corners of the square fiducials in the image, PnP can then be 
used to calculate the position of the camera. By default, the 
relative position and orientation are of the object in a 
coordinate frame centered on the camera which the system 
converts to the world coordinate and orientation of the camera 
in the world frame. To ensure accuracy of PnP, the algorithm 
requires the intrinsic matrix and distortion coefficients of the 
camera. These parameters are calculated by calibrating the 
camera using a checkerboard image. The accuracy of the 
algorithms is shown in Section D of Results. 
C. Machine Learning 
In a nominal scenario, existing autonomous frameworks 
such as flight management systems (FMS) or autopilot are 
highly dependable solutions to achieve autonomous flight. In 
the event of a non-nominal scenario, such as when 
obstructions lie within a flight path or obstruct the touchdown 
and liftoff (TLOF) zone, conventional means of autonomous 
flight are no longer viable and require new technologies such 
as machine learning to fulfill these tasks. 
This system deployed a YOLOv3 (You Only Look Once) 
model for detecting obstructions denoted by model cars within 
the proximity of a known landing zone. YOLOv3 is part of the 
YOLO family of machine learning models, including features 
from its predecessor, YOLOv2, and residual networks [6]. 
This particular model is well-suited for performing real-time 
detection tasks with a reduced loss of accuracy [7]. The 
YOLOv3 network consists of 53 convolutional layers, 
intermixed with several residual network layers [6]. Residual 
layers are the key to YOLOv3’s improved performance, as 
they allow the neural network to exploit deep learning 
technologies as shown in [8]. A custom model was deployed 
based on a single class: a model car. Using a single class 
eliminates the risk of an incorrect classification. Another key 
advantage of YOLOv3 is that it is able to make multiple 
detections in a single frame [7]. To make a classification, a set 
of bounding boxes is generated. Each bounding box in the set 
is compared against the ground truth. Whichever bounding 
box has the greatest overlap against the ground truth is the 
bounding box used to identify the classification in the video 
frame. Upon classifying any objects detected in a frame, a 
bounding box is drawn around the object, printing the class 
and the associated confidence (Fig. 7).

D. Physical System Attributes 
A physical system was assembled to facilitate the project. 
A DJI Matrice 100 quadcopter was used as the test bed and 
other electronic and 3D printed components were attached 
(Fig. 3). The DJI Matrice 100 was chosen because it did not 
contain any sensors besides those required for basic flight 
functionality. There were no cameras attached, and there were 
no object avoidance sensors that could potentially interrupt 
flight tests; it served the purpose of an empty platform. 
The 3D printed components were designed specifically for 
the DJI Matrice and consisted of three main parts: a frame 
holding the majority of the electronics that mounted 
underneath the drone (Fig. 4), a camera mount that allowed 
for two of the selected cameras to be to be attached to the 
drone at selectable angles in increments of 15 degrees, and a 
piece that attached to the top of the drone that held the system's 
battery and the GPS receiver antenna for the control data. 
 
Fig. 3. System side view. 
 
Fig. 4. System frame with NVIDIA Jetson Nano and additional 
components. 
 
The electronics consisted of a system battery, a power 
supply, a Jetson Nano developer kit, two OAK-D S2 cameras, 
an HDMI to analog video converter, and a 5.8 gigahertz video 
transmitter. The Jetson Nano ran the software developed to 
identify the landing zone, compute relative position and 
orientation, and perform object detection. Meanwhile, the 
HDMI to analog video converter took HDMI video from the 
Jetson Nano and sent it to the video transmitter which was then 
received by a video receiver on the ground, providing real time 
feedback and analysis of the system during testing. A diagram 
showing the entire system and the connections between 
components can be seen in Fig. 5. 
 
Fig. 5. Diagram illustrating connections between components.   
 
III. RESULTS 
The results section presents the findings obtained from the 
development and testing of the vision-based landing system 
for eVTOL aircraft.  
A. Environment Setup 
The 
experimental 
design 
assumed 
constant 
and 
unchanging weather conditions throughout the experiments. 
By eliminating the variable of weather, the focus was aimed 
to concentrate solely on the identification of the landing pad 
and any objects on it. 
Three arrangements of 0.61m by 0.61m (2' x 2') ArUco 
markers were tested to find the optimal placement of 
identifiable fiducials to be detected by the system. Initially, a 
single central ArUco marker was tested, aiming to simplify the 
identification process. However, this approach proved to be 
unreliable due to the limited visibility from different angles. 
An alternative configuration was then explored: two markers 
positioned on opposite corners of the landing pad. While this 
arrangement exhibited improved detectability, the use of more 
markers on the landing pad provided more consistent 
detection. An arrangement comprising four ArUco markers, 
each situated in one corner of the landing pad proved to be the 
most reliable. Notably, this configuration only requires the 
recognition of two markers, provided they are positioned 
diagonally opposite each other. 
The landing area utilized in the experiments consisted of a 
1.83m by 1.83m (6' x 6') square, providing a well-defined 
space for the drone to land. To establish the landing area, nine 
0.61m by 0.61m (2' x 2') square wooden boards were securely 
fixed together, preventing any movement of the landing area 
during the experiments. This ensured a stable and consistent 
environment for landing assessments. Furthermore, to 
maintain a standardized orientation for the landing area, the 
top of the square was consistently pointed towards true north. 
This orientation choice allowed the system to be evaluated for 
influence of landing direction on the performance of the UAV 
as it approached the landing area from different angles.

To simulate realistic obstacles encountered during landing 
operations, the researchers employed model cars ranging from 
1:64 scale to 1:16 scale. These miniature vehicles were 
strategically placed on the landing pad, enabling the 
assessment of the UAV's ability to detect and highlight 
obstacles during the landing process.
In specific testing of differential GPS calculations, a GPS 
module was positioned at the center of the landing pad. This 
GPS module was connected to a Jetson Nano and an external 
power supply to operate independently, logging its data 
throughout the testing phase, facilitating a comprehensive 
comparison between its recorded results and the GPS data 
acquired from the UAV. The primary objective was to discern 
any disparities and establish an exact measurement of the 
UAV's position with precision and accuracy.
B. Training
In order to successfully run a machine learning model, 
training a dataset needed to occur based on system 
requirements and specifically
chosen applied metrics. 
Preliminary video footage of the designated landing area was 
captured and used to create a custom dataset. The created 
dataset included images of the landing zone with model cars
placed around randomly within the landing pad. The dataset 
was expanded throughout development which led to improved 
confidence levels achieved by the model.
Datasets require images that are annotated with bounding 
boxes or polygonal segmentation, the former of which was 
used for training this model. Bounding boxes are represented 
by either two coordinates (X1,Y1) and (X2, Y2), or by one co-
coordinate (X1,Y1), and width (w) and height (h) of the 
bounding box [9]. The dataset was annotated using the YOLO 
format with the structure as follows, Class_ID X_Center 
Y_Center Width Height [10]. The unique numerical identifier, 
Class_ID, was used to denote which classification is made 
from the list of classes, in this case, a model car. X_Center and 
Y_Center are the normalized coordinates of bounding box 
annotation, respectively.
Once all images intended for the dataset have been 
annotated, the set is ready for training. Training a dataset 
consists of splitting the dataset into three categories used in 
neural network training. These categories consist of the 
training set, validation set, and testing set. The dataset used 
here consisted of 69%, 19%, and 12%, respectively. 
Experimentally, increasing the size of the testing set resulted 
in a more accurate model. For the configuration of the training 
process, the batch size used for training was set to 64, with 
subdivisions of size 16. The training process consisted of 6000 
iterations, as there was only one class. The learning rate used 
for training was 0.001. The network width and height were 
416 pixels each. A trade study was conducted regarding the 
number of hours a training device would take based on the 
cores of the GPU, this can be seen in Table I below.

There were three training metrics examined to help 
determine what constitutes a sufficiently trained model. The 
intersection over union (Fig. 6) is the intersection of the 
ground truth and the prediction divided by union of the ground 
truth and prediction. The closer to 1 the IOU is, the better the 
model confidence and level of training. Throughout the 
training process, the loss of training data decreases, eventually 
getting close to 0. This model achieved an average loss of 
0.2666 over the course of training for 6000 iterations. The 
final metric is called mean average precision (mAP); a higher 
mAP value yields a more accurate model. Mathematically, the 
mAP depends on the number of true positives, compared with 
true and false positives.

C. Evaluation
The object detection algorithm, trained to detect model 
cars of various sizes, was deployed on an NVIDIA Jetson 
Nano. To improve the accuracy of detections, the prediction 
confidence was set to a high probability of correctness of 90%. 
Having a higher prediction confidence reduces the probability 
of a false positive but could also result in more false negatives. 
The minimum threshold for comparing a calculated bounding 
box to the ground truth classification was set to 30%. The

E. Control Data 
Differential GPS was used as a ‘source of truth’ in which 
to verify the accuracy of the PnP algorithm. To acquire precise 
GPS control data, two GPS receivers were used; one in the 
center of the landing pad and one on the drone. Both GPS 
receivers used Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), enabling 
synchronization of the two GPS readings. The difference in 
position was taken from the two GPS receivers to compute and 
convert to relative position data. This relative position data 
was compared to the output calculated from the PnP algorithm 
for validation. 
Fig. 8 shows the relative position estimates from the PnP 
algorithm compared to the aforementioned ‘source of truth’ 
created using the difference between the GPS receivers. The 
correlation in the data presented indicates that the PnP 
algorithm is a dependable source for determining relative 
position of an eVTOL where GPS may not be reliable. This 
correlation remained consistent during the landing phase of 
flight when line of sight of the landing pad was shifted from 
the 45° camera to the 90° camera. 
 
Fig. 8. Control Data and Relative Position Estimate vs. Time. 
IV. CONCLUSIONS 
The research presented included a concept for a vision-
based landing system, capable of performing the basic 
functions of a pilot without the need for human interaction. 
The future of autonomous flight cannot fully depend on 
existing means of autonomous flight such as flight 
management or autopilot systems when operating in low-
altitude airspaces due to the possibility of encountering 
obstructions or operating in GPS-denied environments. The 
tasks performed by this system were to identify a landing zone 
using square fiducial markers, calculate the position of the 
aircraft relative using a computer vision approach assuming 
the landing zone is visible, calculate its orientation, and detect 
obstructions using a machine learning approach. 
Future research on the project overall would include 
adding robustness to the existing system, such as the ability to 
operate in weather. Other considerations would be 
implementing a more robust machine learning model, as the 
existing model does not track false positive or false negative 
detections nor perform error detection and correction. The 
final consideration would be to use the logged data generated 
by the system as input to a feedback control loop to achieve 
autonomous flight.
