in GNSS-Denied Environments: A Review

the

development
of
interceptor
unmanned
aerial
vehicles
(UAVs),
they
have
become
core

for
counter-UAV
missions.
Interceptor
UAVs exhibit high dynamic performance and high-speed
maneuverability, yet maintaining stable operation in global

remains
a
major
challenge.
Conventional
simultaneous
localization and mapping (SLAM) systems are extremely
susceptible to motion distortion, which can lead to system

interceptor

Traditional
SLAM
and
learning-based
SLAM methods are categorized and summarized according
to sensor types and the adoption of learning techniques,

model interpretability. Furthermore, it outlines promising future research directions for interceptor UAV applications,
such as learning-based cooperative SLAM and distributed swarm intelligence-based SLAM. This review aims to provide
comprehensive research References for researchers and engineers and facilitate the deployment and implementation of
full-scene, high-precision and high-reliability SLAM technologies on new-generation interceptor UAVs.

I. INTRODUCTION
I
N RECENT years, the widespread adoption of unmanned
aerial vehicles (UAVs) has signiﬁcantly driven the devel-
opment of counter-UAV technologies. Interceptor UAVs,
characterized by high dynamic performance and robust anti-
jamming capabilities [1], are capable of tracking targets
continuously and stably [2], as well as rapidly switching
between high- and low-altitude ﬂight modes [3]. These
attributes render them a critical asset in modern security
operations.

Global navigation satellite systems (GNSSs) serve as the
conventional localization paradigm for UAVs; however, their
reliability is severely compromised in extreme environments.
In scenarios, such as urban canyons, enclosed indoor spaces,
and intense electronic warfare theaters, the GNSS signals are
frequently unavailable or degraded. For interceptor UAVs,
the loss of reliable GNSS signals may severely compromise
mission performance and success. Consequently, relying on
onboard sensors to achieve simultaneous localization and
mapping (SLAM) has emerged as a major focal point of
current research.
Interceptor UAVs deliver extreme dynamic performance.
While bare-bones interceptor quadrotor platforms can achieve
substantially higher velocity and acceleration margins in open-
loop ﬂight demonstrations, interceptor UAVs equipped with
onboard multisensor SLAM systems for autonomous nav-
igation operate within a bounded dynamic envelope. For
SLAM-enabled autonomous interceptor UAVs, their maximum
ﬂight speed is approximately 20 m/s [4], the maximum accel-
eration is around 5 m/s2 [4], and the peak attitude angular rate
can reach 230◦/s [5], and the peak yaw angular rate can reach
115◦/s [6]. The attitude control update frequency is 50 Hz
[7], with a payload constraint that the total payload weight

The distinct ﬂight characteristics of interceptor UAVs pose
signiﬁcant technical challenges for SLAM frameworks.
The theoretical foundations of traditional SLAM were
established by a series of landmark studies. Smith et al.
[9] ﬁrst formalized SLAM within a probabilistic framework.
Subsequently, Montemerlo et al. [10] achieved the decoupling
of pose estimation and map estimation, an approach further
optimized by the FastSLAM algorithm [11]. Concurrently,
Lu and Milios [12] introduced the pose graph concept and
pioneered the ﬁeld of graph optimization, which was later
matured by Grisetti et al. [13] and further advanced by
Klein and Murray [14]. To facilitate practical applications,
K¨ummerle et al. [15] provided general-purpose foundational
optimization tools, while Carlone et al. [16] implemented
eﬃcient incremental inference based on this technology.
Leveraging these optimization paradigms, Mur-Artal et al. [17]
proposed a prominent three-thread architecture that integrates
front-end feature extraction, back-end optimization, and loop
closure detection.
Through these iterations, traditional SLAM has gradually
evolved from single-sensor modalities to multisensor fusion
schemes. Despite these advancements, traditional SLAM
frameworks still exhibit inherent limitations when deployed
on interceptor UAVs: they lack resilience in texture-deﬁcient
environments and under severe illumination variations; feature
matching frequently fails under high-speed motion blur; and
they struggle to suppress scale drift. Furthermore, traditional
approaches often lack semantic environmental awareness
and exhibit weak resistance to dynamic disturbances, which
severely constrains their deployment in high-dynamic inter-
ception scenarios.
To mitigate these limitations, the learning-based SLAM
has been introduced in the existing literature. Lai et al.
[18] reviewed the application of deep learning in dynamic
visual SLAM, while K´aroly et al. [19] summarized the deep
network architectures and training strategies that have driven
SLAM advancements in robotics. Similarly, Mokssit et al. [20]
systematically reviewed learning-based SLAM methods across
various neural network typologies. Furthermore, Chen et al.
[21] detailed the deep integration of frameworks, such as con-
volutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural networks
(RNNs), and transformers into standard SLAM architectures.
While these comprehensive reviews highlight how learning-
based SLAM improves environmental adaptability, its appli-
cation to interceptor UAVs remains subject to unique practical
constraints. Speciﬁcally, the learning-based SLAM suﬀers
from degraded recognition accuracy in multiscale environ-
ments due to the paucity of specialized training datasets
tailored for extreme scenarios. Concurrently, the inherently
uninterpretable “black-box” nature of deep neural networks
poses substantial challenges for ensuring safety and reliability
in real-world missions.
Despite the growing body of SLAM review literature, there
remains a critical lack of systematic investigations tailored
to the stringent operational requirements of interceptor UAVs
in GNSS-denied environments. The existing representative

A. Traditional Visual SLAM
1) RGB-Based Visual SLAM: Within the paradigm of visual-
inertial state estimation, the RGB camera-based SLAM has
evolved from early sparse feature-based formulations to
modern dense, multiprimitive, and multisensor fusion frame-
works. Forster et al. [27], [28] pioneered SVO, a semi-direct
visual odometry that bridges the gap between direct and
feature-based methods. This framework achieves high-speed,
pixel-level matching via photometric error minimization while
concurrently reﬁning camera poses through backend opti-
mization coupled with a recursive Bayesian depth ﬁlter. For
RGB-D conﬁgurations, Kerl et al. [43] introduced a dense
SLAM framework minimizing both photometric and geometric
errors, utilizing an entropy-based keyframe selection strategy
to bound tracking drift.
The ORB-SLAM series remains a benchmark for versa-
tility and precision in geometric SLAM. Mur-Artal et al.
[17] established the foundations of monocular ORB-SLAM
by employing uniﬁed ORB features across all threads and
managing local mapping via a co-visibility graph. This
paradigm was subsequently extended to ORB-SLAM2 [34],
which supports stereo and RGB-D conﬁgurations and resolves
scale ambiguity through local bundle adjustment. Building
upon this architecture, Haddadi and Castelan [44] integrated
ORB-SLAM with inertial data through an extended Kalman
ﬁlter (EKF) to enable autonomous quadrotor navigation in
GNSS-denied environments. To enhance computational eﬃ-
ciency, Fu et al. [29] proposed fast ORB-SLAM, boosting
tracking speed via a descriptor-free matching strategy and a
coarse-to-ﬁne motion model.
To mitigate performance degradation in texture-deﬁcient or
unstructured environments, higher level geometric primitives
have been progressively incorporated. Fu et al. [30] and
Gomez-Ojeda et al. [31] (PL-SLAM) developed point-line
feature fusion methodologies, minimizing joint reprojection
errors to improve localization stability in structurally regular
yet texture-sparse scenes. Similarly, Yu et al. [45] enhanced
environmental resilience through adaptive FAST corner detec-
tion and a hybrid reprojection error optimization model.
Concurrently, notable advancements have emerged in global
consistency and dense mapping. Han et al. [32] (GC-SLAM)
decoupled the nonlinear cost functions in global registration
using second-order statistics, achieving high-speed consistency
for massive point clouds. Sch¨ops et al. [33] (BAD-SLAM)
utilized a direct BA strategy to alternately optimize cam-
era poses and Surfel-based models, outperforming traditional
pose-graph optimization in accuracy. Extending visual-inertial
state estimation to urban scales, Li et al. [46] developed
GeoVINS, which eliminates cumulative drift by fusing satellite
orthophotos with visual-inertial data through a tightly coupled
EKF framework, ensuring drift-free estimation in large-scale
aerial navigation.

3) EVSLAM: Event cameras, characterized by asynchronous
pixel-level intensity change detection, oﬀer ultrahigh temporal

well-suited for aggressive UAV maneuvers where traditional
frame-based sensors typically experience signiﬁcant perfor-
mance degradation. Recent surveys by Shahraki et al. [52]
and Zhang et al. [53] systematically categorize the land-
scape of event-based visual SLAM (EVSLAM). These reviews
analyze preprocessing paradigms—ranging from geometric
feature extraction to deep learning representations—and fusion
strategies incorporating IMU, LiDAR, and RGB-D sensors,
identifying frameworks, such as ESVIO and ESVO2 as the
current state-of-the-art for reliable, real-time state estimation.

event tracking with IMU measurements. By utilizing an
expectation–maximization-based feature tracker and an EKF

metric 6-DOF pose estimation without explicit intensity image
reconstruction. Capitalizing on the advantages of binocular
conﬁgurations, Zhou et al. [54] introduced the ﬁrst stereo
event visual odometry, employing a parallel tracking and
mapping architecture that optimizes spatiotemporal consis-
tency for semi-dense 3-D mapping. This paradigm was further
advanced by Chen et al. [41] (ESVIO), which integrates stereo
event corners with IMU data within a sliding-window graph
optimization, using RANSAC-denoised inverse depth features
for joint spatiotemporal constraint satisfaction. More recently,
Niu et al. [42] developed ESVO2, which enhances mapping
eﬃciency ﬁvefold through adaptive event accumulation and
zero-mean normalized cross correlation block matching, eﬀec-
tively suppressing inertial drift via a compact optimization
framework.
A signiﬁcant shift toward continuous-time estimation and
mapless architectures has emerged to better align with the
diﬀerential nature of event sensors. Wang and Gammell [55]
proposed a continuous-time stereo odometry using Gaussian
process regression, preserving native temporal resolution by
estimating trajectories without temporal grouping or bin-
ning artifacts. Similarly, Lu et al. [56] designed a map-free
visual-inertial velometer that couples event normal ﬂow with
IMU preintegration, facilitating high-speed navigation without
the computational overhead of local map maintenance. This
approach was reﬁned by Lu et al. [57], focusing exclusively
on instantaneous linear velocity and IMU bias optimization.
By adopting continuous-time parameterization and stereo tem-
poral surface matching, the system enables low-drift dead
reckoning during extreme UAV maneuvers, providing a real-
time, pure event state estimator capable of operating across
unconstrained environments and motion proﬁles.

B. Learning-Based Visual SLAM
1) Learning-Based Feature Extraction:
Recent advance-
ments have seen learning-based methods increasingly inte-
grated into the front-end feature extraction and matching
modules of visual SLAM to overcome the inherent perfor-
mance caps of hand-crafted descriptors. Cao [68] enhanced
the ORB-SLAM2 [34] framework by embedding HFNet,
a MobileNet-based multitask network, for concurrent key-
point detection and descriptor extraction. When combined
with the trained binary descriptor, this system signiﬁcantly
improves tracking robustness and adaptability under chal-
lenging illumination variations. Transitioning from CNNs to
attention-based models, Fu and Yao [58] integrated vision
transformer (ViT) feature representations into the SLAM front
end. This approach leverages self-attention mechanisms and
image patch embeddings to capture global semantic contexts
and long-range spatial dependencies, thereby enriching the
environmental perception of the tracking front end.
Beyond sparse feature replacement, recent research has
gravitated toward dense mapping and generalizable spatial
representations. Yin et al. [59] (IBD-SLAM) utilized neural

2) Learning-Based Geometric Reconstruction: Integrating
deep learning into depth estimation and geometric reconstruc-
tion addresses the inherent scale ambiguity and sparsity of
traditional monocular SLAM. Tateno et al. [61] pioneered
CNN-SLAM, a real-time dense monocular system that embeds
a ResNet-based fully convolutional network into the SLAM
front end. By inferring dense depth maps from single-frame
RGB inputs, this system enables metric-scale reconstruction
and provides pixel-level semantic labels, eﬀectively bridging
the gap between geometric mapping and semantic understand-
ing.
Further reﬁning this integration, Loo et al. [62] developed
CNN-SVO to enhance semi-direct visual odometry. Unlike
systems that rely solely on CNN outputs, CNN-SVO utilizes
a ResNet50-based depth prediction network to initialize the
depth mean with reduced initial variance within the mapping
thread. This strategy signiﬁcantly diminishes depth uncertainty
for map points and narrows the search range for epipolar
feature matching, thereby accelerating the convergence of
the probabilistic depth ﬁlter and improving overall tracking
stability.
3) Learning-Based Extreme Environment Imaging: Deep
learning signiﬁcantly extends the operational envelope of
visual SLAM by enhancing scene understanding, particularly
in dynamic or visually degraded environments. Liu [63]
improved the ORB-SLAM2 framework for dynamic scenes
by integrating YOLOv8s-seg. This lightweight instance seg-
mentation model generates pixel-level masks to identify and
eliminate feature points on dynamic objects, while the adop-
tion of the BEBLID binary descriptor further optimizes feature
matching accuracy and computational eﬃciency.
Beyond object-level masking, deep learning facilitates more
complex spatial reasoning. Koilada [70] reviewed the integra-
tion of CNNs and GNNs in visual localization, highlighting
the shift toward hierarchical positioning. Notable examples
include HF-Net, which utilizes MobileNetV2 and NetVLAD
for global retrieval, and Superglue, which leverages GNNs
and attention mechanisms to model high-level keypoint asso-
ciations, ensuring robust data association in challenging
topographies.
To address extreme imaging conditions, recent research has
pivoted toward multimodal enhancement. Zhang et al. [64]
proposed MCDFus, a hybrid Mamba-CNN architecture for
visible-infrared image fusion. By applying this deep learning-
based fusion module at the SLAM front end, the system
provides a robust, enhanced multimodal input that improves
the reliability of feature extraction, target tracking, and local
mapping under adverse conditions where single-sensor inputs
may fail.

The
end-to-end
learning

comprehensive survey by Xiao et al. [71], these methods
have evolved from basic feature mappings to sophisticated
network architectures that integrate CNNs, transformers, and
multiagent reinforcement learning.
In the domain of visual odometry, contemporary research
focuses on replacing the conventional tracking and pose-
estimation pipeline with deep regression models. Krishnan
and Sahin [72] (ORBDeepOdometry) introduced a hybrid
framework that extracts ORB corners prior to utilizing a
FlowNetSimple-based CNN for dimensionality reduction and
a stacked long short-term memory (LSTM) for temporal
modeling, thereby directly regressing camera translation and
rotation. Similarly, Yan et al. [73] enhanced this process

structural features, feeding the processed frames into an RCNN
to achieve end-to-end pose estimation.
Another prominent trend integrates perception and con-

simulated environments. Li et al. [65] developed VisFly, a
high-eﬃciency simulator that enables quadrotors to map visual
observations directly to ﬂight commands, reducing reliance on
explicit map construction for tasks, such as cooperative gap
traversal. Building upon this, Sun et al. [66] applied curriculum
reinforcement learning within the VisFly platform, employing
a lightweight CNN–GRU–actor–critic architecture to achieve
zero-shot sim-to-real transfer in obstacle-rich racing scenarios.
To reﬁne these policies, diﬀerentiable physics engines and
simulators have been introduced to enable gradient backprop-
agation through dynamic models. Zhang et al. [74] leveraged
diﬀerentiable particle physics to optimize ﬂight strategies,
while Zhang et al. [75] utilized a diﬀerentiable UAV dynamic
model embedded with a stop-gradient operator to ensure
stable multigap traversal. This direction is further extended by
Su et al. [76] (DiﬀRacing), which incorporates vector-ﬁeld
augmented policy learning for high-speed obstacle avoidance,
and Yu et al. [77], who implemented a two-stage curriculum
with local Lipschitz constraints to enforce smooth, generaliz-
able racing behaviors in unknown tracks.

1) LiDAR-Inertial SLAM: LiDAR-inertial fusion represents
a cornerstone of robust navigation in complex environments,
evolving from conventional ﬁltering methods to sophisticated,
semantic-enhanced frameworks. LOAM [81] established the

ground extraction and “scan context” descriptors for rotation-
invariant loop closure. Moreover, LIO-SAM++ [88] addresses
dynamic object interference by combining geometric normal
consistency with semantic constraints, employing an adaptive
keyframe selection mechanism guided by semantic distribution
divergence.
2) LIV SLAM: LIV fusion aims to leverage the complemen-
tary strengths of geometric, inertial, and photometric data to
achieve superior state estimation and mapping performance.
The R-LIVE series represents a prominent lineage in this
domain. R2LIVE [89] utilizes a two-layer architecture com-
bining a high-frequency ESIKF with low-frequency factor
graph optimization. Building upon this framework, R3LIVE
[90] introduces a dual-subsystem infrastructure, where the
visual-inertial odometry (VIO) renders dense map textures by
minimizing frame-to-map photometric errors. Its successor,
R3LIVE++ [91], further enhances reconstruction accuracy by
incorporating the direct point-to-map registration of FAST-
LIO2 [85], while integrating camera photometric calibration
and online exposure estimation to reﬁne both localization and
radiance reconstruction.
Alternative tightly coupled architectures focus on cross-
modal mutual assistance. LVI-SAM [92] couples a visual-
inertial system (VIS) with a LiDAR-inertial system (LIS) via
a shared factor graph, where the LIS provides real-time depth
initialization for visual features and the VIS oﬀers initial pose
priors for scan matching. SDV-LOAM [93] adopts a semi-
direct depth-enhanced visual module and a scan-reconstruction
strategy to align the asynchronous LiDAR scanning frequency
with camera frame rates. Similarly, SR-LIVO [94] employs
sweep reconstruction to synchronize laser scanning intervals
with image acquisition, delegating state estimation to a high-
accuracy LIO while the vision module handles extrinsic online
optimization and color rendering.
Direct and feature-less methods have recently gained promi-
nence due to their computational eﬃciency and dense mapping
capabilities. The FAST-LIVO series [95], [96] bypasses con-
ventional feature extraction; speciﬁcally, FAST-LIVO [95]
reuses LIO map points for direct visual alignment, whereas
FAST-LIVO2 [96] introduces a sequential update strategy,
voxel ray casting, and point-plane priors to mitigate dimen-
sional mismatches between heterogeneous sensors. Addressing
hardware constraints, Wang et al. [98] proposed a dual-domain
observation model for sensors with a limited ﬁeld of view to
resist environmental degradation. Furthermore, LIVrGs [97]
integrates 3-D Gaussian splatting into the LIV framework,
utilizing adaptive voxel density control and structural simi-
larity losses to achieve high-ﬁdelity, real-time iterative neural
rendering.
.
B. Learning-Based LiDAR SLAM
1) Learning-Based Perception Enhancement: The raw data
quality of LiDAR SLAM is frequently compromised by envi-
ronmental noise and inherent geometric sparsity. To address
these limitations, recent research has increasingly integrated
deep learning techniques to perform raw data denoising and
information enrichment, thereby bolstering the algorithmic
stability of downstream odometry and mapping.
Modern frameworks typically couple deep learning with
traditional geometric ﬁlters to achieve noise suppression and
data puriﬁcation. Lian et al. [99] proposed a multirobot point
cloud puriﬁcation scheme that utilizes a PointCleanNet-based
architecture incorporating residual blocks and STN3d modules
to distinguish valid structural signals from noise. By combin-
ing this dual-branch outlier detection with KD-Tree screening
and DBSCAN clustering, the system achieves highly adaptive
data cleaning. Similarly, DLC-SLAM [100] introduces D-Net,
a lightweight denoising module leveraging dual-scale MLPs
and LSTM networks for adaptive feature fusion. Its seamless
integration into classic frameworks, such as LeGO-LOAM
and LIO-SAM, has demonstrated noticeable improvements in
robustness against distorted LiDAR returns.
Beyond denoising, upsampling sparse LiDAR data for struc-
tural enrichment remains crucial for long-range perception.
Yue et al. [101] developed a multimodal enrichment pipeline
using ERFNet for semantic segmentation and SCNNs for
predicting dense structures from sparse inputs. This fusion
of multiscale dense predictions and semantic labels trans-
forms sparse points into high-ﬁdelity representations for
precise localization. S2L-SLAM [102] extends this concept by
employing an optimized S2L-Net to translate sonar measure-
ments into a “pseudo-LiDAR” format, facilitating seamless
integration with the existing range-based SLAM algorithms.
Furthermore, the self-supervised learning and attention mech-
anisms have been widely deployed to optimize multisensor
feature fusion. Zheng et al. [103] introduced an ESIKF-based

features through geometric clustering to construct a specialized
geometric loss function, establishing a self-supervised adver-

the network to maintain global consistency by capturing long-
term motion patterns, signiﬁcantly reducing cumulative drift

3) Learning-Based Loop Closure Detection:
Deep neu-
ral networks have revolutionized loop closure detection by
extracting high-level semantic features that transcend the
limitations of hand-crafted descriptors, thereby substantially
suppressing cumulative nonlinear drift and enhancing global
map consistency.
In multirobot or cloud-robotic contexts, LCD must reconcile
matching precision with data privacy and communication
bandwidth constraints. FC-SLAM [105] addresses these chal-
lenges through a federated learning-enhanced visual-LiDAR
framework. The system employs a lightweight CNN architec-
ture for feature extraction while utilizing federated learning
to maintain raw data and private models on local agents.
A deﬁning innovation is the integration of a binary acti-
vation layer, which compresses high-dimensional descriptors
into a compact binary format compatible with BoW models.
By transmitting only anonymized BoW data to a cloud-
based dynamic dictionary—optimized via K-means trees—this
framework achieves eﬃcient place recognition without com-
promising data sovereignty.
Beyond architectural eﬃciency, enhancing LCD robustness
in physically challenging scenarios remains a critical focus.
DLC-SLAM [100] introduces FG-Conv–LC-Net, a dedicated
network designed to maintain high descriptive power in
environments characterized by structural ambiguity or high
reﬂectivity. By leveraging a dual-scale MLP and LSTM-based
feature fusion, the system captures complex spatiotemporal
dependencies that traditional geometric methods often over-
look. This deep-learning-driven approach noticeably improves
positioning accuracy and mapping reliability in large-scale,
unstructured outdoor environments.
4) Learning-Based Implicit Mapping: Traditional SLAM sys-
tems typically rely on explicit structures, such as point
clouds, surfels, or occupancy grids. Recent breakthroughs
have introduced implicit neural representations, which encode
environmental geometry directly into neural network parame-
ters, thereby enabling continuous, high-ﬁdelity mapping with
superior memory eﬃciency.
PIN-SLAM [106] pioneered a LiDAR-based framework
utilizing point-based implicit representations. Deviating from
ﬁxed grids, it employs sparse, optimizable neural points as
local feature embeddings. By ﬁtting the environment’s signed
distance ﬁeld through a globally shared multilayer perceptron,
PIN-SLAM achieves a continuous and diﬀerentiable map. This
architecture ensures global consistency during the integration
of incremental observations, providing a robust foundation for
both localization and high-resolution surface reconstruction.

IV. PERFORMANCE COMPARISON IN TYPICAL
INTERCEPTOR UAV OPERATIONAL SCENARIOS
The operational performance of SLAM systems deployed
on interceptor UAVs is strongly scenario-dependent, as dif-
ferent phases of an interception mission impose markedly
distinct constraints on sensing accuracy, computational latency,
and system reliability. To delineate the practical applicability
boundaries of the existing SLAM technologies for intercep-
tion missions, this chapter presents a systematic comparative
analysis across four representative ﬂight scenarios spanning
the full operational envelope of interceptor UAVs.
.
A. Scenario Deﬁnition and Mission Correspondence
Four typical operational scenarios are deﬁned according
to the mission phases of interceptor UAVs in GNSS-denied
environments:
1) High-Speed Cruise Scenario: The UAV ﬂies at constant
high linear velocity with relatively stable attitude, and the core
challenge is motion blur and large interframe displacement.
2) High-Maneuverability Terminal Interception Scenario: The
UAV performs large-angular-velocity maneuvers, rapid dives,
and sharp turns, imposing the strictest requirements on SLAM
real-time performance and dynamic robustness.
3) High-Altitude Patrol Scenario: The long perceptual dis-
tance leads to sparse features and reduced signal intensity.
4) Low-Altitude Close-Range Interception Scenario: It fea-
tures complex ground clutter, dense dynamic targets, and large
parallax, with prominent computational pressure and dynamic
interference.
.
B. High-Speed Cruise Scenario
The core challenge of this scenario lies in severe motion blur
and large interframe displacement caused by sustained high
linear velocity, which breaks the small-motion assumption
of most frame-based SLAM frameworks. Continuous and
stable state output is required during long-distance approach,
with strict requirements for anti-interference capability and
computational eﬃciency.
1) Visual SLAM Frameworks: The RGB-based VISs are
the most susceptible to high-speed ﬂight degradation. Severe
motion blur may degrade image quality beyond the recov-
ery capability of conventional deblurring algorithms, and
large interframe displacement violates the fundamental small-
motion assumption of visual odometry. Reducing exposure
time to mitigate blur will decrease the signal-to-noise ratio
and further degrade state estimation accuracy.
Thermal-inertial systems suﬀer from the thermal halo eﬀect
induced by sustained high-velocity movement, which blurs
temperature gradients across image frames. Meanwhile, rapid
aerodynamic body heating obscures ambient thermal features
and compromises infrared imaging quality.
Event-based VISs exhibit the most prominent advantages
in this scenario. Their microsecond-level temporal resolution
captures rapidly changing scenes and eﬀectively mitigates
motion blur, and the asynchronous event-triggering mechanism
adaptively accommodates high linear velocities.
2) LiDAR-Based SLAM Frameworks: The LISs face severe
motion
distortion
induced
by
rapid
ego-motion,
which
stretches the motion compensation capabilities of FAST-LIO
[84] and FAST-LIO2 [85]. Real-time deskewing also imposes
considerable computational overhead on onboard platforms.
The LIV tightly coupled systems bear severe computational
challenges from massive multichannel data throughput on
resource-constrained embedded platforms [89], [90]. Motion-
induced point cloud distortion and image blur jointly degrade
multimodal fusion consistency, even with the advanced com-
pensation mechanisms of R3LIVE++ [91].
3) Learning-Based SLAM Frameworks: Learning-based fea-
ture extraction networks, such as SuperPoint [59], [60], [69]
and HFNet [68], exhibit strong resilience against motion blur,
but their neural network inference latency restricts seamless
deployment in high-frequency real-time ﬂight control loops.
Single-frame dense depth estimation [61] provides instan-
taneous absolute scale information and accelerates feature
matching, yet aggressive motion blur frequently induces geo-
metric distortions in the predicted depth maps.
Multimodal fusion modules, such as MCDFus [64] and
Superglue [70], achieve superior performance under visual
degradation, but the computation-intensive structure consid-
erably increases processing latency.
End-to-end pose regression via FlowNet or RCNN architec-
tures [72], [73] reduces the computational latency of traditional
pipelines, but ﬂuctuations in model robustness under rapid ego-
motion can compromise penetration stability.
Overall, EVSLAM and LiDAR-inertial SLAM deliver the
strongest adaptability in high-speed cruise scenarios, while
pure RGB and thermal SLAM may suﬀer from notable per-
formance limitations due to their inherent imaging principles.
Learning-based methods improve environmental adaptability
at the cost of increased inference latency, which requires
targeted lightweight optimization for interceptor UAVs.

C. High-Maneuverability Terminal Interception Scenario
This scenario is characterized by extreme angular veloc-
ity, aggressive attitude transitions, and frequent maneuvering,
which cause severe viewpoint deviation, motion distortion,
and spatiotemporal misalignment. From the perspective of
actuation dynamics, high-frequency torque adjustments of the
motor drive system during rapid maneuvers will also introduce
additional torsional and attitude oscillations, further breaking
the smooth motion assumption of SLAM state estimation
[113]. It imposes the highest requirements on SLAM real-
time performance and dynamic adaptability and is one of the
most challenging operating conditions for traditional geomet-
ric frameworks.
1) Visual SLAM Frameworks: The RGB-based systems suf-
fer from severe viewpoint deviations between consecutive
frames caused by aggressive attitude transitions, which exacer-
bates the diﬃculty of feature matching and tracking. Extreme
angular velocities can exceed the frame-rate limits of con-
ventional cameras, and rapid rotations often trigger scale
ambiguity in monocular systems. Processing high-rate visual
data streams also generates loads that exceed the real-time
constraints of embedded onboard platforms.

E. Low-Altitude Close-Range Interception Scenario
This scenario features complex ground backgrounds, fre-
quent illumination ﬂuctuations, numerous dynamic targets,
and rich close-range textures. The core challenges lie in
dynamic interference suppression, massive data processing,
and geometric ambiguity caused by large parallax.
1) Visual SLAM Frameworks: The RGB-based systems face
frequent illumination ﬂuctuations from ground-reﬂected radi-
ance, dynamic shadows, and intricate visual environments.
Close-range landmarks generate substantial parallax that intro-
duces geometric ambiguities into motion parameter estimation.
Thermal-inertial systems leverage distinct thermal signa-
tures to execute target detection and continuous tracking in
low-mobility ﬂight phases, demonstrating high eﬃcacy in
isolating artiﬁcial structures and concealed targets in cluttered
environments.

A. Limitations and Prospects of Traditional SLAM
1) Inherent Limitations: The core drawback of traditional
SLAM lies in the intrinsic mismatch between sensors’ fun-
damental assumptions and the highly maneuverable dynamic
characteristics of interceptor UAV platforms. When applied to
interceptor UAV scenarios, visual sensors face degradation in
both photometric and geometric constraints. The RGB-based
visual SLAM systems, such as SVO [28] and ORB-SLAM
[17], are highly dependent on the assumption of minimal
frame-to-frame motion and photometric consistency. In high-
speed ﬂight, motion blur and rapid attitude changes violate
these prior assumptions, which may result in severe perfor-
mance degradation and even tracking failure. Even advanced
point-line fusion methods like PL-SLAM [31] struggle to
support reliable triangulation due to insuﬃcient geometric
primitives under extreme viewpoints.
Although thermal imaging systems are highly adaptable
to lighting conditions, as demonstrated by BVT-SLAM [38],
interruptions during nonuniformity correction may result in
brief perception gaps, while high-speed ﬂight can induce
thermal halo eﬀects that obscure ambient thermal gradi-
ent changes. Similarly, the LiDAR-based systems, such as
FAST-LIO [84], also encounter scanning distortion during
aggressive maneuvers, which stretches the limits of conven-
tional motion compensation mechanisms.
Event cameras can eﬀectively suppress motion blur due to
their microsecond-level temporal resolution, but this advantage
inevitably leads to data volume explosion. In texture-rich low-
altitude scenes, the event generation rate may exceed the
processing bandwidth of embedded onboard platforms. This
challenge is particularly pronounced in architectures, such as
ESVIO [41]. Furthermore, while multisensor fusion frame-
works, such as R3LIVE [90], achieve excellent localization
accuracy, their substantial computational demands exceed the
strict payload and power constraints of interceptor UAVs.
2) Technical Prospects: Building on the existing research
foundations, traditional multisensor fusion SLAM for inter-
ceptor UAVs will evolve toward higher temporal resolution,

autonomous mode switching across the full interception

next-generation systems can dynamically select the optimal

terminal interception. This architecture balances localization
accuracy, real-time performance, and energy consumption

guided keyframe selection mechanism in LIO-SAM++ [88].

strict payload and power constraints of interceptor UAVs,
the neuromorphic sensing-computing integrated architectures
represent a long-term technical direction. Built on the
asynchronous perception principle of event cameras [40],
[57], future systems can deploy front-end feature extraction
and motion estimation on spiking neural network (SNN)
neuromorphic chips. Compared with traditional von Neu-
mann architectures, neuromorphic computing can achieve an
order-of-magnitude improvement in energy eﬃciency, mak-
ing it possible to run high-performance SLAM within the
5-kg payload constraint.
e) High-ﬁdelity semantic-geometric environmental representa-
tion: The emergence of LIVrGs [97] and 3-D Gaussian
splatting marks a paradigm shift from raw point clouds to
sophisticated environmental representation strategies. Future
SLAM frameworks will integrate geometric normal consis-
tency with semantic distribution divergence [88] to more
eﬀectively distinguish static structural features from dynamic
obstacles. This enhancement is particularly valuable for low-
altitude close-range interception scenarios with dense clutter
and dynamic targets.

B. Limitations and Prospects of Deep Learning-Based
SLAM
1) Inherent Limitations: Although neural networks demon-
strate exceptional performance in solving nonlinear problems,
their application to high-speed interceptor UAVs remains
constrained by multiple practical factors, which can be sum-
marized as follows.
a) Onboard computational and energy eﬃciency bottlenecks:
High-performance learning-based SLAM frameworks, such
as IBD-SLAM [59] with neural radiance ﬁeld depth fusion
and Rover-SLAM [60] with graph neural network feature
matching, all rely on desktop-level GPUs to achieve real-
time performance. However, typical embedded computing
platforms for interceptor UAVs (e.g., Jetson Xavier NX) only
provide 1/10 to 1/20 of the computational capacity of desk-
top GPUs. Deep learning inference introduces an additional
30%–50% system power consumption [114], which directly
conﬂicts with the strict payload and power supply constraints
under the 5-kg weight limit. Even optimized frameworks strug-
gle to maintain the 50-Hz attitude control update frequency
required for terminal interception missions.
b) Lack of model interpretability and safety guarantees: The
“black-box” nature of deep neural networks poses a signiﬁcant
challenge to deployment in safety-critical military interception
missions. End-to-end pose regression models [72], [73] and
deep feature extraction modules cannot provide clear geo-
metric interpretability for their estimation results, making it
impossible to locate failure causes or predict fault boundaries
during mission execution. Unlike traditional geometric SLAM
with mathematically provable error bounds, learning-based
systems generally lack formally veriﬁable safety guarantees,
which is a critical requirement for high-risk interception
operations.
c) Sim-to-real domain gap and generalization deﬁciency:
Currently, the end-to-end SLAM models still suﬀer from
prominent performance degradation when transferred from
simulation environments to real-world scenarios. Meanwhile,
the lack of a standardized sim-to-real comparison paradigm
in this ﬁeld further hinders the objective evaluation of model
generalization ability. Most existing studies adopt idealized
simulation conﬁgurations with uniform noise distributions and
controllable illumination conditions, which deviate greatly
from various complex interferences encountered during the
actual ﬂight of interceptor UAVs.

C. Cross-Cutting Constraints of Multisensor Fusion
Systems
Beyond the algorithm-speciﬁc limitations, all multisen-
sor fusion SLAM systems face common hardware-level and
system-level constraints when deployed on interceptor UAVs.
1) Temporal Synchronization and Online Calibration Errors:
Highly dynamic ﬂight conditions amplify the impact of time
synchronization errors between heterogeneous sensors. Under
the cruise speed of 20 m/s [4], a 1-ms time oﬀset between
LiDAR and IMU will cause approximately 2 cm of point
cloud motion distortion. Under the peak attitude angular rate
of 230 /s [5], a 1-ms synchronization deviation between the
camera and IMU will induce about 0.23◦of attitude angle
error, which translates to approximately 40 cm of spatial
position deviation at a 100-m perception distance. Most exist-
ing fusion frameworks typically assume ideal hardware-level
synchronization and lack robust designs for synchronization
error tolerance.
In addition, airborne vibration and temperature variations
cause slow drift of sensor extrinsic parameters. The high-
frequency vibration mainly comes from the high-speed rotor
drive system, which not only interferes with the sampling
benchmark of IMU, camera, and LiDAR but also aggravates
motion blur and point cloud distortion. Research on adap-
tive vibration suppression based on multimodal sensor fusion
shows that targeted vibration damping can eﬀectively improve
the measurement quality of heterogeneous sensors [115]. An
extrinsic angle deviation of only 0.1◦will produce signiﬁcant
triangulation errors in long-range perception. Current tightly
coupled systems mostly rely on oﬄine calibration results and
lack real-time online extrinsic correction mechanisms under
high dynamic conditions. Calibration errors may accumulate
continuously through the optimization framework, poten-
tially leading to gradual degradation of long-term localization
accuracy.
2) Error Propagation in Tightly Coupled Fusion: In tightly
coupled fusion architectures, noise spikes or transient faults
from a single sensor may propagate to other state variables
through joint optimization, causing cascading performance
degradation. For example, IMU accelerometer bias drift will
simultaneously degrade LiDAR point cloud deskewing and
visual feature triangulation, triggering a chain reaction of
localization accuracy decline.
Quantitatively, in pure odometry mode without loop closure
correction, the position error of typical LIO systems [84]
accumulates at a rate of 0.5%–2% of the ﬂight distance.
The error coupling eﬀect of multisensor fusion increases this
accumulation rate by 15%–30%, which is more signiﬁcant in
high-maneuver scenarios. This error propagation mechanism
means that adding more sensors does not always improve per-
formance; instead, it may introduce additional failure modes
without proper fault isolation mechanisms.
3) Energy and Computational Constraints of Embedded
Architectures: Interceptor UAVs have extremely strict con-
straints on onboard computing resources due to payload and
power limitations. A typical embedded computing platform
(e.g., Jetson Xavier NX) provides approximately 21 TOPS
of INT8 computing power, which can only support real-
time operation of single-modality lightweight SLAM. An LIV
tightly coupled system with a deep learning front end requires
more than 30% additional computing resources beyond this
platform capacity, making it impossible to meet the 50-Hz
control update requirement for terminal interception on current
embedded platforms.
In terms of energy supply, the total continuous power
budget for a 5-kg payload is typically about 20–25 W, of
which the computing platform should account for no more
than 50%. The limited onboard energy directly restricts the
selection of SLAM schemes and the upper limit of computing
power, and mission endurance has always been one of the
core constraints of small UAV platforms. The related stud-
ies have shown that adaptive energy management systems
and energy-aware ﬂight control frameworks can eﬀectively
improve the overall energy eﬃciency of UAVs, prolong ﬂight
duration, and reserve a more reasonable power budget for
perception and computing modules under the same battery
capacity [116], [117]. The introduction of deep learning mod-
ules signiﬁcantly increases power consumption, compressing
the power space available for avionics and actuators, and
directly reducing mission endurance. This energy constraint
is a fundamental factor limiting the large-scale deploy-
ment of high-performance learning-based SLAM on small
interceptor UAVs.

D. Systematic Future Research Roadmap
Based on the above analysis of limitations and technical
prospects, this subsection proposes a future research roadmap
for interceptor UAV SLAM technologies.
1) Incremental Optimization of Existing Frameworks: The
core goal is to adapt mature SLAM frameworks to the
constraints of interceptor UAVs and achieve engineering-
deployable solutions through incremental optimization.
a) Lightweight LIO/VIO algorithm transplantation and embed-
ded optimization: Mature high-performance algorithms, such as
FAST-LIO2 [85] and EVIO [40], have been veriﬁed to adapt
to high-dynamic scenarios. Combined with model quantization
and code optimization technologies, they can be transplanted
to embedded platforms to meet real-time requirements.
b) Robustness enhancement for temporal synchronization and
online calibration: Based on the existing LIV fusion architec-
tures [89], [92], adding synchronization error compensation
and online extrinsic calibration modules can directly improve
system stability without changing the core framework.
c) Semantic enhancement for dynamic interference suppres-
sion: Dynamic object ﬁltering methods validated in dynamic
ORB-SLAM2 [63] and LIO-SAM++ [88] can be migrated
to low-altitude interception scenarios to improve robustness
against dynamic clutter.
2) New Paradigm Integration and Breakthrough: The core
goal is to break through the performance ceiling of traditional
frameworks and achieve full-ﬂight-envelope adaptation for
interception missions.
a) Continuous-time–event–LIDAR
tightly
coupled
fusion
SLAM:
The
independent
maturity
of
continuous-time

foundation for cross-modal integration. This fusion paradigm
will fundamentally solve the motion distortion problem in
high-maneuver terminal interception.
b) Physics-informed learning-based SLAM: Building on dif-
ferentiable dynamic models [74], [75] and implicit neural
mapping [106], embedding UAV ﬂight dynamics constraints
into learning frameworks will signiﬁcantly reduce the sim-to-
real gap and improve generalization in extreme scenarios.
c) Safety veriﬁcation system for explainable SLAM: Develop-
ing explainable AI (XAI)-enabled SLAM with geometrically
interpretable estimation results is an inevitable path to meet
the safety certiﬁcation requirements, extending the research
basis of end-to-end SLAM [72], [73].
3) Cognitive and Swarm Intelligence: The core goal is to
develop distributed, cognitive SLAM systems for future swarm
interception operations.
a) Neuromorphic sensing-computing integrated SLAM: The
development of event camera SLAM [57] and neuromorphic
hardware will enable extreme energy eﬃciency and ultrahigh
dynamic perception capability, adapting to the next-generation
lightweight interceptor UAV platforms.
b) Distributed swarm collaborative SLAM: Based on feder-

[69], interceptor UAV swarms will achieve collaborative map-
ping and localization without central nodes, improving overall
system robustness and situational awareness.
c) Large model-driven cognitive SLAM: Vision-language-

capabilities. Future cognitive SLAM systems will integrate
localization, mapping, and mission understanding capabili-
ties, enabling zero-shot adaptation to unknown tasks and
autonomous decision-making in complex interception scenar-
ios.

[96]. It is recommended for larger interceptor platforms with
relaxed payload constraints, where multimodal fusion robust-
ness justiﬁes the additional computing overhead.
SVO/Fast ORB-SLAM oﬀers extremely low computational
cost and minimal hardware requirements, making it suitable
as a low-power auxiliary localization module during benign
cruise phases, provided motion blur remains within acceptable
bounds [27], [29].
Learning-enhanced
SLAM
improves
robustness
under
visual degradation but remains dependent on desktop-grade
GPUs [60]. Until lightweight optimization and formal safety
veriﬁcation are achieved, these methods are best positioned
as supplementary front-end enhancement modules rather than
stand-alone localization systems.
End-to-end and VLA-based SLAM paradigms, while con-
ceptually promising for cognitive mission adaptation, currently
suﬀer from severe sim-to-real gaps and lack of interpretability
[80]. They remain at the exploratory research stage and
are unlikely to meet formal safety certiﬁcation requirements
within the next few years.
.
C. Critical Discussion and Outlook
The balance among algorithmic accuracy, computational
performance, and power constraints constitutes the primary
bottleneck for the practical deployment of SLAM technologies
aboard interceptor UAVs. The research community has pro-
posed numerous high-precision fusion architectures validated
on desktop workstations, yet far fewer solutions have demon-
strated real-time operation on small interceptor platforms.
Furthermore, the safety-critical nature of interception mis-
sions imposes stringent interpretability requirements that the
majority of current learning-based algorithms fail to satisfy;
regardless of theoretical performance improvements in deep
neural networks, their black-box characteristic remains a key
challenge for the operational adoption of these techniques.
Looking forward, three research directions hold the greatest
promise. First, adaptive multimode switching architectures
that dynamically select the optimal sensor-fusion conﬁgu-
ration according to ﬂight phase can reconcile algorithmic
performance and runtime eﬃciency across the full mission
envelope. Second, event-LiDAR cross-modal tightly coupled
fusion with continuous-time parameterization is expected to
fundamentally overcome motion distortion induced by high-
dynamic terminal interception maneuvers of the UAV. Third,
physics-informed learning methods with formal interpretabil-
ity guarantees, which reconcile the superior environmental
adaptability of deep learning with the safety requirements
of interception missions, represent a credible and practical
development path.
This review aims to provide both a comprehensive research
reference and actionable engineering guidance, facilitating the
deployment of full-scene, high-precision, and high-reliability
SLAM technologies in interceptor UAV systems.
