Abstract
In the field of drone navigation systems, a high-precision positioning solution can be pro-
vided by an integrated strapdown inertial navigation system (SINS)/global navigation
satellite system (GNSS). But when satellite signals are interfered with or blocked by tall
buildings, the errors of SINS will disperse rapidly due to the complex air and mechanical
vibrations, leading to a serious degradation of navigation accuracy. To enhance the po-
sitioning performance in this situation, this paper proposes a hybrid information fusion
method based on attention-augmented temporal convolutional network (TCN) for drone
SINS/GNSS navigation system. A feature integration and prediction model is constructed
to provide a pseudo-positioning reference for the integrated navigation filter during GNSS-
denied periods, in which TCN is used to establish a predictive positioning error correction
model based on inertial measurements and SINS data, while a self-attention model is incor-
porated to extract complex global drone motion features. The performance of the proposed
method has been experimentally verified using Global Positioning System (GPS) and SINS
data collected from real drone flight test. Comparison results among the proposed model,
SINS with TCN, SINS with convergent Kalman filter (KF) prediction section and SINS-only
indicate that the proposed method can effectively improve the drone positioning accuracy
in specific GNSS-denied environments.

1. Introduction
Integrated navigation system consisting of SINS and GNSS can provide continuous
high precision positioning and navigation solutions for most of today’s drones when GNSS
signals are available. The KF, capable of restraining error drift in SINS and calculating the
dynamic zero bias of SINS sensors, has been widely recognized as the standard optimal
estimation tool for current SINS/GNSS integration schemes [1]. However, due to the high
orbital altitude of navigation satellites (e.g., GPS satellites orbit at over 20,000 km), their
signals are weak when they reach the ground. As the demand for drone operations in
low-altitude environments increases, challenges for drone-mounted navigation systems
such as electromagnetic interference and building occlusion are becoming increasingly
severe, which would lead to interruption of GNSS receiver’s positioning process. In the
absence of GNSS measurement, the integrated filter cannot complete measurement updates
and accurate correction for inertial navigation error drift, causing the navigation system’s
accuracy to deteriorate rapidly, especially when using low-cost inertial measurement unit
(IMU) manufactured by micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS) processes.
During GNSS-denied periods, a SINS/GNSS integrated KF that has achieved conver-
gence of the gyroscope and accelerometer state parameters can provide a positive position
error state estimation capability for the SINS through state prediction sections only, since
the convergent gyroscope drift and accelerometer bias conform to the current actual inertial
system error parameters. However, these convergent states remain constant in GNSS-
denied environment and cannot adapt to complex carrier dynamics due to the lack of
measurement update sections.
Many research methods have been proposed to enhance positioning system accu-
racy under GNSS-denied conditions. For example, deploying additional sensors, such
as visual units, can provide auxiliary measurement data that achieve significantly higher
accuracy than SINS-only during GNSS-denied periods [2], but this may incur additional
hardware costs and increase equipment volume. Recently, with the development of ar-
tificial intelligence technology, methods based on neural networks (NNs) for modeling
pseudo-satellite or IMU parameters have been widely used. The main idea behind these
methods is to establish a mapping relationship from dynamic information provided by the
SINS to predictive reference models, such as fusion positioning results [3–7], pseudo satel-
lite navigation position, or the corresponding increment [8–23]. Such predictive models are
trained using corresponding data collected during GNSS availability and integrated filter
convergence to approximate real-time sensor parameter models. They are incorporated
into the integrated navigation system in the absence of GNSS to substitute for navigation
satellites and provide predictive auxiliary measurement information. However, since the
predictive model cannot provide absolute positioning observations, the prediction accuracy
of these models based on SINS will decrease over time. Considering that SINS errors follow
a certain trend, time can be used as the input in some studies [24]. Additionally, to address
covariance estimation issues caused by model accuracy decrease, some studies employ
adaptive parameters to adjust the convergence direction of filters [8,10], or incorporate
covariance-related parameters into prediction models for training and optimization [25,26].
With the rapid development of artificial intelligence, various NN structures have been
investigated for constructing these predictive models, among which recurrent neural net-
works (RNNs), such as GRU and LSTM, can exhibit more accurate prediction performance

owing to their sequence prediction capability [6,8,11,13,15,22]. These methods have shown
promising performance in conventional land-vehicle navigation scenarios. However, their
application to drone navigation remains challenging. Compared with vehicle-mounted
systems, IMUs for drone navigation systems typically require higher sampling rates due
to their high-dimensional motion characteristics, and are subject to more complex high-
frequency vibrations and sensor noise. In such scenarios, models with efficient parallel
computation, a strong capability for capturing long high-frequency sequences, and lower
susceptibility to steady-state sensor noise are particularly desirable.
Temporal convolutional networks (TCNs) provide an attractive alternative due to
their parallel computation capability, large receptive field, and stable training behavior.
Recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of TCN-based models in sequential
prediction tasks [10]. Nevertheless, single TCN models mainly emphasize temporal de-
pendency extraction and may not sufficiently characterize the correlation structure among
multidimensional gyroscope and accelerometer measurements, which is critical for highly
dynamic drone maneuvers.
To address these limitations, this paper proposes a hybrid information fusion method
based on attention-augmented TCN for drone SINS/GNSS integrated navigation system
during GNSS-denied periods. First, a TCN-based temporal feature extraction model was
developed, whose parallel computing mechanism offers higher computational efficiency
and robustness against steady-state noise, making it suitable for high-dynamic, high-
vibration drone inertial systems. Then, a self-attention module is introduced into the
prediction model to capture global correlation features related to maneuverability between
the multidimensional gyroscope and accelerometer inertial sequences. By jointly exploiting
temporal dynamics and inter-sensor correlations, the proposed model improves positioning
error prediction and compensation for drone SINS/GNSS integrated navigation system
during GNSS outages.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 describes the proposed
hybrid information fusion method, including the design of the predictive model and the
execution process of the integrated system. Section 3 presents the experimental flight
testing that validates the proposed method, including the experimental setup and detailed
results. The analysis and discussion are presented in Section 4. The conclusion is presented
in Section 5.
2. Methods
2.1. Hybrid Information Fusion Scheme
As one of the core technologies in modern high-accuracy positioning, navigation, and
timing (PNT) applications, SINS/GNSS integrated solutions are designed to overcome
the inherent limitations of standalone navigation systems. However, GNSS interruptions
can result in periodic discontinuities and accumulation of SINS errors in the navigation
solution. Therefore, an intelligent predictive model is integrated into the SINS/GNSS in
order to replace GNSS in terms of providing alternative measurements and reduce SINS
errors in GNSS-denied environments. The primary purpose of the prediction model is to
establish a correlation between the drone’s dynamic characteristics and the estimation of
SINS horizontal position error divergence when GNSS is operational, thereby improving
the filter’s state correction capability in the form of error observations between SINS and
reference information during GNSS-denied periods.
The hybrid fusion scheme is based on inertial-reference loosely coupled mode due to
its high efficiency, flexibility and simple structure, which facilitates maintenance. In this
scheme, SINS and reference source operate independently, with GNSS or the predictive
model acting as an auxiliary measurement source that periodically supplies position and

Before each update of GNSS measurement, the system continuously collects and
stores partial outputs from the IMU and SINS. These data contain the real-time motion
characteristics of the drone carrier and are used to construct temporal input sequences,
which serve as the training foundation for the predictive model. The input sequence
recording cycle is completed when the filter updates the state vector based on the GNSS
measurements. Simultaneously, the reference correction information is recorded to establish
the mapping relationship with the input sequence, serving as the corresponding training
output for the predictive model.
This recording process is repeated continuously based on the update interval of the
filter, during which the input sequences and the corresponding outputs are recorded and
used to train the predictive model until a stable mapping between them is established.
During GNSS-denied periods, the navigation system switches to the predictor correction
channel. In this phase, temporal sequences formatted as in the training phase are fed into
the pre-trained predictive model at each update cycle of the integrated filter. Utilizing
real-time carrier motion information, the model calculates reference correction information
in the form of an observation vector for the integrated filter to perform measurement
updates and subsequent state correction. Due to the trained predictive model’s inherent
generalization capability, the reference correction information it calculates offers greater
flexibility and accuracy than the constant bias state correction of the convergent filter under
complex flight conditions.
To maintain the stability of the model’s prediction accuracy, the input data excludes
high-dimensional divergent information such as velocity, position, or their associated errors.

2.2. Attention-Augmented TCN Predictive Model
Temporal networks are suitable for constructing predictive models, as there is a signif-
icant time dependency between the IMU sequence and the position error correction state.
Unlike land vehicle-mounted systems, drone navigation systems require higher measure-
ment frequencies due to their high-dimensional motion characteristics, and their inertial
measurement systems incorporate more steady-state noise from motors, propellers, and
mechanical resonance. Therefore, a TCN is employed to construct the regression prediction
model in this work, whose parallel processing mechanism offers higher computational
efficiency and exhibits superior robustness against steady-state noise. In practice, the
“infinite memory” advantage of RNNs is largely absent, and TCNs exhibit longer memory
than recurrent architectures with the same capacity [29].
TCNs employ convolutional rather than recurrent structures for time series modeling,
incorporating expanded causal convolutions and residual connection mechanisms. Causal
convolutions ensure that the temporal order of the sequence is preserved, while dilated
convolutions enable the model to capture long-range dependencies without excessive
network depth. Residual connection blocks enhance training stability and facilitate learning
in deeper architectures. The structure diagram of TCN is shown in Figure 2, taking a three-
layer TCN with a convolution filter size of 3 as an example.

Based on the above principles and analysis, the schematic diagram of the constructed
attention-augmented TCN predictive model is presented. As shown in Figure 5, stacked
TCN layers serve as the backbone network, with multi-head self-attention integrated to
enhance the modeling of global temporal dependencies.
Figure 5. Schematic diagram of attention-augmented TCN predictive model.
Each layer of the TCN consists of a 1D convolutional layer, weight normalization
layer, ReLU activation function layer and dropout layer in the main path. A parallel 1 × 1
convolution and an identity mapping serve as the residual branches, whose outputs are
combined with the main path via an addition layer. In parallel, the input data sequence
is extracted, flattened, and divided into distinct patch segments. Following positional
embedding, the multi-head attention layer extracts global correlation features across the
multi-axis sensor data.
The outputs of the TCN block and the attention-based feature extraction block are
compressed by global average pooling and projected into fixed-length feature vectors.
These vectors are then concatenated for feature fusion. Finally, the fused features are passed
through a fully connected layer and are mapped to a two-dimensional output space.
The convolution kernel size of the TCN is set to a typical value of 3 and the number
of filters is set to 64. These hyperparameters were selected based on common practice in
temporal sequence modeling, providing a balance between short-term dynamic feature
extraction, computational efficiency, and overfitting control. The stride was set to 1 to
preserve temporal continuity and avoid loss of local motion details.
The introduction of multi-head attention layers effectively enhances long-range mod-
eling capabilities and reduces the requirement for excessive layer depth in TCNs. Therefore,
the depth of the convolutional layers in the TCN is set to 5. With dilation factors defined as
2l−1, the effective receptive field of the top layer exceeds 60 time steps.

3.2. Dataset Partitioning for Experiments
All flight experimental data were divided into three temporally independent sections
according to the navigation workflow and the simulated GNSS interruption scenario.
The first section corresponds to the early flight phase from the 61st to the 373rd second,
immediately after takeoff. During this interval, GNSS signals were fully available. These
data were primarily used to verify stable flight status and allow the SINS/GNSS integrated
navigation filter to converge from its initialized state to a steady operating condition.
Excluding this segment from model training and testing also helps avoid the influence of
transient errors that may occur shortly after takeoff.
To evaluate the proposed method under GNSS-denied conditions, it was assumed that
the drone entered a GNSS outage region at the 493rd second of the mission. Accordingly,
the final 240 s of flight data before reaching the destination were extracted and evenly
divided into two consecutive 120 s sections.

The second section, spanning the 374th to the 493rd second, represents the pre-outage
phase with normal GNSS availability. This segment was used for model training, enabling
the proposed network to learn the recent motion dynamics and sensor error characteristics
immediately before GNSS loss.
The third section, spanning the 494th to the 613th second, represents the simulated

The effectiveness of the proposed predictive model structure and information fusion
scheme was validated by comparing the horizontal positioning and velocity errors of four
fusion strategies during operation in the simulated GNSS-denied region: (1) SINS-only,
(2) SINS with convergent KF prediction section, (3) SINS with TCN-based correction, and
(4) SINS with the proposed TCN-Attention-based correction model. Performance validation
under GNSS-denied conditions was conducted using three validation scenarios, denoted as
Experiments 1–3, each designed to examine the proposed method under different outage
durations and temporal conditions.
Experiment 1 evaluates short-term GNSS outage performance over a 60 s validation
interval from the 494th to the 553rd second. In this scenario, the model was trained using
the immediately preceding GNSS-available segment, and the four methods were compared
in terms of positioning and velocity accuracy after the drone had operated inside the
GNSS-denied zone for one minute.
Experiment 2 uses the same 60 s GNSS-denied validation interval as Experiment 1,
but introduces a 30 s temporal gap between the end of the training data and the start of the
validation period. Specifically, the interval from the 494th to the 523rd second was excluded
from training and treated as an unobserved transition period. This scenario was designed
to assess model robustness under temporal distribution shift and delayed adaptation to
changing motion dynamics.
Experiment 3 evaluates longer-duration GNSS outage performance over the full 120 s
interval from the 494th to the 613th second. In this case, the pre-outage training segment
and the outage validation segment were both set to 120 s, providing an equal-duration split
between training and testing. This scenario was designed to compare the long-horizon
prediction stability and cumulative error suppression capability of the different methods
during extended GNSS denial.
All experiments were conducted using the same TCN and TCN-Attention models
trained on the designated model-training segment (374th–493rd second) with the parameter
settings listed in Table 1. No retraining or parameter adjustment was performed for
different validation scenarios. This unified evaluation protocol is necessary to ensure a fair
comparison among experiments and eliminate the influence of scenario-specific tuning, and
to objectively assess the generalization capability and practical applicability of the proposed
methods under varying GNSS-denied conditions. Based on the above experimental settings,

4. Discussion
From Figures 10, 13 and 16, together with Tables 4, 6 and 8, all three aided strategies
significantly reduced the maximum horizontal positioning error relative to the SINS-only
https://doi.org/10.3390/rs18091379
[p.21]
Remote Sens. 2026, 18, 1379
21 of 24
solution in Experiments 1–3. Horizontal positioning and velocity errors are computed as
the Euclidean norms of their corresponding two-dimensional component errors. Com-
pared with SINS-only, the SINS solution, aided by the converged KF prediction section,
achieved reductions of 20%, 26%, and 26%, respectively. This indicates that even without
further GNSS updates, the previously converged filter states still provide useful short-
term compensation for inertial error growth. When real-time motion information was
further introduced through the data-driven correction model, larger improvements were
obtained. The TCN-based method reduced the maximum horizontal positioning error by
24%, 32%, and 31%, respectively, demonstrating that temporal convolution is effective in
learning recent dynamic error evolution from pre-outage inertial sequences. After further
incorporating maneuverability-related feature extraction through the attention mechanism,
the proposed TCN-Attention method achieved the best overall performance, with error
reductions of 26%, 35%, and 34%, respectively. The additional gains suggest that modeling
cross-channel dependencies among multidimensional inertial measurements is beneficial,
particularly under highly dynamic drone motion conditions.
A similar trend can be observed in the horizontal velocity results shown in
Figures 11, 14 and 17, together with Tables 5, 7 and 9. For the learning-based meth-
ods, although the observation vector only contains latitude and longitude error estimates,
velocity errors are also indirectly constrained through recursive propagation of the SINS
error-state equations. Compared with SINS-only, the converged KF prediction method
reduced the maximum horizontal velocity error by 25%, 23%, and 26% in Experiments 1–3,
respectively. The TCN-based method further improved these reductions to 27%, 27%, and
31%, while the proposed TCN-Attention method achieved the largest reductions of 29%,
29%, and 35%. These results demonstrate that the converged KF prediction section remains
effective for short-term compensation because the previously estimated parameters, such as
sensor zero-bias states, are still consistent with the current inertial system. However, once
measurement updates are unavailable, these fixed estimates cannot adapt to evolving drone
maneuvers or time-varying sensor behavior, resulting in progressively limited correction
capability. Both learning-based correction methods outperform the conventional KF predic-
tion strategy in terms of horizontal positioning and velocity accuracy during GNSS outages,
as they adaptively exploit recent IMU motion sequences to estimate time-varying naviga-
tion errors. Consequently, they provide clear improvements over the KF-based approach.
Building on this foundation, the proposed TCN-Attention framework achieves additional
performance gains in all three experiments compared with the TCN-only model. This
indicates that, through the exploitation of global cross-channel dependencies associated
with drone maneuvers, the added attention mechanism further captures the relationship
between recent IMU motion dynamics and accumulated SINS navigation errors, thereby
enhancing correction accuracy. Moreover, the performance advantage becomes more pro-
nounced in Experiments 2 and 3, where temporal mismatch or longer outage duration is
introduced. This suggests that the proposed method has stronger robustness to changing
motion conditions and superior capability for suppressing cumulative inertial drift over
extended GNSS-denied intervals, which explains its consistently superior performance
across different validation scenarios.
5. Conclusions
This paper proposes a hybrid drone SINS/GNSS information fusion method based
on attention-augmented TCN, which can further suppress inertial navigation errors under
GNSS-denied conditions without requiring additional sensors. By leveraging the high
computational efficiency of the TCN and its robustness to steady-state noise, a self-attention
mechanism is introduced to address the limitations of convolutional networks in capturing
https://doi.org/10.3390/rs18091379
[p.22]
Remote Sens. 2026, 18, 1379
22 of 24
long-range feature correlations in multidimensional sensor sequences. Based on real flight
data collected from a drone SINS/GNSS integrated navigation system, three experiments
were conducted, including short-horizon and long-horizon continuous splits, a discontinu-
ous split with a temporal gap, and an ablation study involving the self-attention module.
The experimental results demonstrate that, compared with the standalone TCN model,
the proposed predictive model and data fusion scheme exhibit stronger state correction
capability by establishing a more accurate mapping between inertial measurements and
positioning error correction through multidimensional feature extraction and modeling.
In addition, since the proposed predictive model uses the fused state as training targets
to mitigate observational noise, the framework has the potential to be extended to other
sensor fusion architectures. By incorporating fused positioning outputs from an integrated
navigation system as supervisory references, the model can learn the relationship between
inertial data and optimal or suboptimal reference correction states, thereby providing
redundant correction information under sensor degradation or failure conditions.
Despite the encouraging results, several limitations remain. First, similar to other
hybrid NN-KF architectures, the covariance of the observation data provided by the neural
network must be carefully specified. Since the primary contribution of this work lies
in the structure of regression predictive model, a conventional residual-based constant
parameterization was adopted in this paper. Future research may incorporate adaptive
covariance estimation based on innovation statistics or integrate it into neural network
modeling to learn uncertainty.
Second, the experimental validation in this study was conducted using a single flight
dataset and a relatively limited mission profile. The tested trajectory mainly consisted
of straight flight segments with moderate maneuver transitions, which is representative
of many low-altitude drone operational paths. However, broader validation across more
varied flight patterns is still desirable. Therefore, further verification under more diverse
trajectories, aggressive turning maneuvers, altitude-varying missions, highly dynamic
operational scenarios, and unseen operating conditions is still required. Future work may
investigate online adaptation, continual learning, or domain-generalization strategies to
improve robustness.
Finally, although the proposed model demonstrated promising generalization across
multiple simulated GNSS-denied validation settings, the training process was based on
data collected from one platform and one sensor configuration. Additional studies in-
volving different drone platforms, IMU grades, payload conditions, and environmental
disturbances would be valuable to assess cross-platform transferability and robustness.
