Abstract
Cooperative localization provides a promising solution for multi-UAV navigation in GNSS-
denied environments. However, centralized cooperative localization and exact cross-
covariance-based distributed methods usually require global state management or explicit
propagation of inter-node cross-covariance, resulting in heavy communication and com-
putational burdens. To address this problem, this paper proposes a consistent-innovation-
aided distributed cooperative localization method for multi-glider UAV swarms. The
method uses a strapdown inertial navigation system as the reference source and introduces
inter-node ultra-wideband ranging as cooperative constraints. Each node maintains only its
local state and covariance, while a conservative innovation covariance approximation is con-
structed to perform distributed measurement updates without explicitly propagating global
cross-covariance. The cooperative localization sub-filter is further integrated with altitude,
geomagnetic, and scene-matching sub-filters through a federated filtering framework with
adaptive vector information allocation. A GNSS-denied multi-glider release simulation
is established to compare the proposed method with non-cooperative localization, cen-
tralized cooperative localization, and an exact-cross-covariance distributed baseline. The
results show that the proposed method reduces the position, velocity, and yaw RMSEs
by 27.14%, 21.90%, and 9.68%, respectively, compared with the non-cooperative method.
Compared with the exact-cross-covariance baseline, the proposed method achieves compa-
rable localization accuracy while reducing communication traffic by approximately 91.44%.

1. Introduction
Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have been widely used in many civil applications,
such as emergency rescue, geographic surveying and mapping, energy inspection, and
logistics distribution, which imposes increasingly high requirements on autonomous,
long-endurance, and reliable navigation and positioning capabilities. A typical single-
UAV navigation scheme relies heavily on the Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS).
However, its performance becomes vulnerable in complex environments such as urban
canyons, where satellite signals are easily affected by physical obstruction and severe
multipath effects, resulting in a sharp degradation of positioning accuracy [1]. To overcome
the inherent vulnerability of satellite-based navigation, multi-source integrated navigation
systems have been widely developed, usually taking the inertial navigation system (INS)
as the reference and integrating external sensors such as vision, radar, barometers, and
magnetometers [2]. Perception sensors may suffer from illumination changes, feature
degradation, and image mismatching, and they often require prior maps, which limits
their applicability in unknown exploration scenarios. With the rapid development of UAV
swarm operations, navigation no longer needs to rely solely on the accuracy and stability
of sensors carried by a single vehicle. Cooperative navigation (CN) has therefore emerged
as an effective technology for complex environments [3]. By fusing onboard sensor data
with cooperative information from other nodes, the swarm can achieve complementary
navigation enhancement [4].
Cooperative localization studies initially verified the moving long-baseline concept, in
which a moving leader was used as a dynamic reference station to provide positioning con-
straints for follower vehicles [5]. This concept was subsequently extended to aerial swarm
navigation. Edwards et al. proposed a master–slave cooperative navigation algorithm
and demonstrated that, when the leader provides high-precision position information, the
follower can achieve non-divergent localization using only relative ranging measurements.
In addition, increasing the number of inter-node ranging observations can effectively re-
duce the geometric dilution of precision and improve the localization accuracy of follower
nodes [6]. However, such master–slave methods usually rely on high-precision leader
nodes and relative ranging or bearing sensors, which limits their robustness and scala-
bility in large-scale UAV swarms. To overcome the dependence on a central or leader
node, distributed cooperative localization has become an important research direction.
In this architecture, there is no central processing node, and each UAV estimates its own
state locally by exchanging relative measurements and onboard sensor information with
neighboring nodes within the communication range [7,8]. Inter-node ranging information
is used as a relative constraint to correct the accumulated drift of the onboard inertial
navigation system, while neighboring nodes exchange only necessary state and covariance
information through wireless links. Compared with centralized cooperative localization,
this architecture can reduce dependence on a central node and improve the robustness and
scalability of swarm navigation. Recently, Zheng et al. proposed a multi-UAV collaboration
and IMU-fusion localization method for partially GNSS-denied scenarios, showing that
https://doi.org/10.3390/rs18142286
[p.3]

collaborative ranging and inertial information can enhance localization stability [9]. Akhi-
hiero et al. presented a UAV–UGV cooperative localization architecture in a GNSS-denied
subterranean environment, where UWB ranging, onboard sensors, and nonlinear estima-
tors were integrated to support cooperative state estimation [10]. Li et al. developed a
distributed cooperative localization method based on UWB/INS integration and analyzed
the influence of cross-covariance on localization accuracy, confirming that relative distance
measurements can suppress INS drift in unmanned clusters [11]. These studies demonstrate
the effectiveness of cooperative ranging and multi-source fusion for improving navigation
performance in GNSS-denied environments.
Taking swarm aerial vehicles operating in complex GNSS-denied environments as
the research object, this paper addresses the problems of high cross-covariance mainte-
nance burden and insufficient engineering feasibility in existing cooperative localization
methods. A distributed cooperative localization framework is established, the generation
and propagation mechanism of cross-covariance caused by inter-node ranging updates is
analyzed, and a consistent-innovation-aided distributed cooperative localization method
without explicitly maintaining global cross-covariance is proposed. The remainder of this
paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the materials and methods, including the
centralized cooperative localization model, the proposed consistent-innovation-aided dis-
tributed cooperative localization algorithm, and the adaptive vector information allocation
strategy. Section 3 reports the experimental results under nominal and packet-loss commu-
nication conditions. Section 4 discusses the effectiveness, applicability, and limitations of
the proposed method. Section 5 concludes the paper.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Master–Slave Cooperative Localization Method
In cooperative localization for multiple-UAV formations, the master–slave navigation
architecture is one of the most widely adopted approaches. In this framework, a master
UAV equipped with high-precision positioning sensors serves as a navigation reference for
the entire cooperative network. Before release, the carrier platform transfers its navigation
information to the slave UAV through a transfer alignment module, providing the initial
longitude, latitude, altitude, roll, pitch, and yaw states required for autonomous navigation.
During the subsequent gliding phase, relative range and bearing measurements
between the master and slave UAVs are obtained using onboard ranging and angle-
measurement sensors. Based on these measurements, cooperative localization can be
performed to improve the navigation performance of the slave UAV. The three-dimensional
spatial relationship between the master and slave UAVs is illustrated in Figure 1. In this
figure, Master denotes the position of the master node, which carries a high-precision
anti-interference navigation system and continuously provides accurate navigation infor-
mation. Slave denotes the position of the slave node in the cooperative network. The
relative distance between the master and slave nodes is represented by d, while θ and φ
denote the relative azimuth and elevation observations measured by the slave UAV.
The slave first uses the high-precision position information provided by the master
node in the cooperative network, as well as the relative distance and relative bearing
observations between the slave and the master, to obtain its own position coordinates
through spatial geometric solution. Then, the solved position coordinates are used as
external position observations of the slave inertial navigation system and introduced into
the filter framework to construct the corresponding loosely coupled position observation
equation, so as to correct and constrain the navigation solution of the slave and improve
the localization accuracy of the slave node under satellite-denied environments [9]. The
algorithm flow is shown in the following figure.

2.2. Consistent-Innovation-Aided Distributed Localization
In the cooperative navigation scenario of an unmanned aerial vehicle swarm, all nodes
are moving bodies with similar accuracy, and the cooperative observation data are inter-
node ranging. Therefore, in addition to limited engineering applicability, the operating
mechanism of the above master–slave structure is also not applicable.

The CCL algorithm not only brings huge communication and computational pressure

2.2.2. Distributed Cooperative Algorithm Based on Consistent Innovation Covariance
Referring to the idea of the centralized filtering algorithm, the state vectors of all
nodes are uniformly included in the global state for optimal estimation. If the distributed
system is expected to achieve estimation performance equivalent to that of centralized
filtering, each node i must be able to locally and independently complete all information
involved in prediction updating and measurement updating, including but not limited
https://doi.org/10.3390/rs18142286
[p.7]

to the state transition matrices, predicted covariances, cross-covariances, and so on of all
other nodes. For a system that uses a high-frequency inertial measurement unit as the
reference, the system must continuously broadcast the state transition matrix of each node
itself at a high frequency and complete cross-covariance prediction calculation [14]. As the
scale of the cooperative system expands, the system is required to frequently exchange
a large amount of information, causing the communication and storage burdens to grow
rapidly and making it difficult to satisfy engineering constraints. This reflects that the
traditional cross-covariance maintenance mechanism is difficult to implement in distributed
cooperative localization. The distributed unmanned aerial vehicle swarm system is shown

For the distance measurement between node I and II, the corresponding measurement
matrix H has opposite signs for the elements related to the position states of the two nodes.
This structural characteristic makes the measurement have a strong constraint ability on
the relative position error between the two nodes in a statistical sense. When the two
nodes have the same displacement along the line-of-sight direction, this displacement is
canceled in the innovation, and therefore its direct effect on filter updating is weak. As
the filtering process continues, the relative error is continuously suppressed, while the co-
directional translation error component is difficult to correct effectively, so that the position
errors between nodes tend to show statistical characteristics of co-directional variation in
this direction.
In the verification experiment set up later, multiple glider UAVs are sequentially de-
ployed from the same carrier aircraft, inherit similar initial navigation errors, and then enter
a gliding phase. Neighboring UAVs tend to share similar low-frequency position error com-
ponents caused by initial alignment uncertainty, inertial drift, and common environmental
effects. When projected onto the UWB line-of-sight direction, these error components tend
to vary in the same direction [16]. Meanwhile, UWB ranging mainly constrains the relative
distance between nodes, so the relative error component can be effectively suppressed,
whereas the co-directional translation component is difficult to eliminate directly. This pro-
vides a physical explanation for the positive correlation of position errors along the UWB
line-of-sight direction and supports the conservative innovation covariance approximation
used in the proposed method.
In this section, under the premise of not explicitly maintaining the cross-covariance
block, a consistent innovation covariance construction method for distributed cooperative
localization is proposed. The relationship between the local innovation covariance approxi-
mation and the theoretical value is proved from both mathematical structure and physical
mechanism. Although ignoring the cross-covariance loses a certain degree of optimality, it
is simple to implement in engineering, has controllable computational and communication
overhead, and at the same time maintains a certain degree of conservativeness in the
covariance sense.
2.2.3. Adaptive Vector Information Allocation Algorithm
Based on previous research results [17], the above proposed algorithm is constructed
as a cooperative sub-filter, and then a federated filtering algorithm framework is built
by combining it with an altimeter sub-filter, a geomagnetic information sub-filter, and a
https://doi.org/10.3390/rs18142286
[p.10]
Remote Sens. 2026, 18, 2286
10 of 19
visual scene matching sub-filter. A vector information allocation algorithm based on the
mean-square error matrix is used to optimize the system, so that each state variable can
obtain different information allocation factors in real time, thereby further improving the
filtering estimation accuracy. For the convenience of calculation, the diagonal elements
of the mean-square error matrix can be used to independently allocate information to the
components of the state of the sub-filter.
Figure 4 shows the federated fusion architecture of the proposed method. The dis-
tributed cooperative localization method proposed in Section 2.2.2 serves as the cooperative
sub-filter in the federated architecture. The inertial navigation system provides the common
state propagation reference for all sub-filters. The cooperative sub-filter takes inter-node
UWB range measurements and the broadcast state information of neighboring nodes as
inputs, and performs the distributed cooperative update to generate the local cooperative
estimate and covariance. Simultaneously, the altimeter, geomagnetic, and scene-matching
sub-filters process their corresponding onboard sensor observations and output their own
local state estimates and covariance matrices. The outputs of all sub-filters are fused by the
adaptive vector information allocation module to obtain the global navigation solution.

3. Results
To verify the effectiveness of the proposed method, a multi-glider UAV cooperative
navigation simulation scenario is constructed under GNSS-denied conditions. Multiple
localization algorithms are compared in terms of positioning accuracy, velocity and yaw
estimation, convergence behavior, communication traffic, and robustness under packet loss.
3.1. Experimental Setup
An airborne-release glider aerial vehicle digital simulation platform is built based
on Linux 5.15 LTS, Ubuntu 22.04, and Qt 5.12.12. Through software, the carrier-aircraft
trajectory, target point, environmental parameters, and various sensor model parameters
are set and initialized. With reference to glider aerial vehicle dynamic state propagation and
control quantity calculation, the attitude and velocity of the aerial vehicle are adjusted and

[p.12]

the true motion trajectory is generated. Various sensor measurement data are generated
according to the sensor error model. The navigation and localization module fuses inertial,
ranging, altitude, geomagnetic, and other sensor data in real time through the proposed
algorithm to provide real-time navigation and localization solutions. The recording module
is used for visual display and data recording of the simulation process, providing a data
basis for subsequent statistical analysis.
The carrier aircraft is set to release 10 glider aerial vehicles sequentially at 1 s intervals.
At the release instant, each aerial vehicle uses the high-precision pose information provided
by the carrier aircraft to complete initial alignment and state initialization. Then it enters
the GNSS-denied gliding phase and relies only on onboard sensors and inter-node relative
ranging to complete autonomous localization. The overall experimental process is shown
in Figure 5.

To highlight the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm, four comparison schemes are
considered. Algorithm A is a non-cooperative method, in which each UAV performs multi-
source integrated navigation independently without inter-node information exchange.
Algorithm B is the centralized cooperative localization (CCL) method, which incorporates
all node states and inter-node ranging measurements into a global filter and is used as a
high-accuracy reference. Algorithm C is an exact-cross-covariance distributed cooperative

centralized cooperative localization under ideal communication conditions. Algorithm D
is the proposed consistent-innovation-aided distributed cooperative localization method,
which avoids explicit global cross-covariance propagation and constructs a conservative
innovation covariance approximation for local updates. The four algorithms adopt the
same initial conditions, motion trajectories, and sensor error models, and the evaluation
period is uniformly set to 250 s. The simulated performance indicators of each sensor are

The carrier aircraft sequentially releases ten glider aerial vehicles near the preset
release point. The initial release position is approximately located at longitude 119.98024◦
and latitude 31.00237◦. After release, each aerial vehicle enters the unpowered gliding
phase. The overall trajectory extends toward the southeast, and a certain lateral spacing is
maintained during flight, forming the spatial distribution characteristics of multi-vehicle
parallel gliding. The swarm reference truth trajectory is shown in Figure 6. The average
distance of each trajectory is approximately 50 km. From the planar map trajectory, it can
be seen that after the aerial vehicle swarm departs from the initial release area, it crosses
water and land areas, and the trajectory covers a large geographical space, which can
better simulate the navigation requirements of long-distance gliding flight and complex
geographical backgrounds in actual missions.
As shown in Figure 7, the three-dimensional reference truth trajectory results further

3.3. Robustness Analysis Under Packet-Loss Communication Conditions
The above experiment evaluates the localization performance under stable commu-
nication conditions. In practical UAV swarm missions, cooperative measurements and
neighboring state information may be intermittently unavailable due to packet loss, com-
munication delay, or link interruption. Therefore, a communication-constrained scenario is
further introduced to compare the non-cooperative method, the centralized cooperative
localization (CCL) method, and the proposed distributed cooperative localization (DCL)
method under packet loss.
Figure 11 shows the packet loss rate over time in the communication-constrained
scenario. The first 250 s remain identical to the nominal experiment. After 250 s, inde-
pendent packet loss with a configured probability of 20% is imposed on each cooperative
communication link.

Figure 12a compares the influence of packet loss on different cooperative localization
algorithms. After a 20% packet loss rate is introduced, the global update failure rate of the
CCL method increases significantly because CCL relies on network-wide synchronized
fusion. The loss of several independent communication links may invalidate an entire
global update, causing the CCL error to increase rapidly and weakening its cooperative
advantage. In contrast, the proposed DCL method maintains bounded error behavior and
remains close to its pre-loss performance, since the distributed filter does not require all
network packets to be available at the same epoch. The quantitative results in Figure 12b
show that the position RMSE of CCL increases from 16.0 m before packet loss to 53.1 m
after packet loss, which is even larger than the non-cooperative result of 24.2 m at 250 s.
This demonstrates the fragility of the centralized architecture when global communication
integrity cannot be guaranteed. By comparison, the proposed DCL method maintains
a post-loss position RMSE of 18.5 m, close to its pre-loss value of 17.2 m. These results
indicate that the proposed distributed architecture degrades gracefully under packet loss
and is more suitable for communication-constrained UAV swarm localization.

4. Discussion
The experimental results show that the proposed method significantly improves po-
sition, velocity, and yaw estimation compared with the non-cooperative method, while
achieving accuracy close to centralized and ex-act-cross-covariance cooperative localiza-
tion. Compared with the ex-act-cross-covariance baseline, the proposed method greatly
reduces communication traffic by replacing full cross-covariance propagation with a con-
servative innovation covariance approximation. The packet-loss experiment further in-
dicates that the proposed distributed architecture maintains bounded errors under de-
graded communication conditions, demonstrating better robustness and scalability for
UAV swarm applications.
Despite the above advantages, the proposed method still has several limitations.
First, the current validation is mainly based on a digital simulation scenario. In practical
UAV swarm missions, communication packet loss, transmission delay, asynchronous in-
formation exchange, and abnormal ranging values may affect the stability of distributed
measurement updates. Therefore, real-world flight experiments under complex communi-
cation conditions, dynamic topology changes, and non-line-of-sight ranging environments
will be further carried out in future work. In addition, the proposed consistent in-novation
covariance approximation avoids explicit cross-covariance maintenance by introducing a
conservative approximation. Although this strategy helps maintain filtering consistency
and reduces the communication and computational burden, it may sacrifice part of the esti-
mation optimality compared with a fully centralized filter. Therefore, further optimization
of covariance approximation and adaptive communication mechanisms will be investi-
gated to improve the robustness and scalability of the proposed method in large-scale
UAV swarms.
5. Conclusions
This paper proposed a consistent-innovation-aided distributed cooperative localiza-
tion method for multi-glider UAV swarms in GNSS-denied environments. The centralized
cooperative localization model was first analyzed to clarify the generation and propagation
of inter-node cross-covariance caused by cooperative ranging updates. On this basis, a
conservative innovation covariance approximation was introduced so that each UAV only
needs to maintain its local state and covariance while using neighboring state information
for distributed measurement updates. The cooperative sub-filter was further integrated
with altitude, geomagnetic, and scene-matching sub-filters through an adaptive vector
information allocation framework. Simulation results show that the proposed method
reduces the position, velocity, and yaw RMSEs by 27.14%, 21.90%, and 9.68%, respectively,
compared with the non-cooperative method. Compared with the exact-cross-covariance
distributed baseline, it achieves comparable localization accuracy while reducing com-
munication traffic by approximately 91.44%. Under a 20% packet-loss rate, the proposed
method maintains bounded positioning errors, demonstrating its robustness and practical
applicability for communication-constrained UAV swarm navigation.
