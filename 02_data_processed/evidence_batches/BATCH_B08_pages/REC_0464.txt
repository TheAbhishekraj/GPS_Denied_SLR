Relative Visual-Inertial Odometry for Fixed-Wing
Aircraft in GPS-Denied Environments
Gary Ellingson
Department of Mechanical Engineering
Brigham Young University
Provo, Utah
gary.ellingson@byu.edu
Kevin Brink
Air Force Research Laboratory
Munitions Directorate
Eglin AFB, Florida
Tim McLain
Department of Mechanical Engineering
Brigham Young University
Provo, Utah
Abstract—This paper introduces an odometry-like front-end
estimator for GPS-denied ﬁxed-wing ﬂight using a monocular
camera. This development is a critical component for enabling
ﬁxed-wing aircraft to use a novel methodology called relative
navigation. Relative navigation allows a single vehicle to grace-
fully accommodate intermittent GPS or geo-registered image
updates. It can also enable communication and a computa-
tionally feasible cooperative, batch-based navigation approach
for numerous vehicles. This paper presents a visual-odometry
estimator that is appropriate for ﬁxed-wing ﬂight characteristics
and sensing requirements. We propose a modiﬁcation to the
multi-state-constraint Kalman ﬁlter which, instead of running
the ﬁlter in a single inertial/global frame, regularly resets the
navigation frame to a new local origin. The vehicle operates
based on the most recent keyframe and produces a marginalized
change in pose (odometry) and covariance output whenever the
keyframe is updated. This allows the front end to operate at
the IMU rate to accommodate guidance and control inputs while
sharing marginalized keyframe-to-keyframe odometry estimates
with a back-end, graph optimization at a much lower rate than
traditional batch navigation schemes. Results from testing the
proposed method in a high-ﬁdelity simulation of the ﬁlter is also
presented.
I. INTRODUCTION
Unmanned aircraft systems (UAS) are increasingly in need
of GPS-denied navigation capabilities. In the past several years
traditional navigation methods that fuse GPS with inertial mea-
surements (GPS-INS) have been optimized and miniaturized.
GPS-INS has enabled small UAS to operated in a variety
of new and advanced ways, including in conﬁned spaces,
urban environments, and inside buildings. For UAS to be
widely adopted they will need to maintain the same naviga-
tion capabilities when GPS is unavailable. Civil applications,
such as autonomous drone delivery services and infrastructure
inspection, need to be able to operate during GPS degradation
and dropout due to proximity to buildings and obstacles. In
military theaters, where UAS are being used to observe and
prosecute the enemy, GPS is often spoofed or jammed and
UAS need to perform the same functions without relying on
GPS measurements.
In an attempt to make up for the lack of GPS, additional
exteroceptive sensors can be added to the vehicle and their
measurements fused in a ﬁlter. Cameras, depth sensors, and
laser scanners have been used on UAS to measure the motion

of the vehicle relative to the environment and make the esti-
mated motion more accurate. Without geo-registered updates
to remove drift that accumulates over time, however, global

Extended Kalman ﬁlters (EKF’s), which have become ubiq-
uitous in robotics [4], are often used for sensor ﬁltering and
estimation on UAS [5], [6]. An EKF is able to fuse sensor
measurements and produce state estimates while accounting
for process error and sensor uncertainties. Further, EKF’s
utilize the computational advantage of the linear, Gaussian
update from the Kalman ﬁlter while accommodating the
nonlinearities of the process. Together these properties perform
well when GPS is available and drift errors remain small. The
Gaussian representation of the state belief uncertainty becomes
inconsistent, however, when there are large nonlinearity errors
due to drift and unobservability of the system.
In recent years, researchers in the BYU MAGICC Lab have
proposed on a new approach as a solution the observability
and consistency issues mentioned above. The approach is
called relative navigation [7], [8] and is a methodology and
framework for GPS-denied navigation. It includes a front-end
EKF that estimates the relative state and navigates relative
to the local surroundings, and a back end that optimizes the

Fixed-wing UAS require special considerations for effec-
tive GPS-denied navigation. Most GPS-denied research and
development, including relative navigation, has focused almost
exclusively on multirotor UAS. Aircraft dynamics and ﬂight
speeds of ﬁxed-wing UAS make the sensing and estimation
requirements different from that of multirotor. Often, when
ﬁxed-wing solutions do exist, the approaches make signiﬁcant
simplifying assumptions, such as operating over ﬂat-earth or
in Manhattan world environments, or having speciﬁc sensing
requirements, such as a downward-facing camera or depth
measurements [9]. Because multirotor aircraft can easily hover
in place, they can ﬂy in and around structured environments
where depth sensors such as laser scanners are effective for
measuring the motion of the aircraft. Fixed-wing aircraft are
more suited, however, to ﬂy high above the environment where
depth sensors are much less effective in measuring aircraft
motion.
This work presents the development of an EKF-based,
tightly-coupled, visual-inertial odometry (VIO) to enable the
relative navigation framework for ﬁxed-wing UAS. Since
depth sensors, such as laser scanners and RGB-D cameras,
are impractical for small, unmanned, ﬁxed-wing aircraft the
ﬁlter will use only a monocular camera and will make no
assumption about the distance to observed features. It will
also enable accurate ﬂight at high airspeeds with ﬁxed-wing
dynamics. The ﬁlter will replace the front-end estimation and
its output will enable the advantages of the global back-end
within the relative navigation framework. Testing of the ﬁlter
took place in a high-ﬁdelity aircraft simulation environment
shown in Figure 1. The results demonstrate that the ﬁlter
maintains a total accumulated error of less than 3% of the
distance traveled.
II. RELATED WORKS
This work draws on previously published research from two
distinct areas: The overall GPS-denied architecture utilizes the
relative navigation framework, and the VIO approach comes
from the development of the multi-state-constraint Kalman
ﬁlter (MSCKF). Related work in these areas are introduced
in the following sections.
A. Relative Navigation
The main idea for relative navigation is fairly simple but it
has some profound consequences. The concept can be simply
stated: “I know I am right here, right now.” Put another way, at
any time an agent can maintain high conﬁdence of its motion
relative to the local environment, even though the global
position and heading is unknown or unobservable. An example
is the often-used practice of initializing a navigation ﬁlter for
a robot to zeros with zero covariance and then navigating with
respect to the newly-declared but globally-arbitrary origin.
This idea is utilized by the relative navigation framework
through the relative-reset step, which lends itself to keyframe-
based odometry. Within the front-end, after the vehicle travels
away from its origin, the EKF declares a new origin at the
current vehicle location in coordination with the deceleration
of a new keyframe image. The vehicle can then also zero
the covariance associated of the position states. The states
then evolve from the new origin as the vehicle moves. The
transformation from just before the reset step is sent to the
global back-end as an edge in a directed pose graph. We will
refer to each new origin as a node (or node frame) in the
graph. By allowing the front-end to only operate relative to
a local origin the ﬁlter is able to maintain consistency and
is observable by construction [10]. Uncertainty is effectively
removed from the front-end ﬁlter and sent to a global back
end where graph optimization is more suited to handle it.
The back end is responsible for producing the global po-
sition and heading. It represents the global states in a pose
graph where vertices are the estimated poses of each keyframe
or node and edges are the transformations between nodes.
The global pose, which is necessary for performing a global
mission, can be produced by combining, or composing, the
transforms. These short transformations are able to represent
the nonlinear coupling in SE(3) better than a single pseudo-
global state with a Gaussian uncertainty [10]. The back end is
also able to do edge optimization on the pose graph to improve
global state estimates. This optimization has been used to
robustly incorporate other constraints, such as opportunistic
GPS measurements and place-recognition loop closures, for
increasing the localization accuracy [8], [11]. Over-constrained
graph optimization is well understood and open-source frame-

In prior work, the front end has been implemented using
a loosely-coupled visual-inertial odometry algorithm. This

with inertial measurements and to perform the relative-reset
step [15]. The visual algorithms declare new keyframes regu-
larly to maintain visual overlap with the most recent keyframe
image. The methods used, so far, have also relied on depth
measurements, such as RGBD cameras and laser scanners,
to resolve scale ambiguity [8]. Since these depth sensors are
likely not reasonable for a low cost, small, ﬁxed-wing UAS,
the scale must be resolved without them. This is why we have
selected the MSCKF as basis for a new front-end odometry.
B. MSCKF
The MSCKF has been an important contribution to the
VIO research area. It has been demonstrated for use on smart
phones [16], ground vehicles [17], and spacecraft [18]. With
several extensions, it has achieved good consistency properties
and accumulated error less then 1% of the distance traveled.
The MSCKF is presented as a dual to EKF SLAM [19].
EKF SLAM includes states that evolve with the motion of
the vehicle (ximu), and then augments the state vector with
the location of each image feature. The state vector x has the
following form:
x = [xT

where fk is the location of feature k. While EKF SLAM is
relatively simple and intuitive, it has a number of problems
that originate from the fact that the feature location is uncertain
by a scale factor at the time it is added to the state vector. A
number of extensions and modiﬁcations to EKF SLAM have
been proposed, including inverse depth parameterization, but
the addition of initialization error to the state vector with every
feature remains a problem.
Instead of augmenting the state vector with feature location,
the MSCKF uses the transformation to the camera position at
each frame. The state vector is

where πk is the pose of image frame k. Assuming the ﬁlter
has converged, the location of the camera should be well
known and therefore the error due to state augmentation should
remain small.
A unique measurement model, originally presented in [20],
provides a way to use the feature tracks as measurements.
When a feature track ends, the whole track (including all
the image pixel coordinates) is residualized as a single mea-
surement. This is possible because the time history of the
camera poses are stored in the state vector. Just prior to the
EKF update, the camera poses are used in a least-squares
optimization to produce the location of the feature in the global
frame.
After the optimization, because the feature pose is calculated
from the state vector and the feature pixel coordinates, it is
correlated with errors in the pixel coordinate measurements
and cannot yet be applied as a measurement. This is remedied
by projecting the residual on to the null space of the feature
position. To do this, the residual r is calculated from the
feature pixel location z and the predicted measurement (after
optimization) ˆz as
r ≜z −ˆz .
This residual can be linearly approximated by calculating
two Jacobians: one approximates the residual with respect to
perturbations in the state vector (Hx), and the other approxi-
mates the residual with respect to perturbations in the feature
location (Hf). These, with noise term n, can be written as
r ≃Hx˜x + Hf ˜p + n .
The update is then performed by ﬁrst projecting the residual,
noise, and Jacobian Hx onto the null space of Hf, or
r0 ≜AT(z −ˆz) ≃ATHx˜x + ATn
where A denotes the unitary matrix whose columns form
the basis of the left null space of Hf. Finally, the projected
residual r0 and Jacobian H0 are in the appropriate form
r0 ≃H0˜x + n0 ,
which can be used in the Kalman update.
Several variations and extensions to the MSCKF have been
proposed. The MSCKF 2.0 that includes on-line camera cal-
ibration and a mathematical formulation that ensures that the
state-transition matrix has the correct observability properties
was proposed in [19]. The work in [16] accounts for a rolling-
shutter camera to improve accuracy. There have also been
several slightly different formulations of the state vector. Some
formulations utilize inertial velocity, while others use body-
ﬁxed velocity. The formulation in [17] assumes the ﬁlter
estimates propagate from velocity commands instead of IMU
accelerations, and therefore neglects acceleration bias terms.
[p.4]
Some ﬁlters use continuous-discrete formulations, while others
use a discrete propagation step.
III. DEVELOPMENT
The MSCKF provides an ideal methodology for VIO on
ﬁxed-wing aircraft. We used its measurement model to create
the relative-navigation, front-end ﬁlter because it makes no
assumptions about the distance to observed image features and
has state-of-the-art accuracy and consistency.
This work focuses on the ﬁlter in the relative front end, but
it is important to consider the whole relative navigation archi-
tecture. The front-end ﬁlter enables the back-end localization
that results in several advantages. The back-end optimization
allows the inclusion of other constraints, such as measurements
to known landmarks, multi-vehicle cooperative localization,
and opportunistic GPS measurements. While using state-of-
the-art methods to create a front-end estimator, this work
may sacriﬁce some ﬁlter performance to enable the relative-
navigation, back-end localization.
While this ﬁlter is similar to the previous, relative navi-
gation ﬁlter in [15], there are notable differences. The main
differences are the exclusion of the multirotor-speciﬁc drag
term and the augmentation of the state vector with the pose
of each camera image or πk in (1) that are required for the
MSCKF measurement model.

the process described in [15]. In prior work, the criteria for
performing the reset was determined by heuristics depending
on the distance and yaw angle traveled since the last node.
This work improves these heuristics by making the criteria
for reset dependent on the number of feature tracks that are
maintained with the keyframe associated with the most recent
node frame declaration. If the number of common tracks drops
below a threshold of nine features, a new node is declared and
the reset step takes place. This new heuristic insures that both
sufﬁcient image overlap and an adequate number of features
to provide a keyframe-to-image transformation are maintained,
without resetting sooner than necessary. After the reset step,
all image transformations are removed from the state vector
except for the new keyframe image, which becomes the ﬁrst
image π0. The tracker also reinitializes all feature tracks.
The feature tracker was implemented in C++ with OpenCV
[p.5]
and uses the pyramidal KLT tracker [21]–[23] for ﬁnding
feature correspondences. The feature tracker informs the ﬁlter
when to augment the state with the current camera transform
and when to reset to a new node. It also sends the track to the
ﬁlter as a measurement when the feature goes out of view or
when it can no longer be tracked [16].
The ﬁlter was initially implemented in Python and uses
the Robot Operating System (ROS) for communication with
sensors. The use of Python was sufﬁcient for simulation
study but makes the ﬁlter run inefﬁciently compared other
programing languages. The speed of execution is a limiting
factor at the ﬁlter’s current state of development. Both the
frequency of images and the number of feature track measure-
ments are limited compared to other MSCKF implementations.
Implementation of the ﬁlter in C++ will be required for future
experimental ﬂight tests.
The relative-navigation architecture includes an assump-
tion that the transformations between successive nodes are
independent. This assumption is important for accurate back-
end optimization and for maintaining the consistency of the
front-end ﬁlter. Since velocity and sensor bias terms are not
affected by the relative-reset, the transformations are therefore
correlated. Filter performance results have been accurate and
consistent, however, as long as the ﬁlter velocity remains
observable and velocity errors are small.
IV. RESULTS
The ﬁlter was tested on a high-ﬁdelity simulation of a ﬁxed-
wing aircraft. The simulation was based on the ROS/Gazebo
simulation tools that are distributed with ROSplane [24], [25].
It includes simulated ﬁxed-wing aircraft aerodynamics and
simulated sensors that are representative of those from a small,
unmanned ﬁxed-wing aircraft. To create a realistic scenario
for testing the VIO algorithm, the aircraft was ﬂown over a
detailed cityscape image.
Simulated camera images and IMU measurement were
supplied to the ﬁlter from sensor plug-ins applied to the aircraft
simulation. The simulated IMU was axis-aligned with the
aircraft and used sensor noise and bias walk parameters of
an MPU6050 IMU. Sensor bias was initialized to zero and
allowed to drift throughout the ﬂight. Images were simulated
at 3 Hz and the IMU at 100 Hz. Simulation testing of VIO
ﬁlters often use simulated feature tracks in the place of an
actual feature tracker. This work, however, was able to use the
feature tracker described above to operate on simulated camera
images. Figure 5 shows an example of a simulated image
during ﬂight with the feature tracks overlaid. The simulated
camera produces an image that is 640 by 480 pixels with a
115 degree horizontal ﬁeld of view. The camera was pointed
forward and was angled down 45 degrees from the aircraft’s
longitudinal axis.
The VIO front-end was tested by simulating a ﬁxed-wing
UAS ﬂight. Although it has been tested at a variety of altitudes
and airspeeds, in the results shown, the UAS was ﬂown
approximately 50 m above the ground and at a commanded
airspeed of 11 m/s. The ﬂight path was a hourglass pattern

V. CONCLUSION
This work has demonstrated a feasible method for estimat-
ing the position, velocity, and attitude states of a small, ﬁxed-
wing UAS in GPS-denied environments. It follows the relative
navigation architecture, which has previously been shown to be
effective for multirotor UAS. Using only a camera and IMU
mounted on the aircraft and with no other speciﬁc sensing
requirements, this VIO is able to estimate the aircraft’s motion
and publish transforms appropriate for relative navigation in
GPS-denied environments. The ﬁlter is based on a modiﬁed
MSCKF that also utilizes the relative-reset step and makes no
assumptions about the distance to observed features.
The value of this work can only be evaluated by considering
the whole relative-navigation architecture. This work has fo-
cused on the creation of the relative-navigation, front-end, VIO
estimator. The global back-end uses a graph to represent the
global state, which is necessary for accomplishing a global
mission. The back-end also utilizes graph optimization to
incorporate other constraints, such as loop closures and op-
portunistic, geo-referenced measurements, that are infeasible
in a EKF. The addition of a back-end will greatly expand the
usefulness of this work.
A faster frame rate and more feature tracks may signif-
icantly improve the ﬁlter performance. Due to the reset of
the feature tracks at the declaration of a new node, some
useful information is being discarded. This means that we may
be giving up some performance to gain use of the relative-
navigation back-end optimization and to ensure observability
