GPS-Denied UAV-UGV Relative Positioning
System via Vision-UWB Fusion
Guan Mingyang, Krishna Sagar, and Xu Ning
Advanced Remanufacturing and Technology Centre, A*STAR. Cleantech Loop, #01/01 CleanTech Two, Singapore 637143
{guan mingyang, krishna sagar, xu ning}@artc.a-star.edu.sg
Abstract—Locating drones in GPS-denied environments is a
challenging task, particularly for hardware platforms with lim-
ited computational resources and sensors. A common approach
for positioning a drone within the environment is to cooperate
with an unmanned ground vehicle (UGV) by estimating the
drone’s relative position on the UGV frame. In this paper,
we propose a real-time drone positioning system that uses a
Vision-UWB fusion framework. The fusion system involves two
steps: 1) processing the state estimation using UWB ranging
measurements and vision information separately, and 2) fusing
the estimations from UWB and vision to provide the ﬁnal state
estimation for the drone. To evaluate the accuracy of our method,
we deployed an accurate localization system, named OptiTrack,
as the ground truth system. The experimental results demonstrate
the accuracy and robustness of the developed system, which
overcomes the limitations of individual sensors and enhances
their advantages.
Index Terms—GPS-Denied, UAV localization, Sensor fusion,
Ultra-wide Band
I. INTRODUCTION
Drone localization in GPS-denied scenarios is a critical
challenge in various applications, such as search and res-
cue [1]–[3], infrastructure inspection [4], and ﬁreﬁghting [5].
These tasks often take place in environments where GPS
signals may be weak or unavailable, as shown in Figure I of a
typical UAV-UGV relative position system. Despite the recent
advances in drone localization techniques, reliably locating the
drone in a GPS-denied environment remains an open research
problem.
To achieve accurate and reliable drone localization, one
approach is to rely on self-location information by fusing mul-
tiple onboard sensors on the unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV).
These sensors include IMUs [6], infrared sensors [7], laser
scanning [8], and vision sensors [9]. Among these, visual-
inertial-odometry (VIO) is a notable method that combines
visual information and IMU data to estimate the drone’s
position and orientation [10], [11]. By tracking visual features
in the frames captured by a camera and using the IMU data
to estimate the drone’s motion, VIO can provide accurate and
robust estimates of the drone’s pose even in GPS-denied envi-
ronments. However, these fusion systems are limited by their
range and are susceptible to lighting conditions, occlusion, and
motion blur, which can signiﬁcantly limit their applicability
and increase error accumulation [12], [13].
Another approach to drone localization is to combine Ultra-
wideband (UWB) technology with VIO to reduce the drifts
of the VIO approach [12], [14]. By combining the range

measurements from UWB with VIO, a Kalman ﬁlter or
other probabilistic model can provide a stable estimation of
the drone’s position and orientation. However, these sensing
systems still face challenges due to the limited size, payload,
and power supply of drones that prevent the use of high-end
sensors (e.g., 3D laser scanning) and powerful computational
units [15].
In contrast, some researchers have attempted to estimate
the drone’s location relative to a ground station, such as
an unmanned ground vehicle (UGV) [16]–[18]. For instance,
Tognon et al. [16] proposed a drone tracking system relative to
a moving platform using an observer-based control technique.
Lima et al. [17] presented a ﬁlter-based self-localization sys-
tem for a tethered drone that assumes the drone will form
a catenary shape in static or quasi-static conditions and the
cable-tension force is known. Al-Hourani et al. [18] developed
a 3D location estimation system for a tethered drone using a

To achieve accurate and reliable localization, it is common
to use a combination of sensors, such as UWBs, IMUs, and
cameras, in robotics. UWB is a radio technology that pro-
vides high-accuracy distance measurements between UWB-
equipped devices. IMUs provide information about the orien-
tation and movement of the device to which they are attached.
Cameras capture visual information about the environment and
can be used for object detection and recognition. However,
each sensor has its limitations. For example, UWB is sensitive
to occlusion and reﬂection, IMUs have the issue of drift, and
cameras are sensitive to lighting conditions.
Therefore, fusing these sensors can complement each other
and provide more accurate and stable estimation performance.
There have been many successful fusion approaches to address
the robotic localization problem in various ﬁelds. For example,
UWB with RGB-D camera [19], UWB with IMU [20], RGB-
D camera with UWB and IMU [12], [21], and UWB with
LiDAR [22], [23].
In this paper, we present a contactless drone tracking
system to locate a tethered drone by fusing UWB rangings
and vision information without using cable-tension force. The
developed system has been veriﬁed and evaluated using a high-
accuracy localization system, named OptiTrack. According to
the experimental results, the proposed UWB-Vision fusion
system has achieved an accurate yet reliable drone tracking
performance.

C. Visual object detection
On the other hand, to utilize the information from the
Realsense camera effectively, we applied the YOLOX model
[24] to detect the drone. The YOLO series [25], [26] at-
tempts to balance detection speed and accuracy for practical
applications. However, the YOLO series are still anchor-based
detectors with hand-crafted assigning rules for training. To
implement anchor-free technology for the YOLO series, [24]
proposes a YOLOX model that switches the YOLO detector to
an anchor-free manner and simultaneously applies advanced
detection techniques to further improve the YOLO series.
Moreover, YOLOX won 1st Place in the Streaming Perception
Challenge at CVPR 2021 [24].
As the application scenario involves detecting the drone
based on the image frames captured from the bottom view,
the main challenge for object detection is lighting variation.
To address this issue, we trained our own YOLOX model
for drone detection using the drone images collected in our
environments and an open dataset [27]. YOLOX [24] is an
open-source project1, and the source code can be downloaded

manually in our experiments.

B. Performance evaluation
We conducted experiments to evaluate the performance of
the developed Vision-UWB fusion system. The DJI drone was
ﬂown in a workshop at a height between 1.2m to 2.1m while
the Husky robot followed its movement on the ground. The
fusion system continually estimated the drone’s position with
respect to the Husky robot during the ﬂight. The accuracy of
the system was evaluated using the OptiTrack system as the
ground truth of location.
Figure 3 shows the hardware setup of the system, which
includes a DJI Mavic 2 mounted with a Decawave UWB
tag, a Husky robot mounted with 4 UWB beacons, and
a Realsense camera. The four UWB beacons are mounted
on the Husky ﬁxedly, and their relative positions are well-
calibrated. Note that the UWB units used in our system are
commercial products that generally provide an accuracy of 10-
20 centimeters of range measurement.
The results are shown in Table III-B, which presents the
average errors and standard deviation of the drone’s position
estimation at different ﬂying heights. The developed UWB-
Vision fusion framework provided accurate position estimation
for the drone, with average errors of around 0.2 meters and low
standard deviation (i.e., around 0.1 meters), demonstrating the
accuracy and reliability of the state estimation of the proposed
method.

IV. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we proposed a sensor fusion framework
for the tracking of drones. The experimental results have
demonstrated the accuracy and stability of the developed fu-
sion framework, which combines UWB ranging and YOLOX
object detection. The developed fusion framework has made
a good balance between different information resources and
effectively addressed the limitations of each sensor, resulting
in more accurate and reliable state estimation of the drone.
However, it is worth noting that the tracking results from the
developed fusion framework mostly make a trade-off between
different information resources, and further improvements can
be made to enhance the overall performance of the system. For
example, additional sensors or algorithms can be incorporated
into the framework to provide more accurate and robust state
estimation or the parameters of the current fusion algorithm
can be further optimized. Nevertheless, the proposed fusion
framework provides a promising solution for the tracking of
drones in various scenarios, especially in indoor environments
where GPS signals are not available.
