A B S T R A C T
Autonomous inspection of civil structures using unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) offers a safer and more effi­
cient alternative to manual methods; however, in GPS-denied environments, the absence of global positioning 
makes reliable navigation challenging and requires the use of onboard sensing. Conventional approaches such as 
Visual-Inertial Odometry (VIO) and Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) estimate motion by tracking 
local features, but are susceptible to drift accumulation over time, even though loop closure can partially reduce 
this effect. Recent data-driven methods directly regress UAV poses from images, but their performance depends 
on large, accurately labeled datasets that are difficult to obtain in practice. To address this limitation, this study 
presents an updatable Graphics-Based Digital Twin (GBDT) that provides a globally referenced structural prior 
and generates synthetic image-pose datasets for training Siamese neural pose regressors for absolute pose esti­
mation. The network embeds synthetic GBDT renders and real inspection images into a shared feature space, 
promoting reliance on geometric structure rather than appearance. To reduce the synthetic-to-real gap, geo­
metric cues are emphasized through preprocessing while appearance variations are suppressed, and a geometric 
constraint loss enables indirect supervision on real images without ground-truth poses. By regressing poses 
relative to GBDT template views with known global poses, the method avoids cumulative drift and maintains 
consistent global alignment. Wireless communication between the UAV edge device and a base station enables 
real-time inference. Experiments on a custom-built drone demonstrate stable flight and improved global local­
ization compared to the Intel T265 VIO-SLAM and RTAB-Map. As the GBDT is incrementally updated using 
inspection imagery, localization accuracy improves over successive deployments, enabling increasingly reliable 
autonomous UAV-based monitoring. Code is available at https://github.com/tmatiki2/GBDTpose/tree/main.
1. Introduction
Bridges and other forms of civil infrastructure are vital for economic 
development and public safety, yet they inevitably degrade with age and 
use. To ensure structural reliability and prevent failures, agencies such 
as the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) [1] and the Federal 
Railroad Administration (FRA) [2] mandate recurring inspections of 
highway and railroad bridges. While visual inspection remains the 
dominant practice for identifying surface-level damage, accessing con­
cealed or hazardous regions, particularly the underside of bridge decks, 
remains difficult. These inspections are often slow, expose personnel to 
safety risks, and can miss early warning signs of deterioration [3,4], as 
highlighted by the Mississippi I-40 bridge collapse [5].
Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) equipped with onboard visual 
sensors offer a safer and more scalable alternative to manual inspection; 
however, in GPS-denied areas such as under-deck environments, the 
absence of global positioning makes reliable navigation challenging 
[6–8]. Even when GPS is available, multipath and signal interference

Both feature-based [22–24] and data-driven [35–56] approaches 
have been explored for accurate and drift-resistant absolute pose esti­

UAV images to those in geotagged templates, but depend on 
hand-crafted feature extraction and matching, which can be unreliable 
in landmark-sparse environments. Data-driven approaches address this 
limitation by directly regressing camera poses from images, but require 
large, accurately labeled datasets and can be computationally 
demanding for edge deployment. To overcome these challenges, this 
study leverages an updatable Graphics-Based Digital Twin (GBDT) that

image-pose pairs for training Siamese neural pose regression models.

synthetic-to-real gap. By regressing poses relative to GBDT templates

2. Related works
2.1. Feature-based absolute pose estimation techniques
Several absolute localization techniques have been proposed to 
achieve drift-free and accurate UAV positioning comparable to GPS. In 
general, these methods align known global landmarks with their pixel 
positions in UAV images and estimate the UAV’s pose by triangulating 
relative observations. For example, Conte and Doherty [22] used geo­
tagged reference images and aligned them with UAV imagery to localize 
without GPS, while Niles et al. [23] fused satellite data with LiDAR to 
improve outdoor positioning. However, these pipelines remain depen­
dent on reliable feature matching between UAV and georeferenced im­
ages, which is typically based on hand-crafted computer vision 
techniques and can degrade in environments with sparse visual features 
or repetitive textures [16–19,24]. To improve robustness under 
appearance changes, Kinnari et al. [24] learned season-invariant de­
scriptors that enable matching between satellite imagery and UAV im­
ages, but these approaches remain constrained by the need for a dense 
and well-distributed geotagged image database. Overall, this require­
ment limits their use in GPS-denied inspection environments.
A second class of absolute feature-based pose estimation techniques 
relies on artificial landmarks or align onboard imagery with synthetic 
views generated from virtual environments. For example, Kayhani et al. 
[25,26] introduced a tag-based drift correction method that matches tag 
pixel locations to their 3D coordinates in a building information model 
(BIM) and fuses these measurements with VIO using an extended Kal­
man filter. Because large-scale tag deployment is impractical for civil 
structures, prior work [27] proposed learning natural structural fea­
tures, termed GBDTtags, whose 3D coordinates are obtained from a 
Graphics-Based Digital Twin (GBDT) [28], although performance de­
pends strongly on reliable feature detection. Other studies have aligned 
onboard imagery to template views rendered from simulated environ­
ments; for instance, Lin and He [29] projected structural features from a 
panoramic virtual camera into the real world for absolute pose estima­
tion. While effective in certain cases, these methods require strong 
viewpoint overlap between onboard images and templates, often rely on 
handcrafted viewpoint selection [30], and typically depend on iterative 
closest point (ICP) refinement [31–33], which can be computationally 
expensive and sometimes yields suboptimal solutions. A comprehensive 
review of absolute UAV localization methods and their limitations is 
provided by Couturier et al. [34]. Collectively, these limitations moti­
vate localization frameworks that directly estimate UAV poses without 
relying on geotagged imagery, artificial landmarks, template alignment 
heuristics, or ICP-based refinement.
2.2. Cross-domain neural pose regression techniques
Neural networks have been increasingly adopted for direct camera 
pose regression from images, replacing traditional pipelines based on 
handcrafted feature matching and geometric optimization. These 
models are trained on datasets containing images paired with ground- 
truth poses, enabling them to learn patterns directly from the data and 
predict poses. For example, Kendall et al. [35] fine-tuned the GoogleNet 
convolutional neural network (CNN) [36] for absolute pose regression 
using ground-truth poses derived from Structure-from-Motion (SfM). 
Recognizing the limitations of CNN receptive fields, Shavit et al. [37] 
leveraged a pretrained Transformer architecture for absolute pose esti­
mation, training it across multiple datasets to enhance generalizability 
and later refined the architecture to further enhance pose accuracy [38]. 
While these methods achieved high accuracy on validation images from 
training scenes, they struggled with unseen or diverse environments, 
that are poorly represented by the training dataset limiting their reli­
ability [43].
Some neural pose regression approaches [39–41] utilize template 
images with known absolute poses to improve accuracy. Features are 
extracted from both the template and query images using Siamese neural 
networks [42,43], enabling relative pose estimation through feature 
comparison, with the absolute pose derived from the template's known 
pose. However, their accuracy is highly dependent on the selection of 
appropriate templates, which typically requires handcrafted and 
error-prone vision-based matching techniques. Moreover, similar to 
geotagged absolute pose estimation techniques, these methods require a 
large and well distributed dataset with high-quality ground-truth poses, 
which remains a significant challenge. Recent learning-based methods 
[44–46] have focused on enhancing neural architectures for relative 
pose regression; however, improvements in model design alone cannot 
guarantee generalization to the diverse geometric and textural patterns 
present in civil structures [47]. Critically, this underscores the central 
gap: current neural pose regression models do not lack representational 
power; rather, they require robust and efficient methods to generate and 
curate representative datasets that capture the full variability of the 
target structure. Importantly, because the appearance and geometry of 
civil structures evolve over time, the framework must support iterative 
refinement of pose regression models using new inspection imagery, 
allowing localization performance to improve across successive 
inspections.
2.3. Domain-specific neural pose regression techniques
Recent work [47–50] has explored domain-specific dataset genera­
tion for training neural pose regressors by using NeRFs [51] and 
Gaussian Splatting [52] for novel view synthesis. Although these 
methods suggest that large neural pose regressors are primarily limited 
by dataset bias rather than network capacity, the synthesized training 
poses are often only defined up to an unknown scale, and both NeRFs 
and Gaussian Splatting can yield incomplete or degraded renders under 
sparse viewpoint coverage or weak texture [53–55]. Acharya et al. [56]

In addition, the entire pose regression system must be optimized for 
real-time deployment on UAV platforms to support autonomous in­
spection. Despite these needs, an integrated framework that combines 
data generation, preprocessing, model training, and deployment for 
reliable localization in the target civil structure remains absent in the 
literature.
To address these issues, this study leverages an updatable Graphics- 
Based Digital Twin (GBDT) as a globally referenced structural prior and 
a source of datasets for training and retraining Siamese neural pose re­
gressors throughout the structure’s lifecycle. The GBDT, as described in 
Wang et al. [28], is a computer graphics model that integrates structural 
information such as finite element models and is designed to remain a 
living representation of the as-is structure. It can be updated using UAV 
survey data to reflect photometric and geometric changes, including 
damage, while corresponding updates are applied to the finite element 
model. This update mechanism is demonstrated by prior work through 
INAF [57], which refines initial pose estimates via feature-based 
matching to enable GBDT retexturing for future inspection cycles. 
Although feature-based initialization can fail in repetitive or low-feature 
regions and INAF is not designed for real-time localization, it establishes 
the key capability of incremental GBDT refinement. In turn, the pose 
regression models developed in this study provide stronger initialization 
for digital twin updates, creating a closed-loop digital-twin system in 
which inspection imagery improves both localization and the evolving 
structural model over time. Table 1 positions the proposed Parametric 
GBDTpose relative to prior work, highlighting the associated advantages 
and trade-offs.
2.4. Contributions
This research makes the following contributions: 
1. Formulates an updatable GBDT as a dataset generation hub for 
training and iterative retraining of large neural pose regressors, 
reducing reliance on large georeferenced image datasets.
2. Consolidates state-of-the-art handcrafted and learning-based image 
processing techniques to amplify geometric similarity between the 
GBDT and the physical structure, improving pose regression 
accuracy.
3. Designs a Siamese network that maps both GBDT-generated and real 
images to a shared latent space, incorporating a geometric constraint 
loss to provide indirect supervision on real images.
4. Integrates the neural pose regression model into prior digital-twin 
frameworks for GBDT updating and condition assessment, enabling 
improvements in both autonomous UAV inspection and structural 
health monitoring.
For training the Siamese network, Gazebo [58] and ROS [59] host 
the digital twin and capture images from a wide range of simulated 
viewpoints, ensuring sufficient data diversity for deep learning. INAF is 
then employed to calibrate a limited set of real images of the physical 
structure and provide corresponding target poses, which are subse­
quently used to synthesize additional novel views at arbitrary view­
points, further augmenting the dataset generated by the GBDT. Although 
the GBDT already encodes strong geometric information from the BIM, 
geometric cues are amplified through targeted image preprocessing, 
while appearance differences are suppressed to minimize the 
synthetic-to-real domain gap. A geometric constraint loss enables indi­
rect supervision on real images without ground-truth poses, improving 
generalization across both simulated and real domains. During UAV 
navigation, the pose is inferred by aggregating relative estimates across 
all templates via Siamese latent vector comparisons, removing reliance 
on selecting a single best template. The framework, is validated 
numerically in the digital environment, benchmarked against 
state-of-the-art SLAM and neural pose regression methods, and

3. Proposed parametric GBDTpose framework overview
As illustrated in Fig. 1, the Parametric GBDTpose framework for 
adaptive neural pose regression consists of five key components. The 
process begins with the design of a graphics-based digital twin (GBDT), 
which is used to generate representative image-pose datasets for su­
pervised training. Because the GBDT-generated images exhibit domain 
shift relative to real inspection imagery, and because the physical 
environment is dynamic with new objects appearing during each UAV 
survey, a data preprocessing block is introduced to perform domain 
adaptation by isolating the reference GBDT structure within the images. 
Following preprocessing, a Siamese neural pose regression network is 
trained to map both GBDT-generated and real images into a shared 
latent space, enabled by a geometric constraint loss that enforces pose 
consistency. After UAV inspections, the regressed poses are refined and 
used to update the GBDT model for subsequent inspection cycles, pro­
gressively improving its fidelity to the as-is structure and, in turn, 
enhancing pose regression accuracy over the structure’s service life.
In formulating the proposed framework, three key assumptions are 
made. First, the building information model used to initialize the GBDT 
is assumed to be geometrically accurate. Given the current level of so­
phistication in civil construction, BIMs generally provide high-fidelity 
geometric representations of as-is structures [60–62]. Although minor 
dimensional deviations may exist, these are typically small and sparsely 
distributed, and therefore do not substantially degrade pose regression 
accuracy. Moreover, because the GBDT is designed to be both geomet­
rically and photometrically updatable, it can be progressively refined 
within the proposed framework to better match the as-is structure over 
time. For older infrastructure where a BIM is unavailable, time-of-flight 
sensing technologies such as LiDAR can be employed to reconstruct the 
geometry and develop a sufficiently accurate GBDT.
Second, the framework assumes the availability of a limited set of 
real images of the physical structure. These images are necessary not 
only to train the data preprocessing block for effective domain adapta­
tion, but also to train the Siamese neural pose regressor through the 
geometric constraint loss, which enforces consistency between real and 
GBDT-generated images in a shared latent space. Although an initial 
UAV survey may be conducted to collect such imagery, ground-truth 
pose annotations are not required, because the geometric constraint 
loss enables indirect supervision of the real images without known pose 
labels.
Lastly, the framework assumes that the structure contains sufficient 
visual features to support learning-based pose regression. Deep neural 
networks rely on discernible patterns to map image inputs to pose out­
puts [35,37,39]; completely textureless or uniform surfaces would 
provide insufficient cues. Fortunately, civil structures typically contain 
abundant geometric features such as edges, corners, curves, and in­
tersections that are informative for pose estimation. Although repetitive 
patterns may introduce ambiguities, the use of wide-angle or fisheye 
cameras provides broader spatial context that mitigates this issue. In 
addition, the framework naturally accommodates multiple fisheye 
cameras, further reducing ambiguities caused by repetitive textures. The 
following sections detail the contributions of each component of the 
framework.

scalable neural pose regressors. Refs. [28,63–66,68] provide detailed 
methodologies for developing high-fidelity GBDT models across various 
applications, including UAV trajectory planning and damage detection. 
Typically, this involves creating realistic texture and displacement maps 
from finite element simulations and field-acquired images, which are 
then applied to corresponding components in the BIM of the physical 
structure within Blender [67] as illustrated in Fig. 2(a). Although initial 
GBDT design can be time-intensive, these models enable critical

5. Data preprocessing
Preprocessing synthetic images is essential to bridging the gap be­
tween the GBDT’s appearance and its physical counterpart. Several data 
preprocessing techniques were applied in this research, including the 
isolation of the physical twin in an image. Without this isolation oper­
ation, the performance of trained networks will degrade when encoun­
tering objects in the image not previously seen during training (e.g. 
tables, cars, etc.). Additionally, separating the physical structure from 
background elements enhances the apparent similarity between the

[p.8]
GBDT and the physical twin, facilitating the neural network's ability to 
learn mappings from images to poses across both domains. To achieve 
this isolation, the bounding box annotations previously generated with 
the CVAT tool was used, while annotations for GBDT and INAF-enabled 
synthesized images were automated due to the known camera poses. 
This process yielded a comprehensive dataset comprising approximately 
100 annotated real images and 2500 synthetic images. The prepared 
dataset was then used to fine-tune the pretrained YOLOv8 model [74], 
enabling precise bounding box detection of the physical twin in 
observed images. Fig. 6(a, b) illustrates examples of bounding box de­
tections in physical twin images. Notably, the isolation process for GBDT 
images does not require YOLOv8 because camera poses are pre­
determined, allowing direct computation of bounding box coordinates.
The bounding boxes were utilized to isolate the physical twin by 
masking out irrelevant regions in the images. These transformations 
were applied consistently to both synthetic and real images during 
training, prior to their conversion into PyTorch tensors (normalized to 
range between 0–1). While alternative object detection models (such as 
Faster R-CNN [75]) could perform this task, YOLOv8 was chosen due to 
its rapid detection speed and low memory footprint. Selecting any 
suitable model with minimal memory overhead is critical, as the isola­
tion operation must also be applied during pose estimation with the 
Siamese network in both training and deployment stages. To further 
enhance robustness, photometric transformations were applied to the 
grayscale input images. These transformations help mitigate the effects 
of motion blur (caused by UAV movement) and noise while preserving 
the original poses of the images, ensuring no adjustments to the 
ground-truth or target poses are necessary. Template images underwent 
the isolation operation but were not subjected to photometric trans­
formations. Grayscale images were selected to reduce the input volume 
size and mitigate textural inconsistencies between the GBDT and phys­
ical twin images. Fig. 7(a, b) showcases examples of the transformed 
images used for training the Siamese network. While bounding boxes 
may not provide a perfect solution for isolating the structure, the 
resulting images predominantly focus on the target structure, facilitating 
the learning process. The next section details the loss functions and 
training procedures.
6. Training procedure and loss functions
Learning through comparison has proven to be a highly effective 
approach for self-supervising large neural networks, as demonstrated in 
[76–78]. This methodology underpins contrastive learning, where the 
features of input queries are adjusted to align closely with their positive 
counterparts (target examples) while diverging from non-targets 
(negative examples). In this context, a neural network maps query im­
ages and templates into a shared latent space. To facilitate this process, 
the Triplet Contrastive Loss function [79] is employed to tune the 
network weights, achieving the objectives of contrastive learning. This 
type of network, which processes both template and query inputs to 
enable learning through comparison, is referred to as a Siamese network 
[42,43]. The Siamese network architecture is adopted to introduce 
additional constraints that enhance learning, as previously shown 
effective in related studies [39–41]. By comparing a query image to 
multiple templates and predicting relative poses for each template, the 
network learns a broader and more robust mapping, rather than relying 
on a single absolute pose prediction from the query image. Fig. 8 illus­
trates the Siamese network architecture developed for this research, 
including its Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) pose prediction heads.
This research investigated the use of ResNet101 [80] and ViT_b_16 
[81] as the backbone for the Siamese network; however, any suitable 
neural encoder can be utilized. During each training epoch, pre­
processed template image tensors (i.e., ICk) and observed image tensors 
(i.e., ICi) are passed through the Siamese network to generate latent 
vectors for each input (i.e. FCk and FCi respectively as shown in Fig. 8).

observed image, producing a resultant vector (i.e., FCi

7.1. Verification of neural pose regression model in the digital 
environment
The trained neural pose regression models were first evaluated 
numerically in the simulation environment, where ground-truth poses 
are available. This verification step is essential, as suboptimal perfor­
mance within the controlled GBDT domain would indicate limited po­
tential for reliable deployment in the physical environment. In addition, 
the simulation-based evaluation enables comparison and benchmarking 
of pose estimation accuracy across different neural backbones used 
within the Siamese architecture. Fig. 11(a–d) presents pose estimation 
results of the trained parametric models, using the ResNet-101 and ViT 
backbones, within the SSTL and KLB Smart bridge GBDT domains. The 
fisheye camera in Gazebo was randomly positioned and oriented, with 
ground-truth (GT) poses directly obtained using ROS. For these experi­
ments, 100 template images were used for the SSTL Bridge and 500 for 
the KLB Smart bridge. The larger number of templates for the KLB Smart 
bridge reflects its significantly greater size compared to the SSTL bridge. 
As shown in Fig. 11, both models (ResNet101 and ViT backbones) 
achieved highly accurate pose predictions within the GBDT domain, 
demonstrating the Siamese network’s capability to approximate pose 
estimations effectively from input images. The median absolute error 
(MAE) is presented in Fig. 11(a, b). Although the ResNet101 backbone 
provided superior accuracy, the ViT model offers enhanced scalability 
by enabling the addition of neurons, making it well-suited for future 
applications requiring more refined approximations. Additionally, the 
performance of the models tends to decline as the size of the GBDT in­
creases, which is attributed to the larger space of possible images that 
must be mapped to unique poses.
Fig. 12(a, b) presents a comparative analysis using the parametric 
GBDTpose with ResNet101-backbone against GBDTpose2 [30], and 
RTAB-Map [83], a feature-based SLAM algorithm, within the SSTL 
bridge simulated environment. The first test scenario involved lateral 
camera movement with no rotational motion, whereas the second 
scenario introduced random rotations between −10◦and +10◦, com­
bined with vertical perturbations ranging from −50 cm to +50 cm. 
These ranges of rotations and translations were chosen to maintain 
tracking of the camera by the SLAM algorithm. In the first test, all three 
estimators successfully tracked the camera's pose with good accuracy. 
However, under the rotational and positional perturbations introduced 
in the second test, RTAB-Map exhibited significant drift, while 
GBDTpose2 produced erratic pose estimates due to its inability to reli­
ably select the virtual camera with viewpoint overlap. Tables 2 and 3
provide a quantitative comparison of the three pose estimators under 
pure lateral translation and combined translation with rotation sce­
narios, respectively. In addition to the median absolute error (MAE), the 
root mean squared error (RMSE) is also computed to compare the pose 
regressors. Notably, the Parametric GBDTpose model outperforms the 
feature-based pose estimators in accuracy, and also provides a smooth 
and drift-free pose estimate in comparison to RTAB-Map.

prediction performance. In addition, optimizing the size and spatial 
distribution of template images offers a promising direction for further 
improvement. These factors currently limit the effectiveness of the 
Parametric GBDTpose framework and warrant further investigation. 
Overall, the indoor and outdoor results demonstrate the robustness and 
adaptability of the Parametric GBDTpose framework for UAV localiza­
tion around the target civil structure, particularly in comparison to the 
T265 VIO-SLAM system and the Reloc3r neural pose regression model. 
The following section presents ablation studies to further evaluate the 
contributions of the framework’s individual components.
7.3. Ablation study using KLB smart bridge environment
To systematically evaluate the contribution of each component in the 
Parametric GBDTpose framework, several model variants were trained 
for the KLB bridge case study. These components include the GBDT- 
generated dataset, the YOLOv8-based isolation block, the INAF- 
enabled novel view synthesis module, and the geometric constraint 
loss. To assess the effect of isolating the physical structure within the 
images, two models were trained using only GBDT-generated images, 
with and without the isolation block. To evaluate the benefit of INAF- 
enabled novel view synthesis, an additional model was trained using 
isolated images generated solely through INAF and compared against 
the GBDT-based models with isolation. Finally, to examine the com­
bined contribution of all components, the complete Parametric 
GBDTpose model was trained using both GBDT- and INAF-generated 
images together with the geometric constraint loss, which provides in­
direct supervision for unlabeled real images without requiring ground- 
truth pose estimates. The results of these ablation studies are pre­
sented in Fig. 17.

8. Integration of parametric GBDTpose with Intel T265 camera 
and deployment on UAV platform
The primary goal of the parametric GBDTpose model is to enable 
UAV navigation for civil infrastructure inspection in GPS-denied envi­
ronments. However, running large parametric regressors on edge de­
vices such as Jetson Nano, which is suitable for mounting on a UAV, 
poses a challenge due to limited memory capacity. To address this 
limitation, a bidirectional wireless communication protocol is estab­
lished between the edge device on the UAV and a base-station PC using 
ROS (see [85]). In this setup, images from the UAV's onboard camera are 
wirelessly transmitted to the base station (through the edge device), 
where pose estimation is performed. The resulting poses are then sent 
back to the edge device on the UAV, which relays them to the UAV’s 
flight controller. This approach allows for the deployment of large 
models that would otherwise be impractical to run directly on the edge 
device. Fig. 18 illustrates the communication pipeline, detailing the 
transmission of images from the UAV to the base station and the return 
transmission of pose data to the flight controller.
Although this setup introduces latency during pose estimation due to 
the transmission of images to the base station, the authors observed that 
its impact is minimal for a camera operating at a frame rate of 30 Hz, 
particularly when supported by reliable internet connectivity. Addi­
tionally, using an Nvidia GeForce RTX 2080 Ti GPU at the base station 
ensures that pose computations are performed nearly in real time, 
matching the rate at which images are received. Videos showcasing the 
experiments conducted are available at the GitHub repository [81]. In 
all tests, the transmitted poses enabled autonomous navigation of the 
DIY drone in Altitude-Hold, Position-Hold, and Guided flight modes, 
using the Ardupilot flight controller, Mission Planner, and MAVROS.
9. Research limitations and potential applications
Although the Parametric GBDTpose model with ResNet101 back­
bone has been successfully validated in the laboratory using a prototype 
bridge and outdoors using the KLB Smart bridge, scaling this approach to 
very large structures presents significant challenges. These challenges 
arise from the expanded range of possible images that must be mapped 
to distinct poses, as well as the increased preprocessing and training 
times required to develop a reliable pose regressor. While increasing the 
neural network's size could address the issue of mapping complexity, it 
would also introduce substantial computational overhead. A more 
practical solution involves dividing the physical twin into smaller re­
gions and training a dedicated pose regressor for each region. During the 
preprocessing phase, a YOLOv8 model can be trained for region classi­
fication, enabling the system to identify the region depicted in each 
image. Once identified, the pose regressor corresponding to that region 
can be utilized for localization. A key advantage of this scheme is its 
potential for decentralized computation, where multiple base-station 
PCs can independently run distinct models trained for localizing the 
UAV within specific regions of the physical twin. While not implemented 
in this research due to computational demands, the decentralized 
approach shows promise for UAV-based inspection of large-scale infra­
structure and will be explored in future work. By leveraging multiple 
instances of Parametric GBDTpose models within a decentralized 
framework, this method can significantly enhance scalability and 
robustness in such large-scale applications.
Aside from model and architectural optimization, another limitation 
of the proposed framework is its reliance on wireless internet connec­
tivity between the base station and the UAV. In the event of a network 
interruption, the UAV can be programmed to temporarily hold its po­
sition. However, if the connection is not restored within a short period, 
control of the UAV may be compromised. One potential solution is to 
equip both the UAV and the base station with independent cellular 
network access and establish communication through a virtual private 
network (VPN). In addition, an extended Kalman filter could be imple­
mented to fuse the real-time pose estimates from the T265 VIO-SLAM 
system with those produced by the Parametric GBDTpose framework,

10. Conclusion
Automated visual inspection is increasingly essential for ensuring the 
safety and longevity of civil infrastructure, as it enables more frequent, 
consistent, and objective assessments. Although UAVs are well suited for 
inspection tasks, reliable navigation in GPS-denied environments re­
mains challenging. Existing localization methods in GPS-denied envi­
ronments, such as VIO and SLAM, rely on tracking local features and are 
therefore susceptible to drift in repetitive or texture-poor scenes. Neural 
pose regression addresses this by learning global representations for 
direct pose estimation, but requires large, accurately labeled datasets. 
To overcome this limitation, this study proposes an updatable Graphics- 
Based Digital Twin (GBDT) framework that provides a globally refer­
enced structural prior and generates synthetic image-pose data for 
training neural pose regressors capable of absolute, drift-free localiza­
tion. Compared to the Intel T265 VIO-SLAM and Reloc3r, the proposed 
Parametric GBDTpose framework achieves robust, drift-free pose esti­
mates at the correct metric scale. Moreover, as the digital twin is pro­
gressively refined using inspection imagery, localization performance 
improves over time, enabling more accurate, reliable, and scalable 
autonomous UAV inspections throughout the structure’s service life.
