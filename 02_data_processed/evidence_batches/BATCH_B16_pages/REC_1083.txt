Abstract
Reliable navigation is critical for UAVs operating in GNSS-denied environments, where
conventional Inertial Navigation System/Global Navigation Satellite System (INS/GNSS)
integrated navigation struggles to meet the requirements of high-reliability and long-
endurance missions. As a passive and autonomous approach, terrain-aided navigation
(TAN) offers strong concealment and a high degree of autonomy. However, most existing
TAN methods rely on handcrafted features, which limit their ability to fully exploit multi-
level terrain information, while sensitivity to elevation noise and attitude variations further
degrades matching accuracy and robustness. To address these issues, this paper proposes a
GNSS-denied UAV terrain matching navigation method based on an autoencoder network
with contrastive learning. A Global–Local Dual-branch Feature Extraction Network (GL-
DualNet) is designed to combine the local detail extraction capability of CNNs with the
global dependency modeling ability of the Swin Transformer, enabling effective multi-scale
terrain representation. In addition, an Autoencoder Contrastive Learning Model (ACLM)
is developed to jointly optimize reconstruction and contrastive objectives, enabling unsu-
pervised learning of terrain features with improved discriminability and robustness against
noise and rotational disturbances. Experiments on a public terrain dataset show that the
proposed method outperforms conventional terrain matching approaches under different
noise levels, rotational disturbances, and search ranges, demonstrating its effectiveness and
robustness for UAV navigation in complex GNSS-denied environments.

1. Introduction
Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have become indispensable in modern applications
such as reconnaissance, infrastructure inspection, disaster response, and logistics, where
autonomous flight and precise task execution are critical. The performance and safety of
these platforms rely heavily on accurate navigation and positioning systems. However,
UAVs often operate in challenging environments, such as mountainous regions, urban
canyons, or areas under electronic warfare, where GNSS signals may be unavailable or
severely degraded, posing significant challenges to mission continuity [1].
In practice, the Strapdown Inertial Navigation System/Global Navigation Satellite
System (SINS/GNSS) integration remains the primary navigation solution for UAVs, effec-
tively mitigating inertial drift and providing high accuracy. Nevertheless, in GNSS-denied
scenarios, the performance of SINS/GNSS deteriorates, making long-term, high-precision
navigation for UAVs difficult to sustain [2–9]. To overcome these limitations, alternative
navigation methods, such as Terrain-aided navigation, have been developed to serve as
autonomous backup or supplementary systems, enhancing the reliability and robustness of
UAV navigation in all-time, all-area operations [10–12].
TAN enables UAVs to maintain accurate positioning by matching real-time terrain
elevation measurements with pre-stored digital elevation maps (DEM). This capability
is particularly critical for UAVs operating in GNSS-denied or contested environments,
supporting autonomous, covert, and continuous missions in complex terrains [13–15].
However, conventional terrain-matching algorithms face significant challenges when ap-
plied to UAV platforms. Classic methods such as Terrain Contour Matching (TERCOM)
determine position by minimizing the difference between measured and reference terrain
profiles, providing robustness but showing sensitivity to terrain rotation [16,17]. Iterative
Closest Contour Point (ICCP) improves accuracy through rigid transformations but remains
vulnerable to large initial errors [18]. Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) maximizes
observation likelihood, but its performance degrades under strong interference [19,20]. Re-
cursive Bayesian approaches, including Sandia Inertial Terrain-Aided Navigation (SITAN)
with Extended Kalman Filter and the Unscented Kalman Filter, improve navigation by
correcting INS errors and avoiding linearization, yet they are still prone to divergence or
lack robustness in complex noise conditions [21–24]. Particle Filters handle nonlinearities
effectively, but their high computational cost limits real-time UAV operation [25–27].
Most of these approaches rely on handcrafted features or direct elevation matching,
which fail to fully exploit the high-dimensional structural information in terrain data,
making it difficult to balance accuracy, robustness, and real-time performance for UAV
missions. To address these limitations, deep learning-based TAN methods have been
introduced. For example, Fan’s self-distillation framework and Yang’s TMP-Net leverage
unsupervised or self-supervised learning to extract terrain features and perform matching-
based localization [28,29]. Despite their promise, existing deep-learning methods remain
insufficiently optimized for UAV applications, as feature extraction and robustness under
noise, rotational disturbances, and varying terrain conditions are still limited.
As an unsupervised learning approach, an autoencoder can effectively extract repre-
sentative features from raw data. It is an unsupervised learning model that automatically
learns data features and performs dimensionality reduction by mapping input data to a
compressed latent representation through an encoder and then reconstructing the original

input via a decoder. Currently, enhanced architectures such as the sparse autoencoder,
denoising autoencoder, and variational autoencoder have been widely studied to improve
the robustness and diversity of feature extraction [30–32]. For instance, the variational
autoencoder learns a probability distribution in latent space, strengthening generative
capability [33]; the denoising autoencoder injects noise into the input and trains the model
to recover the original data, thereby improving robustness to noise [34].
Meanwhile, contrastive learning has achieved significant progress. By pulling together
similar samples and pushing apart dissimilar ones, it markedly improves discriminability
in the feature space [35–38]. Classic methods such as MoCo and SimCLR substantially
improve training efficiency and feature consistency by combining a momentum encoder
and a queue mechanism with data augmentation. MoCo enhances training by constructing
a large-scale dictionary of negative samples, whereas SimCLR systematically demonstrates
the importance of augmentation combinations and a nonlinear projection head for visual
representation learning. Later methods, including BYOL, SwAV, and Barlow Twins, further
introduce innovations in negative-free learning, online clustering, and reducing feature
redundancy [39–43].
The integration of autoencoders with contrastive learning has also been investigated
to combine the strengths of both paradigms. For example, Cao proposed ContrastNet,
an unsupervised framework for remote sensing image classification that uses different
autoencoder variants to generate multiple feature views of the same input and then ap-
plies contrastive learning to enhance feature discriminability [44]. Inspired by this ap-
proach, the present work extends the autoencoder–contrastive learning paradigm to UAV
terrain-aided navigation.
These approaches offer new insights for feature extraction in UAV terrain-aided
navigation under GNSS-denied and challenging environments. Accordingly, this paper
integrates an autoencoder and contrastive learning for terrain matching, aiming to extract
effective features and enhance robustness. The goal is to build a system that achieves high
accuracy, strong robustness, and good terrain adaptability. The main contributions are
as follows:
1.
GL-DualNet is proposed, integrating CNN-based local detail capture with the Swin
Transformer’s ability to model global dependencies. This design enables compre-
hensive extraction and fusion of multi-level terrain features. The local branch stacks
Local Residual Spatial Attention (LRSA) modules, combining CNNs with adaptive
spatial attention to focus on fine-grained texture details. The global branch is built
on Swin Transformer Blocks (STB), which establish long-range dependencies via
window partitioning, shifted windows, and self-attention, thereby capturing terrain
macro-structure and trends accurately.
2.
An Autoencoder–Contrastive Learning Model (ACLM) is proposed. By jointly opti-
mizing reconstruction and contrastive losses, ACLM enables self-supervised learning
of terrain features under unsupervised conditions and strengthens invariance to noise
and rotational interference. The autoencoder is trained end-to-end, using the raw
DEM as the reconstruction target to perform terrain feature extraction and recon-
struction, forcing the encoder to learn interference-invariant representations while
preserving key terrain structural information. The contrastive learning task takes the
dual-branch features extracted by the encoder as samples and trains on constructed
positive and negative pairs, improving robustness to interference and enhancing the
discriminability of multi-scale features. To the best of our knowledge, this work is
the first to jointly introduce an autoencoder and contrastive learning into a feature-
learning framework for terrain-matching navigation.

2. Methodology
This section begins with an overview of the terrain-matching navigation system
architecture and then introduces the GL-DualNet. By fusing local detail with global con-
tour cues, GL-DualNet enables effective extraction of multi-scale terrain information. To
further strengthen feature representation, an Autoencoder–Contrastive Learning Model
(ACLM) is proposed to couple autoencoding with contrastive learning, improving fea-
ture discriminability and robustness to interference. Finally, the complete localization
pipeline is described, spanning feature extraction, similarity matching, and multi-source
information fusion.
2.1. Overview
Designed for unmanned platform operating in GNSS-denied environments, the pro-
posed terrain matching navigation method consists of three main components: the terrain
matching navigation unit, the inertial navigation unit, and the data fusion unit. The overall
system framework is shown in Figure 1. To achieve high-precision terrain matching navi-
gation, the GL-DualNet is introduced as the feature extractor for the terrain matching unit.
Prior to the UAV mission, this unit performs offline feature extraction on the pre-stored
DEM, constructing a terrain feature database. During the mission, the onboard encoder ex-
tracts high-dimensional multi-scale features from real-time terrain measurements obtained
by the UAV’s altimeter, including laser altimeters and Synthetic Aperture Radar Altimeters.
These features are then combined with the position estimate from the INS and rapidly
matched within a confined region of the feature database using cosine similarity, yielding
the final matching result. Subsequently, a complementary filter performs weighted fusion
of the position information from both the INS and terrain matching subsystems, producing
an optimal position estimate and enabling robust, anti-interference autonomous navigation.
As evident from the above description, terrain matching navigation and positioning are
primarily achieved through feature extraction and similarity calculation based on terrain
data. Therefore, the quality of terrain features extracted by the GL-DualNet is crucial to
achieving accurate localization.
To enhance the feature quality of the feature extraction network, a joint training strat-
egy combining autoencoder reconstruction and contrastive learning is proposed. Recon-
struction constraints improve the network’s capacity to represent original terrain structures,
while contrastive learning strengthens feature invariance and discriminability against
noise and rotation disturbances, thereby improving matching accuracy and robustness
under complex conditions. In the following subsections, the feature extraction network
architecture, training strategy, and matching process are detailed.

2.2. GL-DualNet: Global-Local Dual-Branch Feature Extraction Network
To enable feature extraction from terrain data, this paper introduces the principles of
CNN and Swin-Transformer, designing the feature extraction network with two parallel
branches: one for global features and one for local features. The network structure is
shown in Figure 2. Both branches take an n × n DEM patch as input. After multi-layer
feature extraction and fusion, the network outputs a high-dimensional terrain feature that
integrates both global and local information.
Figure 2. Global–local dual-branch feature extraction network.
2.2.1. Local Feature Extraction Branch
The local feature extraction branch consists of multiple cascaded Local Residual Spatial
Attention (LRSA) modules. Each LRSA module includes a residual connection layer and
an adaptive spatial attention layer. The residual connection layer is composed of two
sequential residual blocks, with each block consisting of three layers: the first and third
layers are 3 × 3 convolutional layers with a stride of 1 and padding of 1, while the second
layer is a ReLU activation layer. This design, which employs convolution kernels with a
small receptive field, is beneficial for extracting fine-grained texture features within the
https://doi.org/10.3390/drones10050339
[p.6]

region. Each residual block uses a skip connection that adds the input directly to the output,
and the residual connection layer ultimately adds its output to the original input, effectively
mitigating the gradient vanishing problem during deep network training.
Building upon this, an adaptive spatial attention layer is introduced to enhance the
response to key local regions. This layer dynamically generates spatial weight distributions
based on feature content: the output of the residual connection layer is passed through
a 3 × 3 convolutional layer with stride 1 and padding 1, adjusting the number of feature
channels to 9. The features are then globally pooled using average pooling. The resulting
feature vector is passed through two 1 × 1 convolutional layers with a ReLU activation
function between them, generating 3 × 3 dynamic convolution kernel parameters. This
convolution kernel is applied directly to the residual connection layer output, and through
grouped convolution and Sigmoid normalization, a spatial attention weight matrix is
obtained. The weight matrix is then element-wise multiplied with the original feature
map, achieving adaptive enhancement of key local features, thereby further improving the
discriminability and expressive power of the local features. In the network’s hierarchical
structure, the output of each LRSA layer serves as input to the next LRSA module and the
Swin-Transformer Block (STB) in the global feature extraction branch, enabling the fusion
of local and global information.
2.2.2. Global Feature Extraction Branch
The global feature extraction branch consists of multiple cascaded Swin-Transformer
Blocks (STBs). As input features pass through each STB, they are first divided into non-
overlapping local windows of fixed size, where self-attention is computed within each
window. The window shifting mechanism then shifts and reorganizes the windows, and
attention is computed again within the newly formed windows, allowing for interaction of
terrain information across different windows. This structure significantly reduces computa-
tional complexity while effectively modeling global dependencies across regions, enabling
efficient extraction of macro-level global terrain features. Within the branch, the output of
each STB serves as input to the next STB.
The terrain features output by the network are obtained by merging the outputs of
the local feature extraction branch and the global feature extraction branch, as expressed
mathematically by:
F(x) = FL(x) + FG(x)
(1)
where F(x), FG(x), and FL(x) denote the local branch features, global branch features, and
fused features, respectively.
2.3. ACLM: Autoencoder Contrastive Learning Module
To train GL-DualNet without labels and improve its robustness to noise and rotational
offsets, this paper proposes an ACLM as a self-supervised training framework. ACLM uses
GL-DualNet as the encoder and forms paired original and perturbed samples via noise
and rotation augmentation. A reconstruction constraint is incorporated to preserve key
terrain structure, together with contrastive learning: for each branch, the original feature
and its augmented feature form a positive pair, whereas the original global feature and
the original local feature form a negative pair, thereby improving interference-consistent
representations and multi-scale discriminability simultaneously. After joint optimization of
the reconstruction and contrastive losses, more representative and robust terrain features
are obtained to support subsequent matching-based localization.

2.3.1. Data Augmentation
In practical terrain-matching navigation, dynamic changes in the natural environment,
vehicle motion states, and sensor performance inevitably introduce interference errors
during measurement. Such interference can be broadly categorized into two types. One
is noise in the measured elevation values, which is typically modeled as a Gaussian
distribution with zero mean and variance δ. The other is a rotational offset between the real-
time terrain data and the reference DEM. These errors disrupt terrain-feature consistency
and degrade the reliability and accuracy of matching algorithms; therefore, the model must
be strengthened to cope with such disturbances.
A data-augmentation module is incorporated during training. By generating diverse
augmented samples to emulate real disturbances, the model is encouraged to learn ro-
bust feature representations that are invariant to noise and rotation, thereby improving
generalization and practical performance in complex environments. The procedure is as
follows. First, the original terrain data are augmented in parallel by adding elevation
errors that follow a zero-mean Gaussian distribution with variance δhi ∼N (0, δ), and by
applying random-angle rotations to simulate measurement noise and geometric deviations
encountered in practice.Tnoise denotes the operation of adding zero-mean Gaussian noise
with standard deviation δ, and Trotate denotes the random rotation operation.
After augmentation, the data are normalized and interpolated to meet the model input
format. An augmented sample Xaug that contains both noise and rotational offsets is then
generated, and the original sample X is paired with Xaug. The paired samples are fed into
the subsequent ACLM for joint training, providing data support for the feature extraction
network to learn robust representations.
2.3.2. Training Architecture
To train GL-DualNet, this paper draws on the training paradigm of the autoencoder
and constructs an autoencoder-based feature learning framework, using GL-DualNet as
the encoder to extract multi-scale terrain feature representations. The overall architecture,
shown in Figure 3, consists of three stages: data augmentation, feature encoding, and
feature decoding.

zero-mean Gaussian noise and applying random rotations, the corresponding augmented
DEM Xaug is generated from the original DEM X. X and Xaug form paired training samples
for reconstruction learning, providing the data basis for the model to learn robust features.

Using two parallel branches, the encoder extracts global and local features for each
input sample. During training, two types of feature pairs are constructed for contrastive
learning. Positive pairs are formed by features extracted from X and Xaug through the
same branch, whereas negative pairs are formed by the global and local features extracted
from the original DEM. Positive pairs encourage feature consistency under disturbances,
and negative pairs enhance discriminability between multi-scale features.
The decoder adopts a multi-stage upsampling structure to progressively reconstruct
terrain details. As shown in Figure 4, the input features are first mapped to 32 channels
by a 3 × 3 convolution, followed by batch normalization and ReLU activation, and then
upsampled to a resolution of 200 × 200. A subsequent 3 × 3 convolution expands the
features to 64 channels, and after the same processing, they are upsampled to 400 × 400.
The features are then reduced back to 32 channels and upsampled to 800 × 800. Finally,
a 3 × 3 convolution compresses the features to a single channel, and a Tanh activation
followed by downsampling restores the original input size of 100 × 100, yielding the
reconstructed DEM.

2.3.3. Training Loss
During training, the network is learned by jointly optimizing a reconstruction task
and a contrastive learning task.
In the reconstruction task, an end-to-end autoencoder is trained using the original
DEM X and the augmented DEM Xaug as input samples. Specifically, the model uses X as
the reconstruction target and performs feature extraction and reconstruction for both the
original DEM and its augmented version. This training strategy forces the encoder to learn
disturbance-invariant representations while preserving key terrain structural information,
thereby improving generalization and feature robustness in real-world environments.
The contrastive learning task uses the dual-branch features extracted by the encoder
as samples and is trained by constructing positive and negative pairs. Specifically, the
fused features output by the encoder for the original DEM and the augmented DEM are
treated as positive pairs to enhance robustness to disturbances. Meanwhile, the global
features and local features extracted from the original DEM are treated as negative pairs
to improve discriminability across scales and increase the separability of features from
different branches. With this contrastive training scheme, the learned representations
form a more structured clustering distribution in the embedding space, where similar
features are pulled together and dissimilar ones are pushed apart, thereby improving
matching accuracy.

2.4. Navigation and Positioning Process for UAV
The proposed method achieves accurate localization for UAVs by integrating an
inertial navigation system with terrain feature matching. The overall workflow is shown in
Figure 1, and the implementation steps are summarized as follows.
Step 1: Offline terrain feature database construction
Before the mission, the preloaded DEM is interpolated to the network input resolution
and partitioned into grid cells. Each cell is encoded by the GL-DualNet to obtain a high-
dimensional feature vector, which is stored with its geographic coordinates to form a
structured database for real-time matching during UAV flight.
Step 2: Initial search region determination
After the mission starts, the INS position estimate PINS is used as the center to define
the matching search region based on INS error characteristics and preset parameters. This
constraint avoids global exhaustive search and improves matching efficiency.

3. Experiments
To systematically validate the proposed method, this chapter presents a series of exper-
iments on the public ASTER GDEM V3 terrain dataset. Comparisons with classical terrain
matching algorithms and retrieval methods demonstrate the advantages of the proposed
approach. To clarify the contribution of each component, ablation studies investigate the
effects of the feature extraction branches, data augmentation, and training strategy. The
method is then quantitatively evaluated under varying elevation noise levels, rotational
perturbations, and search ranges. Finally, navigation and positioning experiments confirm
its feasibility and stability in practical navigation environments.
3.1. Datasets and Evaluation Metrics
Model training and testing are conducted on the public terrain dataset ASTER GDEM
V3. For objective evaluation and comparison, the original geographic coordinates are
converted to Mercator coordinates, and grid-based position indices are assigned with the
origin set to 0. A terrain data matrix is then constructed, in which each cell stores the
elevation at the corresponding location in meters.
The dataset is divided into training set A and test set B. Because terrain diversity
strongly influences matching performance, representative subsets are constructed to reflect
practical navigation requirements. Training set A includes 10,368 samples of size 100 × 100,
covering both rugged areas with pronounced relief (Figure 5a) and relatively flat areas
(Figure 5b) to improve generalization and feature extraction in feature-scarce terrain. Test
set B is constructed from gently varying terrain to evaluate and compare different methods

4. Conclusions
This paper presents a UAV terrain matching navigation method designed for GNSS-
denied environments based on ACLM. By constructing the GL-DualNet, the method
effectively combines the local detail representation power of CNNs with the global structure
modeling advantage of the Swin-Transformer, enabling comprehensive extraction and
fusion of multi-scale terrain features. To train this network, ACLM integrates autoencoder
reconstruction learning with contrastive learning, allowing the model to learn terrain
https://doi.org/10.3390/drones10050339
[p.24]
Drones 2026, 10, 339
24 of 26
feature representations with strong discriminability and robustness to interference in an
unsupervised manner. This significantly enhances the robustness of the matching algorithm
under noise and rotational disturbances.
Simulation results on public terrain datasets demonstrate that the proposed method
outperforms traditional matching algorithms, showing high accuracy and stability across
various noise levels, rotational biases, and search ranges. Ablation experiments confirm the
effectiveness of the dual-channel structure, data augmentation strategy, and joint training
framework for UAV applications. Positioning tests further validate the method’s reliable
tracking performance along UAV flight trajectories.
Overall, the proposed method offers a robust solution for UAV navigation in com-
plex GNSS-denied environments, combining strong feature representation with enhanced
resistance to interference and demonstrating significant potential for real-world missions.
