Dust-Resilient Autonomous Navigation and Mapping for UAVs in
GNSS-Denied Underground Tunnels
Marco A. Montes-Grova1, Jos´e M. Gonz´alez-Mar´ın1, Francisco J. P´erez-Grau1 and Antidio Viguria1
Abstract—Autonomous UAVs enable safe inspection of haz-
ardous underground environments, but simultaneous GNSS
denial and visibility degradation from airborne dust present
critical navigation challenges. Conventional visual odometry
fails under feature scarcity, while LiDAR-based methods suffer
from noise artifacts as suspended particles are misinterpreted
as obstacles. This paper presents a UAV system for autonomous
mapping in dust-laden, GNSS-Denied tunnels. LiDAR-Inertial
odometry was integrated with IMU pre-integration to achieve
100 Hz state estimation for responsive control in confined spaces.
An intensity-based dust filtering algorithm removes point cloud
contamination in real-time, enabling autonomous capabilities
despite visibility degradation. Experimental validation in a real
mining tunnel under controlled visually degraded conditions
demonstrates autonomous operation. The system completes
exploration and mapping in 480 seconds, achieving 0.19 m mean
mapping accuracy and 0.60 m RMS error against Total Station
ground truth. Results establish quantitative benchmarks for
UAV deployment in underground construction and emergency
response scenarios where human access is restricted.
I. INTRODUCTION
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) offer a safe and ef-
ficient solution for inspection and mapping in underground
environments such as tunnels and mines, where human ac-
cess is limited by safety, accessibility, or operational con-
straints [1]. In these settings, accurate and timely 3D maps are
essential for construction progress monitoring, structural as-
sessment, and post-blast safety evaluation. Global Navigation
Satellite System (GNSS) provides absolute positioning for
aerial vehicles in open environments; however, underground
settings block these signals entirely, forcing the system to
rely solely on onboard sensing for localization. Autonomous
UAV operation underground therefore remains challenging
due to this simultaneous absence of GNSS and severe per-
ception degradation caused by airborne dust and low or zero
illumination [2].
In post-blast tunnel construction scenarios, suspended par-
ticulate matter significantly degrades sensor performance.
Visual odometry rapidly fails due to feature loss and motion
blur, while LiDAR-based perception suffers from spurious
returns as airborne particles are incorrectly interpreted as
obstacles. Although LiDAR–Inertial Odometry (LIO) - a state
estimation approach that fuses high-rate IMU measurements
with LiDAR scan matching to estimate the robot’s ego-
motion - has demonstrated robustness in GNSS-Denied en-
vironments, existing systems are typically evaluated under
clean atmospheric conditions or rely on manual piloting,

Recent advances in LIO frameworks have improved local-
ization robustness in geometrically degenerate environments
such as tunnels [3]–[5]. Methods incorporating IMU pre-
integration and intensity-augmented correspondences have
shown strong performance in feature-sparse scenarios. How-
ever, prior tunnel-focused evaluations have been limited to
clean environments and teleoperated flights, without address-
ing the coupled challenges of real-time dust mitigation,
autonomous exploration, and high-speed onboard state es-
timation required for safe operation in confined spaces.
Dust mitigation has been explored in ground robotics
and automotive applications, where filtering techniques and
learning-based approaches have been proposed to remove
weather- or debris-induced artifacts from LiDAR data [6]–
[8]. Nevertheless, underground construction dust differs sig-
nificantly in particle size, density, and reflectivity, and aerial
platforms further exacerbate the problem through propeller
downwash that continuously re-suspends settled material.
As a result, existing solutions do not directly translate to
autonomous UAV operation in post-blast tunnel environ-
ments [9], [10].
This paper addresses these limitations by presenting a fully
autonomous UAV system for exploration and 3D mapping
in dust-laden, GNSS-Denied underground tunnels. Building
upon a robust LiDAR–Inertial localization backbone, the sys-
tem integrates real-time intensity-based dust filtering, reactive
exploration planning, and onboard guidance and control to
enable end-to-end autonomous operation without operator
intervention. The proposed approach is experimentally val-
idated in a real mining tunnel under simulated post-blast
visibility conditions, with quantitative accuracy assessment

II. UAV PLATFORM
The aerial platform, shown in Fig. 2, is a custom-built
quadrotor specifically designed for autonomous operation
in confined, GNSS-Denied underground environments. The
airframe incorporates protective rotor guards to prevent col-
lisions with tunnel walls and a fairing system that protects
onboard equipment from dust and moisture in tunnels. In
addition, an onboard LED lighting system has been incorpo-
rated to improve visibility in low-light conditions and make
it easier to see the drone. The propulsion system uses sealed
motors with protective covers to prevent dust ingress into
the motor coils, ensuring reliable operation in contaminated
environments. The total takeoff weight is approximately
5.2 kg, with a flight endurance of 12 minutes under typical
tunnel inspection missions.
The perception system integrates complementary sensors
optimized for harsh underground conditions. The primary
ranging sensor is an Ouster OS0-128 3D LiDAR providing
128-channel point clouds at 10 Hz with 90° vertical field
of view, configured in dual-return mode to reject airborne
particles and mounted in a side-facing orientation for hor-
izontal tunnel mapping. To capture data from inside the
tunnels a FLIR Hadron 640R dual-sensor system combining
a 640×512 LWIR thermal image with a 1080p RGB camera,
integrated on a Gremsy G-Hadron 3-axis gimbal for stabiliza-
tion. The Pixhawk Orange Cube autopilot integrates a high-
performance IMU (ICM-20948) streaming raw measurements
at 100 Hz for tightly-coupled LiDAR-inertial odometry.
All perception and navigation algorithms execute onboard
using an Intel NUC 10 Performance (i7-10710U, 16 GB
RAM) running Ubuntu 20.04 and ROS Noetic. The computa-
tional architecture supports real-time processing of LiDAR-
inertial odometry, dust filtering, real time mapping with loop
closure capabilities and exploration planning.

III. SOFTWARE ARCHITECTURE
Fig. 3 shows the architecture of the software that runs on
the companion aboard the aircraft to perform autonomous
operations in the tunnel. It is important to note that all of
this can be executed without operator intervention, as the
system operates fully autonomously. A communication link
to a control station outside the tunnel is maintained solely
for monitoring purposes.
A. Point Cloud Pre-processing & Dust Removal
The data processing pipeline begins with a dust removal
module designed to clean the raw point cloud data before
it reaches downstream components. Beyond configuring the
LiDAR to operate in dual-return mode, a multi-stage fil-
tering approach was implemented. First, an intensity-based
clustering threshold was established through the manual
examination of extensive test flights and field data. This
threshold aims to eliminate residual noisy points generated by
airborne particulates (dust) while preserving legitimate low-
intensity measurements. Second, to optimize computational
efficiency and filter near-field artifacts, a spatial filter was
applied to a specific Region of Interest (ROI) surrounding
the sensor, as observations indicated that dust-induced noise
appeared predominantly at specific radial distances.
B. GNSS-Denied Localization & Mapping
In this implementation, the system addresses the challenge
of drift accumulation inherent to dead-reckoning systems
by decoupling the high-frequency odometry estimation from
the global mapping optimization. For real-time ego-motion,
COIN-LIO [11] is employed, a tightly coupled LiDAR-
Inertial odometry framework. This algorithm was specifically
selected for its robustness in geometrically degenerate envi-
ronments (e.g., featureless tunnels or symmetrical structures)
where traditional geometric-only methods struggle. COIN-
LIO integrates LiDAR intensity gradients with point-to-plane
registration, enabling the system to maintain accurate track-
ing by leveraging photometric information when geometric
features are insufficient. To generate a precise, drift-free
global point cloud, a back-end optimization stage is executed
asynchronously. The GTSAM [12] framework is used to
construct and solve a factor graph, where odometry poses
form the backbone of the graph. To mitigate drift, loop

C. Reactive & Autonomous Navigation
The navigation stack is designed as a hierarchical archi-
tecture, ensuring both efficient local reaction and optimal
global exploration. To handle the computational constraints
of onboard processing, ROG-Map [14] is used, a robocentric
occupancy grid mapping framework. ROG-Map maintains
a sliding-window local map that moves with the robot,
providing a high-resolution representation of the immediate
environment while discarding geographically distant data to
limit memory usage. This efficient data structure supports
real-time query operations essential for the planning layers.
1) Global Planner: For autonomous decision-making in
unknown environments, the GBPlanner (Graph-based Plan-
ner) [15] is employed. This planner operates on a bifurcated
architecture: a local layer builds a random graph within a
sliding window to identify paths that maximize exploration
gain (volumetric coverage), while a global layer maintains
the overall topological graph to guide the robot out of dead-
ends or towards unexplored frontiers, while also maintaining
a safe return-to-home path in case of emergency.
2) Local Planner: To execute the high-level paths gener-
ated by the exploration module, Fast-Planner [16], has been
integrated. This module converts the waypoints into smooth,
dynamically feasible trajectories. It utilizes a topological [17]
path searching frontend followed by a B-spline optimization
backend, ensuring that the generated flight paths respect the
robot’s physical limits (velocity, acceleration) while safely
avoiding obstacles detected. Notably, while the original Fast-
Planner implementation includes its own occupancy mapping
module, a custom integration was developed to utilize ROG-
Map as the underlying world representation. This decoupled
approach not only enhances computational efficiency by
leveraging ROG-Map’s robocentric scrolling properties but
also grants the end-user greater flexibility to fine-tune map
resolution and performance parameters independently from
the planning logic.
3) Guidance & Control: To bridge the gap between the
local planning layer and the physical platform, LUCAS
framework [18], has been integrated. This open-source ar-
chitecture provides a robust abstraction layer that decouples
high-level autonomy from low-level flight control through
two core components:
• Control Manager Finite-State-Machine (FSM): This
module
orchestrates
the
operational
logic
of
the
UAV. It manages transitions between multiple control
states—such as autonomous takeoff, trajectory tracking,
and emergency contingencies—ensuring that the system
remains in a safe and predictable mode throughout the
mission.
• Cascade PID Controller: This component handles the
interface with the low-level autopilot. It receives the dy-
namically feasible trajectory commands from the local
planner layer and processes them through a cascaded
control architecture to generate precise setpoints. This
ensures that the UAV accurately follows the planned
paths while maintaining stability, even in the presence
of external disturbances or aerodynamic effects.
By leveraging this dual-component structure, the system
achieves a high degree of reliability, allowing the exploration
and mapping modules to operate seamlessly across different
hardware configurations.

D. Surveillance Subsystem
This surveillance system was created with the aim of
capturing information on the state of the interior of the tunnel
after the detonation. In addition to allowing visibility through
smoke under visually degraded conditions, it will enable
the detection of hot, undetonated explosives in the tunnel
environment, so that alerts and markers can be generated on
the map created to inform operators. It is important to note
that the RGB/IR camera is part of the surveillance subsystem
exclusively and plays no role in the navigation or mapping
pipeline.
IV. EXPERIMENTAL VALIDATION
To validate the proposed system, a center in Spain spe-
cializing in research, training, and testing activities in the
field of underground works and mining operations was used.
Although there are two tunnels adjacent only one of them has
been chosen to carry out the experiments, with a straight-line
length of around 500 meters.
It should be noted that ground-truth measurements cannot be
obtained due to the real-world environment; however, a map
generated by a surveying tool over two days is available,
which will be assumed as ground-truth going forward.
In order to monitor the mission, a mesh communications
network has been installed throughout the selected tunnel.
This mesh network has been created using TP-Link EAP225
access points and controlled by TP-Link Omada OC200.
Adhesive directional antennas have been placed on the front
and rear of the aerial robot to maximize communication
quality at any location in the tunnel.
To simulate the degraded visibility conditions in which the
aircraft must operate, no actual detonation was carried out.
To generate this atmosphere, the ANTARI Z-3000II R smoke
machine was used. In addition, it should be noted that there
are no lighting installations along most of the length of the
tunnel.
Next, the results of the two main functionalities of the
system will be evaluated: On the one hand, the capability to
explore in a fully autonomous way an environment without
GNSS positioning that has not been previously visited, i.e.,
completely unknown, in conditions of degraded visibility.
On the other hand, the real-time mapping algorithm will be
evaluated. This will be an indirect measure of the quality of
the deployed localization algorithm, as the map is dependent
on the location in the environment.
A. Autonomous exploration with visually degraded condi-
tions
During the autonomous mission inside the tunnel, there
will be two distinct phases of operation: a first phase of
pure exploration, where the environment will be surveyed
and mapped, and a second phase of return, where instead of
exploring, the safest route back to the takeoff point will be
planned using the map created. The trigger for returning to
the take-off point can be for several main reasons: the drone
detects that the battery level is at the safe limit to be able to
exit the tunnel, or it detects that there are no new areas to
explore because the map is completely closed. In both cases,
the trigger will be autonomous; or the operator requests a
return to the take-off point.
For safety reasons, the maximum exploration path length
was set to 8 meters, ensuring the UAV maintained adequate
clearance from walls and obstacles while keeping its trajec-
tory centered within the tunnel. To minimize exploration time
and sustain high speeds, each subsequent path was generated
several meters before the UAV completed the current one.
After repeating the experiment a number of times with
satisfactory results, it has been found that the most efficient
way in terms of movement dynamics is a speed of 1.5m/s
and an acceleration of 0.75m/s2 during the exploration
phase. During the return phase, given that the environment is
assumed to be familiar and there is less chance of obstacles
appearing, the decision is made to navigate faster, specifically
at a speed of 2.5m/s and an acceleration of 1.25m/s2. These
dynamic conditions mean that the aircraft travels from the
outside of the tunnel in exploration conditions to the end of
the tunnel, at the excavation front, in 310 seconds. As for the
exit, again, it is completed in around 170 seconds. This means
that the entire operation, from the moment the drone enters
the tunnel until it exits, completely autonomously, takes 480
seconds.

B. Real-Time 3D Mapping
A further strength of the proposed work is its ability to
generate real-time 3D maps of the environment, allowing the
operator to make use of them when the aerial robot exits the
tunnel. In Fig. 6 is shown an overlapping between Ground-
Truth cloud and the one generated by the aerial robot.
To further quantify the geometric fidelity of the UAV-
generated map, a cloud-to-cloud (C2C) distance analysis
was performed against the LEICA Total Station reference
model. This metric computes, for every point in the UAV
cloud, the Euclidean distance to its nearest neighbour in the
ground-truth model, thereby providing a per-point measure

mean accuracy of 0.19 m, a root-mean-square (RMS) error
of 0.60 m, and a standard deviation of 0.57 m. In Fig. 7,
each point is colour-coded according to this distance, where
green denotes low error and red indicates higher deviations;
the colour scale is clamped at the 95th percentile to enhance
contrast in the dominant error range. The heatmap reveals
that the central sections of the gallery exhibit consistently
low deviations, benefiting from the strong geometric priors
provided by the tunnel walls to the odometry algorithm. In
contrast, the two regions with the highest error correspond to
the tunnel entrance and the excavation front, each attributable
to a distinct cause.
At the tunnel entrance, the increase in error is due to
vegetation and surrounding objects that were captured by the
UAV’s LiDAR scanner but do not appear in the total station
model, as the environment at the tunnel entrance has changed
due to the time difference of a couple of years between the
two maps. At the excavation front, the discrepancy originates
from the temporal gap between the two surveys: the ground-
truth model was generated approximately two years prior to
the UAV mission, during which period the tunnel advanced
beyond the geometry recorded by the Total Station. Conse-
quently, the excavation front captured by the UAV has no cor-
responding reference surface, producing large C2C distances
that do not reflect an actual mapping inaccuracy but rather
a genuine geometric change in the environment. Excluding
these two boundary regions, the core tunnel reconstruction
demonstrates a high degree of geometric consistency with the
reference model, confirming that autonomous aerial scanning
provides sufficient precision for progress monitoring in drill-
and-blast tunneling operations.
It is important to note that, in addition to the metrics
obtained, the total station map took approximately two days
to capture the data and generate the 3D map, while the map
generated by the drone was available as soon as the aircraft
left the tunnel. In other words, the time it takes to generate
the map with the UAV is the same as the time it takes to
perform the scan, which in this case is around 480 seconds.
V. CONCLUSIONS
This paper presented a fully autonomous UAV system
capable of exploration and high-fidelity 3D mapping in
GNSS-Denied underground tunnels under severe visibility
degradation caused by airborne dust. By tightly integrating
LiDAR–inertial odometry, real-time intensity-based dust fil-
tering, reactive exploration planning, and onboard guidance
and control, the system demonstrated reliable autonomous
operation in conditions where both visual and conventional
LiDAR-based approaches typically fail. Experimental valida-
tion in a real mining tunnel under simulated post-blast con-
ditions confirmed the robustness of the proposed approach.
The system achieved a mean geometric accuracy of 0.19
m with respect to surveying-grade Total Station ground truth,
while completing a full autonomous exploration and return
mission in under eight minutes. Compared to traditional
surveying workflows requiring days of on-site work and
human exposure, the proposed UAV-based solution provides
near-immediate availability of dense 3D tunnel maps. This
capability is particularly relevant for underground construc-
tion and mining industries, where accurate and timely digital
representations of tunnel geometry are critical for progress
monitoring, safety assessment, and decision-making during
post-blast operations.
Future work will focus on extending the system to more
complex tunnel networks with branches and intersections,
as well as increasing robustness under even denser par-
ticulate conditions. Additional research will also address
long-duration missions through multi-robot cooperation and
improved energy management, and the integration of se-
mantic mapping to enrich geometric reconstructions with
structural and safety-related information. These developments
aim to further advance autonomous aerial inspection as a
practical tool for routine deployment in active underground
construction environments.
