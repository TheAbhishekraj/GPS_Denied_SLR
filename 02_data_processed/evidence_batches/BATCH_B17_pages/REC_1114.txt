A B S T R A C T
Accurate geolocation remains challenging in areas with limited Global Positioning System (GPS) availability, 
such as dense forests, rural settings, urban canyons, and disaster zones. To overcome this, we introduce 
Inverse Navigation (I-NAV), a geolocation framework explicitly designed for decision-making in GPS-denied 
environments. Unlike conventional methods that determine optimal routes from known origins to destinations, 
I-NAV inversely identifies probable user locations based on contextual data from questionnaires such as 
recently passed landmarks, travel-time constraints, and directional cues. Consequently, it can be utilized in 
critical applications, including public health monitoring, drone surveillance, search-and-rescue operations, and 
emergency response. Applying a modified Dijkstra’s algorithm, I-NAV generates all feasible paths at a high 
resolution that is reachable within a specified travel budget. Within a Bayesian inference framework, we 
introduce an Adaptive Gaussian Likelihood Model that combines a reachability prior with travel-time and 
directional observation likelihoods to produce a posterior probability distribution over candidate locations, 
effectively capturing uncertainties in travel conditions. Our experimental results demonstrate that I-NAV’s 
deterministic isochrone component achieves spatial coverage comparable to established routing services such 
as OpenRouteService, while the probabilistic component outperforms several baseline localization approaches 
across multiple evaluation metrics.
1. Introduction
Geolocation is fundamental to contemporary technological systems, 
underpinning applications from everyday navigation to critical emer-
gency response operations. Despite its widespread utility, the effective-
ness of the GPS significantly diminishes in environments with limited or 
obstructed satellite connectivity—such as rural areas, dense forests, ur-
ban canyons, and disaster-stricken regions. Consequently, geolocation 
accuracy often deteriorates precisely when it is most critical, highlight-
ing a notable gap in current navigation capabilities and emphasizing 
an urgent need for robust, satellite-independent solutions.
Substantial advancements in geolocation have emerged through 
methods such as Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) and 
visual–inertial odometry. Odometry specifically involves estimating a 
vehicle’s position using sensor-derived motion data; when augmented 
by visual inputs, it forms the cornerstone of visual–inertial odom-
etry systems. Thrun et al. [1] pioneered foundational principles of 
modern probabilistic robotics, greatly influencing contemporary SLAM

In GPS-denied environments, significant efforts have explicitly fo-
cused on robust localization leveraging deep-learning and visual SLAM 
variants. Cohen and Klein [12] cataloged significant progress in deep-
learning-based inertial navigation, showing that lightweight neural 
filters effectively bound low-cost Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems 
(MEMS) drift on land, at sea, and in air. Complementary progress in 
visual SLAM has produced dense, semantically aware systems such 
as Dense Neural Semantic-Informed SLAM (DNS-SLAM) [13], Implicit 
Mapping and Positioning in Real-Time (iMAP) [14], Neural Implicit 
Scalable Encoding for SLAM (NICE-SLAM) [15], each demonstrat-
ing centimeter-scale indoor accuracy without GPS. Advances in map-
matching and visual place recognition further support resilient geoloca-
tion. Large-scale evaluations of vehicle map-matching techniques [16] 
and speed-up heuristics such as contraction hierarchies [17,18] clarify 
trade-offs between preprocessing cost and query latency. Compact 
global descriptors like SuperVLAD [19] and event-camera-based Event-
VPR [20] provide robust landmark confirmation under challenging 
conditions.
Landmark-based and qualitative mapping approaches have pro-
vided reliable solutions in GPS-denied contexts, often with minimal 
inputs. Sapkota et al. integrated prominent landmarks with an Ex-
tended Kalman Filter (EKF) to guide troops through GPS-jammed en-
vironments, achieving errors under 3.3 m [21]. Hua et al. developed 
qualitative place maps enabling localization using descriptive cues 
(e.g., ‘‘next to the library’’) without precise coordinates [22]. Singh 
and Sujit demonstrated Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) path planning 
by graphically connecting visible landmarks, enabling reliable flight in 
GPS-denied areas [23]. Indoor pedestrian dead reckoning also benefits 
significantly from landmark updates, substantially reducing drift [24]. 
These methods often incorporate probabilistic filtering techniques such 
as Kullback–Leibler Divergence (KLD)-Sampling, dynamically adjusting 
particle counts to balance accuracy and computational cost [25]. Re-
cent work has further emphasized probabilistic localization frameworks 
designed for uncertain sensing environments and GPS-denied navi-
gation scenarios, highlighting the importance of explicitly modeling 
uncertainty in both measurements and motion estimates [26].
Alternative approaches leverage signals-of-opportunity, prior map 
knowledge, and mobility models. Chang et al. comprehensively re-
viewed UAV navigation without GPS, covering vision-based methods, 
map-based approaches, and signals of opportunity [27]. Vision-based 
systems by Mateos-Ramirez et al. align onboard imagery with satellite 
maps to correct visual odometry drift, enabling navigation over ex-
tensive distances without GPS [28]. Doherty et al. demonstrated that 
isochrone-based mobility models effectively reduce search areas by 
realistically estimating travel distances [29]. Note that isochrone is a 
line drawn on a map connecting points at which something occurs or 
arrives simultaneously. Khalife et al. employed signals-of-opportunity 
techniques using ambient radio frequency (RF) broadcasts to achieve 
GPS-level accuracy [30]. Emergency localization methods also benefit 
significantly from automatically transmitting caller locations, consider-
ably reducing localization errors and enhancing response efficiency in 
medical crises [31].
In addition to these methods, classical routing and heuristic search 
approaches have significantly influenced geolocation solutions, es-
pecially in structured scenarios. Algorithms such as Dijkstra’s and 
the heuristic-based A-star (A*) algorithm [32] are widely employed 
in various geospatial applications for optimal pathfinding and route 
optimization. Engineering aspects of such route-planning algorithms, 
including practical enhancements and optimizations, have been exten-
sively explored by Delling et al. [33], while dynamic adaptations of 
these methods, such as the D* Lite algorithm proposed by Koenig and 
Likhachev [34], have provided substantial improvements for real-time 
dynamic pathfinding scenarios. Liu et al. [35] successfully adapted 
Dijkstra’s algorithm specifically for geomagnetic navigation contexts, 
demonstrating its versatility in alternative navigation strategies. Fur-
ther extending heuristic search capabilities, Cao et al. [36] devel-
oped methods targeting safety-oriented path planning. Additionally, 
the concept of isochrones — regions accessible within specific time 
or cost thresholds — has grown critical for dynamic accessibility 
mapping, especially pertinent to emergency response and rural navi-
gation. Batz et al. [37] introduced fast multimodal isochrone compu-
tation techniques integrating diverse transportation modes for accu-
rate route planning. Furthermore, OpenRouteService (ORS), developed 
through integrating open-source geographic datasets, has demonstrated 
effective real-time geospatial processing capabilities for generating 
isochrones [38]. The rise of volunteered geographic information, high-
lighted by Goodchild [39], underscores its potential to supplement 
traditional geospatial datasets, further improving spatial analyses.
Despite the significant advancements, existing GPS-denied local-
ization methods have notable limitations. SLAM and visual–inertial 
odometry rely heavily on visual clarity and accurate sensor inputs, 
degrading in feature-sparse environments. Classical isochrone methods 
often require extensive preprocessing and stable conditions, limiting 
real-time adaptability in dynamic scenarios. Methods such as Wi-Fi 
or cell-tower triangulation require robust existing infrastructure, usu-
ally unavailable in remote or disaster-affected regions. Consequently, 
a clear research gap exists for robust, real-time geolocation meth-
ods explicitly tailored for GPS-denied environments characterized by 
no sensor inputs, sparse infrastructure, and rapidly changing condi-
tions, particularly where explicit destination points are undefined. 
We propose the Inverse Navigation (I-NAV) framework, a geolocation 
method explicitly designed for GPS-denied environments to address 
these limitations.
Inverse to traditional navigation, which finds routes from known 
origins to predetermined destinations, I-NAV determines probable user 
locations by leveraging minimal contextual information, such as re-
cently encountered landmarks, travel-time constraints, and directional 
data. Conceptually, this formulation can be interpreted as an inference 
problem under partial observability, where the user’s current location 
must be estimated from incomplete and uncertain observations. Similar 
estimation challenges arise in state-estimation frameworks that operate 
under limited sensing and partial information [40]. Using a modi-
fied Dijkstra’s algorithm, I-NAV generates high-resolution isochrones 
within a specific travel budget. In contrast to conventional accessibility 
analysis, where isochrones represent the final output, I-NAV treats 
the reachable region as a prior constraint within a probabilistic infer-
ence framework used to estimate the user’s unknown location. These 
isochrones are subsequently transformed into continuous probabilistic 
accessibility maps by leveraging our Adaptive Gaussian Likelihood 
Model (AGLM), which dynamically captures variations in traveler be-
havior and environmental uncertainties. This integrated approach — 
combining real-time user-generated questionnaire data, probabilistic 
modeling, and adaptive algorithms — is the first crafted explicitly for 
GPS-denied environments.

The most comparable work is the Secure Navigation using
Landmark-based Localization in a GPS denied Environment (LanBLoc) 
by Sapkota et al. [21], which also targets GPS-jammed scenarios and 
leverages known landmarks for navigation. While LanBLoc shares some 
high-level goals with I-NAV, it is tightly coupled to military use cases. 
It assumes access to real-time stereo imagery, pre-trained deep learning 
models, and a central control server that generates predefined safe 
paths. These constraints limit its usability outside structured battle-
field settings, especially without access to expensive hardware such 
as sensors and cameras. In contrast, I-NAV is designed for flexible, 
infrastructure-free geolocation using only sparse human input like 
estimated travel time. Any further information, such as direction or 
observed landmarks, will enhance the performance of I-NAV. These 
features make I-NAV attractive for real-world civilian applications with 
limited or unavailable sensors and connectivity.
Outside of traditional navigation approaches, there exists a parallel 
body of work on travel time estimation (TTE). TTE methods aim to 
predict trip durations, typically in real time, using sensor or trajectory 
data. They are commonly grouped into three families: segment-based, 
path-based, and origin–destination (OD)–based [41]. Below, we de-
scribe how each works, how it relates to our system, and the key 
differences.
Segment-based methods: These approaches assign a travel time or 
speed to each road segment using sensors (e.g., loop detectors), probe 
vehicles, or learned models, and then sum across the route. Like I-
NAV, they operate at the road-segment level and aggregate costs. The 
difference is that they require continuous sensor or probe data and 
accurate map-matching, whereas I-NAV assigns costs using static map 
features and can function without live data.
Path-based methods: These approaches use deep models (CNNs, 
RNNs, Transformers, GNNs) to predict the total travel time of a com-
plete route from GPS traces or encoded paths. Similar to I-NAV, they 
consider the path as a whole rather than only isolated segments. The 
difference is that they require large GPS datasets and frequent retrain-
ing to remain accurate. I-NAV works offline and instead produces a 
probabilistic region of all reachable locations within a budget, rather 
than a single route estimate.
OD-based methods: These approaches train on large archives of 
past trips (e.g., taxi logs, ride-hailing data) to learn the typical duration 
between an origin, destination, and departure time, and then return 
that duration instantly at query time. Our system is similar in that it is 
also validated against historical data, such as ORS and real taxi rides, 
to confirm realism. The difference is that OD-based models require 
extensive archives to function and provide a single estimate for one OD 
pair, whereas I-NAV does not depend on such data and instead produces 
the full set of destinations reachable within a time budget.
Segment-, path-, and OD-based TTE methods are designed for data-
rich, connected cities with dense trajectories and sensors. In contrast, 
I-NAV targets GPS-denied, offline, or rural environments where such 
data are not available. Nonetheless, TTE outputs can complement I-
NAV. Segment-based estimates can replace static per-link costs with 
learned travel times, path-based models can give learned travel times 
for entire routes, which I-NAV can use to adjust cost values so that the 
reachable region better matches real travel times, and OD-based estima-
tors can bias likelihood maps toward historically common destinations. 
Importantly, these inputs can improve performance when available but 
are not required for the data-minimal formulation of I-NAV.
Here are the four main contributions of this paper:
• I-NAV for GPS-Denied Geolocation: The proposed system (I-
NAV) is a uniquely designed, context-driven solution optimized 
explicitly for reliable and precise geolocation in GPS-denied envi-
ronments, addressing critical gaps in current localization method-
ologies.
• Bayesian Probabilistic Framework: The geolocation task is for-
mulated as a principled Bayesian inference problem in which a 
reachability prior, a Gaussian travel-time likelihood, and a von 
Mises directional likelihood are combined to produce a posterior 
distribution over candidate locations. All model parameters are 
calibrated through data-driven Bayesian optimization.

2. Problem formulation and methodology
2.1. Problem statement
In GPS-denied environments, standard geolocation methods relying 
on satellite signals, continuous connectivity, or sensor-rich systems

often fail. These settings, which are characterized by sparse infras-
tructure, dynamic conditions, and limited data, require alternative 
solutions. Given minimal user-reported data such as recently passed 
landmarks, estimated travel duration, and general travel direction, our 
goal is to estimate the user’s probable location and generate both de-
terministic and probabilistic reachability maps. Formally, the problem 
has the following inputs and outputs:
Inputs:
• User-reported starting landmarks (landmark indices)
• Approximate travel duration (time budget)
• Optional directional cues
• Road network data from OpenStreetMap (OSM)
Outputs:
• Deterministic isochrone boundaries defining reachable areas
within a time budget
• Probabilistic accessibility maps representing the likelihood of 
reaching each location (via AGLM)

2.3. System workflow
To systematically address the stated problem, we propose a two-
stage integrated geolocation framework. First, the I-NAV component 
discretizes the target area into grid-based cells and applies a mod-
ified Dijkstra’s [42] algorithm to calculate deterministic isochrones, 
clearly marking accessible regions within a defined travel-time bud-
get. These deterministic outputs define the feasible support of the 
inference problem. The AGLM then assigns each reachable grid cell a 
nonnegative likelihood value based on the reported travel duration, 
the network travel-time residual, local road-connectivity-dependent 
uncertainty, and user-reported cues. These values are subsequently 
normalized over the reachable set to obtain a posterior probability 
distribution over candidate cells. Fig. 1 provides a high-level overview 
of this process, which is elaborated upon below.
3. I-NAV design
This section details each stage of the I-NAV pipeline, from data 
acquisition and preprocessing to final isochrone generation and visu-
alization.
3.1. Data acquisition and preprocessing
We use open-source geospatial data from OSM to acquire data and 
extract road networks using the Overpass Turbo Application Program-
ming Interface (API). The raw JSON data from these sources is parsed 
into a usable format, and the region of interest is discretized into a grid 
of square tiles with a resolution of 0.0001◦ (approximately 11.1 m). 
Each tile is then assigned attributes based on the type of road and 
corresponding speed limits, ensuring that the grid-based representation 
accurately reflects the underlying geography. In the discretized grid, 
a cell is classified as a road cell if an OSM road segment is present 
within its bounds after preprocessing. Cells that do not overlap with 
any road geometry are labeled as non-road cells. These remain in the 
grid structure but are assigned prohibitively high traversal costs and 
excluded from routing, as they are not traversable by car.
This definition ensures that isochrone generation is constrained to 
realistic paths while retaining flexibility for future extensions (e.g.,
modeling off-road mobility).

3.3. Isochrone generation
The I-NAV algorithm generates isochrones by representing the dis-
cretized map as a grid-based graph, where each cell is a node connected 
to its adjacent and diagonal neighbors. This structure is initialized 
by preprocessing the OSM-derived road network, assigning each node 
a travel cost based on speed limits, intersection delays, and terrain 
attributes.
In I-NAV, a landmark is defined as a fixed, recognizable location 
that serves as the origin for isochrone generation. By design, the 
starting point in every experiment is always treated as a landmark, en-
suring that isochrone expansion begins from a contextually meaningful 
position.
A modified Dijkstra’s algorithm is applied to the graph to identify 
all nodes reachable from the source point within a user-defined travel-
time budget. Unlike the conventional version, this implementation 
supports diagonal traversal and integrates turn penalties to improve ac-
curacy in urban environments, where intersections and route geometry 
significantly influence travel time.
Fig. 2 illustrates the preprocessing and discretization steps: (a) 
shows the OpenStreetMap view of the pilot area in North Dallas, Texas, 
(b) displays the discretized grid at 0.0001◦ resolution, and (c) provides 
a zoomed-in view highlighting the discretized node structure used in 
the graph.
Rather than computing a single optimal route, the algorithm iden-
tifies the complete set of accessible nodes, producing a spatially con-
tinuous isochrone. These can be rendered as layered heatmaps, where 
multiple isochrones (e.g., 0–5 min, 5–10 min, 10–15 min) are overlaid 
to visualize the progression of accessibility over time. This visualization 
supports rapid geolocation and decision-making in dynamic scenarios. 
This visualization is presented in the Results section. In what fol-
lows, I-NAV refers by default to the direction-free framework. When 
directional cues are incorporated, the extended model is referred to 
explicitly as Directional I-NAV.
3.4. AGLM probabilistic modeling
We now define each component of the single-observation posterior 
(Eq. (1)). The AGLM complements the deterministic isochrone maps 
produced by I-NAV by transforming discrete travel-time outputs into 
a continuous likelihood field that provides a probabilistic measure of 
accessibility. Unlike traditional binary approaches, each reachable grid 
cell is assigned a nonnegative likelihood value based on the consistency 
between the reported duration and the network travel time to that cell. 
These values are then normalized over the reachable set to produce a 
posterior probability map over candidate location cells.

reachable cell based on the consistency between predicted travel time

over candidate locations. This combined framework provides a princi-

4. Results
We evaluate the proposed I-NAV framework using real-world
geospatial data from OSM, discretized into a uniform spatial grid with 
a resolution of 0.0001◦ (approximately 11.1 m per cell). Each cell was 
assigned road-related attributes such as speed limits and connectivity 
to reflect realistic travel conditions.
Our results are structured around two major components: (1) the de-
terministic isochrone generation, which outlines all reachable areas 
within a given travel-time budget, and (2) the AGLM, which overlays 
probabilistic scores based on travel-time uncertainty and directional 
cues.
To evaluate the real-world applicability of I-NAV, we validated 
the system using authoritative real-world mobility datasets and widely 
used routing engines. Specifically, we use the CRAWDAD EPFLmobility 
dataset [54], which contains a large collection of real urban taxi 
trajectories from San Francisco, together with travel-time queries from 
the OpenRouteService (ORS) routing engine. These sources provide 
realistic benchmarks for mobility patterns, travel-time estimation, and 
route feasibility.
The evaluation pipeline consists of two stages: parameter calibration 
and held-out evaluation. Parameter calibration was performed using a 
combined dataset of 175 real taxi trajectories from the EPFLmobility 
dataset and 120 additional trips generated using ORS within the Dallas–
Fort Worth metropolitan area. For the ORS-generated trips, starting 
and ending landmarks were randomly sampled within the region and 
the corresponding ORS travel time was used as the reported duration 
input to the model. These synthetic trips were used only for parameter 
calibration in order to improve geographic diversity and reduce the risk 
of overfitting to a single dataset.
Model parameters were calibrated using Bayesian optimization over 
the joint I-NAV/AGLM parameter space. The objective function used 
during optimization was the median endpoint confidence level on the 
training set. The confidence level is defined as the smallest cumulative 
probability mass required to include the true endpoint cell; lower 
values indicate tighter localization performance. Bayesian optimization 
iteratively explores the parameter space using a surrogate model and 
acquisition strategy to identify configurations that minimize this objec-
tive. Confidence level was chosen as the optimization objective because 
it directly measures localization tightness, which is our primary oper-
ational goal. Proper scoring rules (NLL, CRPS) are reported separately 
to confirm that the resulting distributions are also well-calibrated, not 
merely concentrated.
To ensure unbiased evaluation, the dataset was split into training 
and evaluation subsets following an approximately 80/20 protocol. 
Parameter optimization was performed exclusively on the training 
portion, while a held-out set of 94 real taxi trajectories was reserved 
for final evaluation. The optimized parameter configuration was then 
fixed and applied to all experiments reported in this section.
After parameter calibration, we first visualize deterministic reach-
ability maps produced by I-NAV and validate their boundaries against 
ORS and Google Maps. We then present the Adaptive Gaussian Like-
lihood Model (AGLM) probability maps. Additional analysis examines 
the relationship between travel time and likelihood values to verify that 
the probabilistic model concentrates probability mass near travel times 
consistent with the reported duration.
Beyond traditional spatial overlap comparisons, the evaluation
framework introduces a comprehensive set of probabilistic localization 
metrics and baseline comparisons. We compare I-NAV against three 
baselines: (i) a uniform reachable-road prior, (ii) a kernel density esti-
mation (KDE) model based on travel time, and (iii) a particle-filter-style 
estimator.
The evaluation includes several complementary metric categories:
• Probabilistic accuracy metrics: Negative Log Likelihood (NLL) 
and the Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS) to evaluate 
how well the predicted probability distribution matches the true 
endpoint.

• Localization concentration metrics: mean and median confi-
dence level, which measure the probability mass required to 
capture the true endpoint.
• Coverage

Coverage@75%, and Coverage@90%, which assess whether the

Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive evaluation of 
probabilistic calibration, localization accuracy, and ranking perfor-

earlier deterministic metrics such as IoU, precision, and recall, which 
only assess reachable-area overlap rather than probabilistic geolocation 
accuracy.
All experiments were conducted on a standard workstation with an

environments.

Bayesian optimization and are summarized in Table 4. Parameter 
ranges were selected based on the modeling considerations described 
in Section 3, while the final values correspond to the configuration that 
minimized the median endpoint confidence level during training.

4.2. AGLM likelihood map results
Now, we present the probabilistic outputs produced by the AGLM. 
Unlike the deterministic isochrones that define binary reachability, 
AGLM assigns a continuous likelihood value to each grid cell based on 
modeled uncertainty, expected travel time, and local road connectivity. 
These likelihood values represent the probability of reaching each 
location under typical conditions. We first show the unconstrained like-
lihood field and then apply directional filters to highlight the benefit 
of incorporating user cues.
4.2.1. Direction-free likelihood mapping
Fig. 9(a) shows the full-area likelihood distribution generated by 
AGLM without any directional input. Higher probability mass is con-
centrated around more likely regions as shown by the darker color, 
tapering smoothly toward the outer edge of the isochrone, consistent 
with travel-time uncertainty.
Fig. 9(b) presents a zoomed-in view, highlighting smooth gradients 
of likelihood values along roads. These gradients demonstrate how 
AGLM reflects realistic accessibility patterns while avoiding sharp, 
unrealistic cutoffs.
4.2.2. Directionally filtered likelihood mapping
When the user reports a travel direction, the von Mises directional 
likelihood (Eq. (16)) is multiplied into the temporal likelihood before 
normalization, concentrating probability mass toward cells consistent 
with the reported heading. Fig. 10 illustrates this effect. Panel (a) 
shows the direction-free posterior, where probability is distributed 
across all reachable road cells based solely on travel-time agreement. 
Panel (b) shows the posterior after incorporating a directional input 
of north. Probability mass shifts toward the northern quadrant while 
cells in off-direction regions are attenuated, narrowing the candidate 
region without introducing sharp cutoffs. This filtering reduces spatial 
uncertainty when even a coarse heading estimate is available.
4.3. Comparison with ORS using probabilistic AGLM output
To evaluate how well AGLM likelihood values reflect actual travel 
times, we compare them against ORS predictions. Specifically, we 
investigate whether high-likelihood regions in the AGLM output cor-

specified budget. We also quantify the spatial agreement between 
AGLM and ORS using Intersection-over-Union (IoU) and precision–
recall metrics.

locations with travel times close to the 25-minute budget. Because 
this experiment analyzes the relationship between travel time and 
model response across the reachable region rather than evaluating a 
specific endpoint, we use the unnormalized AGLM likelihood values 
instead of the normalized posterior probabilities. This avoids distor-

combination with the coverage results, they indicate that AGLM pro-

evaluation on the same 94 held-out trips with the von Mises directional

true geometric bearing from origin to endpoint was snapped to the 
nearest intercardinal compass prompt (E, NE, N, NW, W, SW, S, SE) 
and used as the directional observation 𝑧𝜃. This simulates an idealized 
questionnaire response in which the user correctly identifies a coarse 
heading. Because the directional cue is derived from the ground-truth 
bearing rather than actual human input, the results represent an upper 
bound on the improvement achievable from directional information.

the top 25% of posterior mass. NLL improved from 9.12 to 8.34, and 
mean endpoint probability more than doubled from 1.23 × 10−4 to 
2.70 × 10−4.

4.5. Comparison with baseline methods
To further assess the effectiveness of AGLM, we compare it with 
three baseline probabilistic localization approaches using the same 
evaluation dataset of 94 trips described earlier. For all methods, the 
candidate set of reachable cells is defined by the deterministic I-NAV 
isochrone generated from the reported travel-time budget. Restricting 
inference to the same reachable support ensures that differences in 
performance arise from the probabilistic modeling strategy rather than 
variations in the underlying search space.
Uniform Reachable-Road Baseline: This baseline assigns equal 
probability to all road cells within the reachable region produced by 
the I-NAV isochrone. It represents a naïve assumption where every 
reachable location is equally likely.
KDE-over-Time Baseline: This approach applies a kernel density 
estimation (KDE) model over the travel-time dimension. Cells whose 
network travel time is close to the reported duration receive higher 
probability, but the model does not account for road-network connec-
tivity or adaptive uncertainty.
Particle-Filter-Style Baseline: This method samples candidate lo-
cations using a simplified particle-filter formulation. Particles prop-
agate along the reachable network according to the reported time 
constraint and are weighted by travel-time consistency. However, the 
method tends to collapse probability mass onto a small number of 
particles, often producing overconfident predictions.
The methods were evaluated using probabilistic accuracy met-
rics, calibration measures, localization tightness, and ranking-based 
retrieval metrics. Proper scoring rules such as negative log-likelihood 
(NLL) and the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) were used 
to assess the quality of the predicted distributions.
Across these metrics, AGLM consistently achieves the strongest per-
formance. In terms of probabilistic accuracy, AGLM obtains the lowest 
NLL (9.119) and best CRPS (0.99986), indicating that it assigns higher 
probability to the true endpoint while maintaining well-calibrated dis-
tributions. The model also produces the most concentrated localiza-
tion, with a mean confidence level of 0.234, compared to 0.445 for 
KDE-Time, 0.519 for Uniform, and 0.961 for the Particle baseline.
Coverage analysis further supports the reliability of the AGLM pos-
terior distribution. AGLM achieves the highest informative coverage 
across all thresholds. In particular, Coverage@25% reaches 52.13%, 
compared with 38.38% for KDE-Time, 23.40% for Uniform, and only 
7.45% for the Particle method. AGLM is the only method to achieve 
100% coverage at the 50% threshold, meaning every true endpoint falls 
within its top-50% highest-density region.
Ranking metrics show a similar pattern. AGLM produces the highest 
Top-100 and Top-500 hit rates, indicating that the true endpoint is 
more frequently ranked among the highest-probability cells.
Overall, these results demonstrate that AGLM provides the most bal-
anced probabilistic estimator, achieving superior accuracy, calibration, 
and localization tightness compared with the alternative approaches 
(see Table 5).

4.6.1. Travel-time noise injection
Reported travel durations are often approximate and may contain 
user estimation error. To quantify the effect of such noise, we injected 
multiplicative perturbations into the reported travel time for the 94 
evaluation trips and measured the resulting endpoint confidence level.
Starting from the baseline median confidence level of 0.242, the 
reported duration was perturbed by symmetric noise levels of ±10%, 
±20%, and ±30%. As expected, localization quality degrades progres-
sively as timing error increases. The resulting median confidence levels 
were 0.388, 0.738, and 0.764 for ±10%, ±20%, and ±30% perturbations, 
respectively.
To better understand the asymmetry of the effect, positive and 
negative perturbations were analyzed separately. Fig. 18 illustrates this 
behavior for a representative trip. Panel (a) shows the baseline AGLM 
posterior probability map using the original reported travel duration. 
Panel (b) shows the effect of a −10% perturbation, which reduces the 
reachable region. Panel (c) shows the effect of a +10% perturbation, 
which enlarges the feasible region and produces a broader probability 
distribution while still maintaining feasibility of the true endpoint. 
Overestimation of travel time (+10%, +20%, +30%) produced median 
confidence levels of 0.369, 0.621, and 0.705. Underestimation (−10%, 
−20%, −30%) produced larger degradation with median confidence 
levels of 0.407, 0.685, and 0.771. This asymmetry arises because 
underestimation truncates the reachable region during graph expan-
sion, potentially excluding the true endpoint from the candidate set, 
whereas overestimation simply enlarges the feasible region and reduces 
localization precision.
The AGLM temporal likelihood includes two uncertainty parameters 
that help provide robustness to moderate timing error. The parameter 
𝜎base controls the nominal spread of the travel-time residual distribu-
tion, determining how quickly probability decays as modeled travel 
time deviates from the reported duration. The parameter 𝜎min en-
forces a lower bound on dispersion, preventing the likelihood from 
collapsing to an overly sharp peak in highly connected regions. To-
gether, these parameters allow moderate duration mismatch to be ab-
sorbed probabilistically rather than immediately eliminating candidate 
locations.
4.6.2. Landmark perturbation study
In addition to duration noise, the reported landmark used as the 
origin may also be imprecise. In the I-NAV framework, the landmark 
functions as a spatial anchor on the road network rather than requir-
ing strict semantic precision. To evaluate robustness to such spatial 
uncertainty, we conducted an origin-perturbation experiment on 30 
trips.
For each trip, the reported landmark was displaced by fixed radii of 
100 m and 250 m in the four cardinal directions (0◦, 90◦, 180◦, and 
270◦). Each perturbed origin was then snapped to the nearest road 
cell before running the inference procedure, while the endpoint, travel 
duration, and model parameters were kept fixed. Fig. 19 illustrates 
this behavior for a representative trip. Panel (a) shows the baseline 
posterior probability map using the original landmark location. Pan-
els (b) and (c) show the results after shifting the landmark by 100 m 
and 250 m, respectively. While the probability distribution shifts with 
the displaced anchor point, the model continues to produce meaningful 
probability regions around the true endpoint, demonstrating robustness 
to moderate landmark imprecision.
Baseline performance with the original landmarks yielded a mean 
confidence level of 0.278 and a median of 0.264. With 100 m perturba-
tions, the mean confidence increased to 0.389 and the median to 0.317. 
With 250 m perturbations, the mean confidence increased to 0.450 and 
the median to 0.403. These results indicate a predictable reduction in 
localization tightness as anchor uncertainty increases.
The snapping distances remained small relative to the perturbation 
magnitude (median 15.1 m for the 100 m perturbations and 18.5 m 
for the 250 m perturbations), confirming that the observed degradation 
primarily results from anchor displacement rather than artifacts of the 
road-snapping procedure.
Overall, these experiments demonstrate that the I-NAV with AGLM 
framework remains operational under moderate questionnaire noise. 
While localization precision degrades as input uncertainty increases, 
the probabilistic formulation allows the system to maintain meaning-
ful probability distributions rather than failing catastrophically under 
realistic perturbations.
5. Discussion
This section reflects on the core findings of our experimental eval-
uation, emphasizing the strengths, limitations, and real-world implica-
tions of the I-NAV framework. We analyze how integrating determin-
istic isochrones with probabilistic modeling enhances geolocation in 
GPS-denied environments, and outline both the technical performance 
and practical opportunities for future deployment and extension.
5.1. Key insights and system performance
Our experiments demonstrate that the I-NAV framework achieves 
high accuracy, robust probabilistic reasoning, and computational effi-
ciency, making it well-suited for deployment in GPS-denied or resource-
constrained environments.

Deterministic accuracy: The I-NAV algorithm consistently delineates 
maximum reachable regions within minutes of benchmarks provided by 
ORS and Google Maps under low-traffic conditions. Comparison with 
low-traffic condition is important because this shows the maximum 
reachability, which is the goal with the deterministic isochrone. This 
close alignment confirms that the deterministic isochrones generated 
by I-NAV effectively capture the true travel-time envelope, even when 
ground truth GPS data is unavailable.
Probabilistic precision via AGLM: The AGLM enhances the I-NAV out-
put by transforming binary reachability into a continuous probability 
field. This probabilistic representation captures inherent uncertainty 
in real-world travel, such as delays or network noise, and improves 
localization precision when augmented with user-reported directional 
and landmark data. In scenarios where only limited directional or 
landmark information is available, AGLM significantly narrows the 
probable search region while preserving smooth, continuous transitions 
in likelihood across the map.
Uncertainty quantification. As emphasized in prior work on travel time 
estimation [41], deterministic models often fail to capture the uncer-
tainty introduced by dynamic factors such as traffic fluctuations, human 
behavior, and reporting noise. The authors argue that probabilistic 
extensions are essential to provide confidence-aware predictions of 
reachable times, rather than single deterministic estimates. I-NAV’s 
AGLM addresses this challenge by assigning probability to reachable 
cells, thereby reducing the over-prediction risk inherent in binary 
isochrones. This shift from binary coverage to probabilities captures 
uncertainty, giving confidence-aware predictions rather than simple 
coverage.
Computational efficiency: Generating isochrones with I-NAV was bench-
marked and shown to support near–real-time inference. Let 𝑉 denote 
the number of vertices (grid-cell centers in the discretized road graph) 
and 𝐸 the number of edges (valid connections between neighboring 
cells). With a binary-heap implementation of Dijkstra’s search, the 
worst-case runtime is:
𝑂((𝐸+ 𝑉) log 𝑉).
This is because each of the 𝑉 vertices is removed from the priority 
queue once, every one of the 𝐸 edges is relaxed at most once, and 
each queue operation costs 𝑂(log 𝑉). In an 8-connected planar grid, 
𝐸≈8𝑉, so the algorithm scales almost linearly with map size. This 
means doubling the number of cells roughly doubles runtime, aside 
from the modest log 𝑉 factor. This efficiency is enabled by a grid-
based spatial discretization and lightweight travel-cost calculations that 
incorporate the Haversine formula, road-type-based speed adjustments, 
and turn penalties. These design choices support scalable, large-area 
geospatial computations even under constrained compute resources.
Hardware requirements: I-NAV is an offline system that produces results 
efficiently on modest hardware. All experiments were run on a standard 
workstation with an Intel Core i7 processor and 16 GB RAM. On 
this setup, the average runtime for generating a single trip isochrone 
with AGLM is between 5 and 30 s for trips of 25 min or less. This 
demonstrates that the framework can operate reliably on standard 
computers without the need for specialized hardware such as GPUs or 
high-performance servers. Aside from a standard computer, I-NAV does 
not require any specific hardware or sensors.
Real-time deployment: Many geolocation applications, such as travel 
time estimation [41], search-and-rescue modeling [29], and disaster 
response mapping [57], do not require continuous real-time data. 
Instead, they depend on reliable offline analysis. I-NAV provides this 
capability using validated datasets, while its low runtime also makes it 
practical for near real-time deployment if needed.
5.2. Constraints and limitations
While the I-NAV framework offers strong performance across a 
range of geolocation scenarios, its practical deployment must account 
for several operational and data-driven limitations.
Applicability across datasets and regions: While our validation experi-
ments use ORS and the CRAWDAD EPFLmobility datasets, the I-NAV 
framework itself is not tied to any specific dataset or mapping platform. 
The method operates on a generic road-graph abstraction and uses 
travel-time budgets defined over that graph. OSM was used in this study 
due to its global coverage, open-access availability, and reproducibility. 
However, any equivalent road-network dataset can be used without 
modification to the algorithm.
As with all routing-based systems, localization performance depends 
on the completeness and accuracy of the underlying road graph. In 
regions where road data are incomplete or outdated, inference quality 
may degrade proportionally. Recent AI-assisted mapping systems such 
as MapWithAI [58] and automated road extraction approaches such as 
RoadTracer [59] use deep neural networks and high-resolution satel-
lite imagery to detect and reconstruct missing road segments. These 
technologies significantly improve road coverage in under-mapped re-
gions. Because I-NAV operates directly on a graph abstraction rather 
than OSM-specific constructs, it can readily incorporate such enhanced 
or AI-augmented road networks. Consequently, improvements in map 
quality directly translate to improved localization performance without 
requiring structural changes to the I-NAV framework.
In our evaluation, we demonstrated applicability in both a sub-
urban U.S. setting (North Dallas, Texas) and a dense urban environ-
ment (downtown San Francisco), illustrating the framework’s robust-
ness across different geographic contexts. These results suggest that 
the methodology can be transferred to other regions provided that a 
reasonably complete road-network representation is available.
User input sparsity: AGLM relies on user-reported cues such as travel 
direction and recently passed landmarks which may be collected via 
low-bandwidth channels like SMS. These inputs are typically sparse, 
subject to human error, and may introduce ambiguity due to recall 
bias or vague descriptions. Inaccurate or missing cues can reduce the 
precision of the probability surface, particularly in dense or feature-
sparse environments. Additionally, users may input multiple cues. In 
the current implementation, if multiple cues are provided sequentially, 
the most recently reported landmark and directional cue are used as the 
active observations for inference. This design reflects the assumption 
that the latest cue best represents the user’s current context, while 
earlier cues may correspond to locations already traversed.
Incomplete or noisy road data: The quality and completeness of road 
network data — especially in remote or rapidly changing
environments — remain a significant challenge. OSM and other public 
data sources may lack critical segments or include outdated informa-
tion, limiting the model’s ability to compute accurate connectivity, cost 
propagation, and variance-based likelihood adjustments.
Environmental disruption: Natural hazards such as flooding, landslides, 
or collapsed infrastructure can render road networks temporarily or 
permanently inaccessible. Because I-NAV operates on a static road 
graph, it may overestimate reachability if real-time disruptions are not 
integrated. Mainstream geolocation services such as Google Maps may 
become unreliable when GPS signals are lost or cellular towers fail.
Real-time deployment challenges: To be useful in time-critical appli-
cations such as search and rescue or emergency triage, I-NAV must 
produce results with low latency. However, the integration of adaptive 
variance modeling, directional filtering, and edge decay adds computa-
tional complexity. These processes will enrich I-NAV’s performance at 
the cost of higher computational complexity.

5.3. Future directions
Building on the I-NAV framework’s core capabilities, several av-
enues exist for extending its functionality and impact across domains 
that require geospatial reasoning in uncertain or GPS-denied environ-
ments.
Feature expansion: Integrating additional data layers, such as popu-
lation density, land use, vegetation coverage, or animal movement 
patterns, can enhance the contextual accuracy of likelihood estimates. 
These features may improve accessibility modeling for both human and 
wildlife mobility studies.
Machine learning integration: Incorporating learning-based methods
could allow the AGLM to dynamically tune feature weights based 
on real-time sensor inputs, historical movement patterns, or terrain 
classifications. This would enable adaptive behavior across diverse 
geographies and use cases.
Multimodal travel modeling: Future extensions may incorporate walk-
ing, cycling, and off-road mobility into the travel cost function. Sup-
porting mixed-mode transit is especially relevant in rural and disaster-
stricken areas where conventional vehicles are not always available.

5.4. Potential application domains
While not yet implemented at scale, several applications can benefit 
from our integrated geolocation framework:
Public health and one health initiatives: I-NAV can support accessibil-
ity mapping for resource delivery and zoonotic disease surveillance. 
Many regions of sub-Saharan Africa are at risk for zoonotic disease 
spillover events; high biodiversity of wildlife overlapping with dense 
human populations creates opportunities for interactions between wild 
animals and humans, as well as wild animals and domestic animals. 
Pastoralist communities are also widespread in sub-Saharan Africa; 
their nomadic lifestyle necessitating creative solutions to human health-
care and veterinary care delivery. A One Health approach [60–62] to 
zoonotic disease prevention and control requires surveillance of both 
human and animal disease, but this type of care can be inaccessible 
as travel is difficult between hard-to-reach rural communities and 
hospitals or clinics. Harnessing the existing infrastructure of simple 
button cell phones in these rural regions will be invaluable in the 
efforts to reduce infectious disease outbreaks. Equipping community 
health workers with a free system of symptom reporting using their 
simple button phones and integrating these reports into I-NAV can 
inform risk mapping and intervention planning in sub-Saharan Africa 
and similar settings worldwide. By estimating the probable location 
of a symptomatic individual based on minimal input, I-NAV enables 
targeted response even in areas without GPS or sensor infrastructure.
Drone surveillance and mapping: Building on prior efforts [63], our 
framework enables real-time path prediction and probabilistic area 
coverage for drones operating in GPS-denied environments using visual 
cues and environmental features.
Disaster response and emergency routing: In post-disaster areas with 
damaged GPS infrastructure, I-NAV can provide robust fallback navi-
gation for rescue teams, aiding triage and evacuation planning [57].
Search-and-rescue in remote areas: I-NAV can estimate reachable zones 
from witness reports using direction and travel time, helping responders 
narrow down the target zone efficiently [64]. This research should 
include both land and water transportation modes.
Autonomous vehicle recovery: When UAVs or other autonomous systems 
lose GPS lock, I-NAV may be able to backtrack flight paths using 
speed, heading, and duration. This can assist search and recovery oper-
ations [65], with optional cryptographic extensions [66]. Although this 
paper focuses on ground-based automotive applications, extending I-
NAV to three-dimensional aerial scenarios remains a promising avenue 
for future research.
Integration with vision-based cues. Beyond geospatial road-network data 
and questionnaire inputs, future versions of I-NAV could incorporate 
image or computer vision–based cues. For example, a user might cap-
ture a photograph of their surroundings, which can then be cross-
checked against known environmental layers such as vegetation in-
dices, land use, or built-environment features. This would enable cross-
modal validation of the reported landmark or direction, improving 
robustness in cases where textual or categorical cues are noisy. Ad-
vances in lightweight image classification and feature-matching models 
make such cross-checks feasible even on resource-constrained devices, 
offering an additional stream of evidence for probabilistic localization.
Extraplanetary applications. The principles of I-NAV are not limited to 
terrestrial contexts. On planetary bodies such as the Moon or Mars, 
where GPS does not exist and infrastructure is sparse, landmark-based 
geolocation offers a promising navigation paradigm. Known craters, 
ridgelines, or instrumented beacons could serve as landmarks, with 
travel-time budgets derived from rover mobility models. By extend-
ing I-NAV’s landmark-anchored and grid-based reasoning to such do-
mains, the framework could support future exploration, planetary map-
ping, and autonomous navigation tasks in extraterrestrial environments 
where conventional localization tools are infeasible, given that velocity 
conditions are available.
6. Conclusion
This paper introduced the I-NAV framework, a scalable geoloca-
tion solution for GPS-denied environments where traditional meth-
ods fail due to sparse infrastructure, dynamic conditions, or lack of 
endpoint knowledge. By integrating grid-based isochrone generation 
with a modified Dijkstra algorithm and formulating geolocation as 
a Bayesian inference problem through the AGLM (Adaptive Gaussian 
Likelihood Model), I-NAV provides deterministic reachability together 
with continuous probabilistic localization.
Experimental evaluation shows that I-NAV can perform accurate 
offline geolocation on a standard laptop (Intel Core i7, 16 GB RAM). 
For a 10-minute travel-time budget in North Dallas, the deterministic 
isochrone produced by the algorithm closely matches the corresponding 
ORS region (IoU = 0.795, precision = 0.848, recall = 0.928), while ex-
plicitly identifying reachable road segments rather than only a general 
coverage region.
Evaluation on real taxi trips in downtown San Francisco further 
demonstrates the effectiveness of the probabilistic layer. After data-
driven parameter calibration, the model consistently concentrated
probability near the true endpoint and outperformed several baseline 
probabilistic localization methods across multiple evaluation metrics. 
Additional perturbation experiments showed that localization degrades 
gradually under increasing travel-time noise and landmark uncertainty, 
indicating that I-NAV remains usable under moderate input ambiguity 
while clearly defining its operational limits.

In summary, I-NAV (i) produces isochrones comparable to com-
mercial routing engines while operating fully offline, (ii) provides a 
Bayesian probabilistic inference layer that integrates travel-time and 
directional observation likelihoods to capture route uncertainty, and 
(iii) demonstrates robust localization performance across real-world 
trajectory datasets and baseline comparisons.
These properties make I-NAV promising for applications such as 
public health, emergency response, and search-and-rescue operations in 
GPS-denied environments. Future work will explore machine learning 
integration, multimodal travel support, and expanded contextual inputs 
to further enhance predictive capability and adaptability.
