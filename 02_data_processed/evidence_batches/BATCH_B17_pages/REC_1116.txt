Abstract
Autonomous monitoring is essential for precision agriculture in greenhouses, yet deploying
unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) in confined, GPS-denied environments remains limited by
payload, power, and cost constraints. This study developed and validated an autonomous
UAV system for reliable, low-cost operation in such conditions. The proposed system
employs a dual-link edge-computing architecture: a lightweight onboard controller handles
flight control and sensor acquisition, while visual simultaneous localization and mapping
(V-SLAM) is offloaded to an edge computer via the FPV video link. Phenotyping (flower
detection and tracking/counting) is performed offline from the side-view RGB stream
and does not participate in the flight control loop. Using muskmelon (Cucumis melo L.)
flower development as a case study, the UAV autonomously executed daily missions
for 27 days in a commercial greenhouse, performing flower detection and tracking to
monitor phenological dynamics. Localization and control accuracy were evaluated against
a validated UWB reference system, achieving 5.4~8.0 cm 2D RMSE for trajectory tracking
and 12.7 cm translation RMSE for greenhouse mapping. This work demonstrates a practical
architecture for autonomous monitoring in GPS-denied agricultural environments, with
operational boundaries characterized through the sustained field deployment. The system’s
design principles may extend to other indoor or communication-limited scenarios requiring
lightweight, intelligent robotic operation.

1. Introduction
Integrated sensing and control platforms support the development of data-driven
greenhouse management. Internet of Things (IoT) sensors and mobile monitoring systems
have emerged as crucial technologies for greenhouse data acquisition. Fixed IoT sensors
can continuously monitor environmental parameters for plant management, while mobile
platforms offer high-resolution sensing and real-time information for crop monitoring
instead of fixed stations [1]. Within the greenhouse, automated guided vehicles (AGVs)
have been developed and proven proficient in data collection, such as pepper fruit counting
with three-dimensional scanning and machine vision-based leaf density estimation for
spraying decisions in tomato production [2,3]. Autonomous unmanned aerial vehicles
(UAVs) offer a complementary mobile platform that can address some of these ground-
based limitations through three-dimensional mobility. A UAV system provides flexibility
and lower costs for continuous spatial data collection; it is also a highly modular platform
capable of performing various tasks, making it suitable for multiple imaging systems [4,5],
such as multispectral imaging [6], RGB imaging [7], or thermal imaging [8]. The adoption
of UAV technology in agriculture has expanded rapidly [9], with applications in green-
houses including environmental monitoring, communication assessment, and pollination
studies [10–13].
The greenhouse environment presents various challenges for autonomous UAV op-
eration beyond those encountered in other indoor settings. These include GPS-denied
environments, restricted maneuverability and payload budgets in confined corridors, tem-
poral scene changes driven by plant growth and variable illumination, and the need for
perception outputs reliable enough to support agronomic decisions.
Navigation in GPS-denied greenhouses is further complicated by the propagation
environment.
Dense vegetation with high water content affects RF signal propaga-
tion while metallic structural elements and irrigation infrastructure introduce multipath
reflections [14,15], limiting the reliability of RF-based positioning. Although greenhouse
flight paths typically remain consistent and predictable, plant growth and changing plant
morphology significantly change the visual landscape over time and variable lighting
conditions further complicate vision-based navigation methods [16].
Unlike ground-based platforms, UAVs require additional sensing and control mea-
sures to maintain flight stability [17]. Sensor payloads impact flight endurance through
added weight and increased onboard power consumption [18,19], while larger multirotor
configurations that accommodate heavier computer hardware sacrifice maneuverability
and increase system costs substantially [20]. Autonomous navigation and real-time per-
ception demand significant onboard computational resources, creating tensions between
processing requirements and the size, weight, and power constraints of practical aerial
platforms [21]. For small to medium-scale greenhouse operations, cost-effectiveness repre-
sents a critical threshold [22]. Beyond platform constraints, the monitoring system must
ultimately produce phenotyping outputs reliable enough to inform agronomic decisions,
even when perception is degraded by the environmental and computational limitations
described above.
In GPS-denied environments, validated navigation methods include motion-capture-
based indoor positioning [23], ultra-wideband (UWB) positioning [24], and self-sensing vi-
sual simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) techniques [25]. While infrastructure-
based positioning systems (e.g., motion capture, UWB) can provide precise localization,
they demand extensive installation and calibration, which limits deployment flexibil-
ity, especially for small to medium-scale operations. Onboard sensing approaches offer
infrastructure-free alternatives, though with distinct trade-offs: LiDAR-based methods
provide robust mapping but impose substantial payload and cost burdens [26], while stereo

remaining vision-based solutions, monocular SLAM presents a pragmatic balance in cost,
size, and integration simplicity [30–32], though it lacks metric scale without supplementary
sensors and remains sensitive to feature-poor or visually changing environments.
Recent studies have explored V-SLAM on agricultural platforms. Krul et al. [33]
demonstrated UAV flight feasibility in a greenhouse but without autonomous navigation or
multi-day deployment. Sukvichai et al. [34] applied ORB-SLAM for greenhouse mapping
yet did not progress to autonomous flight. Islam et al. [35] developed a stereo SLAM
system for agricultural navigation on a ground vehicle, avoiding the payload constraints
inherent to aerial platforms. No prior study has combined autonomous V-SLAM-based
UAV navigation with sustained multi-day deployment and integrated phenotyping under
the compute and payload constraints of a small aerial platform. Given these trade-offs,
monocular V-SLAM was selected as the core navigation strategy in this study.
Using muskmelon flower cultivation as a case study, the system executed daily au-
tonomous missions over 27 consecutive days in a commercial greenhouse, characterizing
both navigation reliability and phenotyping feasibility under real growing conditions.
Navigation accuracy was evaluated against an independent UWB reference with quantified
measurement uncertainty. The decoupled architecture serves two purposes: autonomous
navigation under stringent compute constraints, and consistent imagery acquisition for
longitudinal phenotyping. The main contributions of this work are: (1) a constraint-
driven, modular architecture for greenhouse UAV monitoring, where payload, compute,
and cost constraints shaped each design choice, including dual-camera decoupling, edge-
offloaded V-SLAM, and offline phenotyping; (2) operational characterization of a green-
house UAV through a 27-day field campaign, covering navigation performance across
trajectory types, sustained daily operability, and sensitivity to environmental variation; and
(3) the proof-of-concept flower counting pipeline supports temporal trend recovery despite
systematic undercounting.
This paper first describes the UAV system architecture, including hardware integration,
sensor configurations, and computational frameworks for real-time processing. It then
details the navigation and localization strategies, with a focus on Visual SLAM-based
pose estimation and flight control mechanisms. The experimental setup is introduced
next, outlining the greenhouse environment, data collection procedures, and validation
methods. This is followed by an analysis of UAV navigation performance and flower
detection experiments, evaluating system accuracy, tracking reliability, and growth pattern
analysis. The paper concludes with a discussion of operational boundaries, design trade-
offs, limitations, and deployment feasibility.
2. Materials and Methods
2.1. UAV System Architecture and Design
This study presents an integrated methodology for autonomous greenhouse moni-
toring using a UAV platform. Designed to operate effectively in GPS-denied greenhouse
environments, the platform was tested at the National Taiwan University Experimental
Farm in a muskmelon greenhouse, as shown in Figure 1a. The system architecture con-
sists of three primary components: a field-deployed UAV, online processing, and offline
analytics. The UAV platform integrates multiple sensing modalities, comprising two RGB
cameras, an optical flow sensor, and a rangefinder. Its compact dimensions (34 cm × 31 cm
× 26 cm) and total mass of 1210 g are optimized for navigation in confined greenhouse
spaces. The UAV is powered by a 2200 mAh lithium-ion battery, as illustrated in Figure 1b,
with the control and processing units detailed in Figure 1c.

2.2.2. Vision-Based Pose Estimator
The pose estimation system comprises two components: onboard localization, which
fuses data from the rangefinder and optical flow for flight control, and the Visual SLAM
framework, which serves as the primary source for global localization, mission guidance,
and greenhouse map construction.
An optical flow sensor is used for onboard localization based on the brightness con-
stancy assumption, which states that a pixel’s intensity remains constant over time. How-
ever, airflow disturbances can affect barometer readings in indoor environments, making
precise altitude estimation challenging. To address this, a ToF range finder is integrated for
height estimation. The sensor fusion process utilizes an extended Kalman filter on the flight
controller, combined with the PX4 drone pose estimation module. This onboard localization
system is integrated with the fast control loop to improve real-time navigation accuracy.
For global positioning, the system implements Visual SLAM through an OpenVS-
LAM architecture, selected for its highly modular framework and compatibility with
mobile devices compared to ORB-SLAM2 and ORB-SLAM3 [37].
The OpenVSLAM
architecture includes three distinct components: tracking, map-building, and global
optimization modules.
Visual data is acquired using a wide-angle First Person View (FPV) camera operating
at 1080 p and 60 Hz, received with the Avatar FPV VRX, and transmitted to the edge com-
puting unit via an HDMI capture card. The images are rescaled to 720 p resolution, and the
frame rate is resampled to 15 fps, utilizing a pinhole camera model with integrated OpenCV
distortion correction algorithms for real-time operation through a customized ROS 2 node.
For the SLAM framework, the tracking system detects and matches keypoint features using
the FAST (Features from Accelerated Segment Test) corner detection method, with an initial
threshold of 20 and a minimum threshold of 7 to ensure robust feature detection under
greenhouse lighting conditions. The companion computer and the edge computing unit
are connected through ROS 2 under the Eclipse Cyclone DDS (Data Distribution Service)
middleware, allowing the position controller to process the onboard localization and global
localization in real time. Through monitoring, the location is updated every 126 ms. For
reproducibility, V-SLAM pose estimates are computed in the camera optical frame by the
edge computer and published to the companion computer in the ROS 2 ENU convention;
MAVROS converts these to the PX4 NED body-frame convention before forwarding to the
flight controller. Time alignment between the companion computer and PX4 is maintained
through the MAVLink TIMESYNC protocol. When off-board V-SLAM pose updates are
unavailable, control falls back to onboard optical-flow-based position hold; if the off-board
source timeout exceeds 2 s, the mission controller initiates autonomous landing.

2.3. Validating UAV Performance Through Muskmelon Flower Detection
2.3.1. Overview of the Image Processing Pipeline
In this study, muskmelon (Cucumis melo L.) flower detection was selected as a case
study to validate the UAV’s navigation performance and assess its feasibility in greenhouse
plant monitoring applications. Muskmelon is an ideal candidate due to its economic
importance, relatively short growth cycle, and sensitivity to environmental factors. It is
frequently studied to optimize irrigation strategies, fertilizer management, and greenhouse
environmental control [38,39]. Among the phenotypic traits influencing plant growth,
flowers are recognized as key indicators [40]. Variations in the timing and number of flowers
significantly affect pollination success [41]. However, monitoring floral development is
labor-intensive, making UAVs a promising tool for automating this process.
The overall experimental workflow is illustrated in Figure 6. Initially, raw footage
from the UAV, combined with gyroscopic log data, is processed to correct lens distortion
based on camera intrinsic parameters. Since frame quality significantly impacts detection
and tracking performance, the footage utilizes gyroscopic stabilization processes from the
Gyroflow library, which employs onboard sensor data to enhance video quality. Subse-
quently, the stabilized frames are fed into a plant-tracking module designed to identify
and track individual plants over consecutive image sequences. Each identified plant is
assigned a unique ID, and frames corresponding to each plant are extracted as separate
image sequences. In the next phase, flower tracking is performed on the plant-specific
image sequences using YOLO detection and trajectory grouping methods. The results from
this process are then associated with the respective plant IDs established in the previous
tracking step. The subsequent sections describe each stage in detail.

The muskmelon flower detection utilizes two specialized models: one for plant detec-
tion and another for flower detection, both based on the YOLOv8 architecture but optimized
with different configurations for their specific tasks. The plant detection model is built on

patience window of 100 epochs was configured but not triggered in either case.
2.3.3. Determination of Flower Count from Footage
The flower counting process consists of six sequential stages: (1) plant instance iden-
tification and tracking, (2) flower tracking with low threshold parameters, (3) optical
flow-based movement compensation, (4) trajectory analysis, (5) spatial clustering, and
(6) flower count determination.
Initially, individual plant instances are identified and tracked across frames using
the default BOT-SORT configuration with the YOLOv8 detector. For each tracked plant, a
corresponding mask is applied to extract the region of interest, and all frames in which that
plant appears are saved in a dedicated folder to streamline subsequent processing. Next, in
the flower detection and tracking process, YOLOv8 is employed with a deliberately low
confidence threshold (τ = 0.2) to enhance sensitivity to small flower features; the BOT-SORT
configuration is adjusted by implementing a detection confidence threshold of 0.3, an IoU
matching threshold of 0.2, a minimum hit requirement of 2 frames for track confirmation,
and a maximum track age of 15 frames. Although this approach ensures that most flowers
are captured, it may introduce tracking instabilities and false positives. To mitigate these
issues, a movement compensation mechanism is applied using the Lucas-Kanade optical
flow method. Global frame motion is estimated across a grid of points defined as:

2.4. Experimental Setup
2.4.1. UAV Performance Evaluation—UWB Reference and Uncertainty Modeling
The UAV navigation experiments and muskmelon flower counting experiments were
conducted to evaluate the system’s localization accuracy, flight stability, and the effective-
ness of automated flower detection and tracking in a greenhouse environment. Experiments
were performed in a 7 m × 10 m greenhouse featuring three parallel racks, each contain-
ing five muskmelon plants (Figure 7). Three positioning-related elements served distinct
roles in these experiments: onboard V-SLAM provided operational navigation during
autonomous flight (Section 2.2); marker boards on the greenhouse racks contributed ad-
ditional visual features to the mapping environment; and an external UWB positioning

(defined by 0.2 s windowed σ > 8 cm) comprised 8.8–10.9% of path length and were flagged
during evaluation.

= 1.80–16.40 cm. Across all runs, the pipeline produced 1737 filtered reference points
used in evaluation. These post-processed UWB measurements serve as a metrologically
independent reference for Visual SLAM evaluation in the greenhouse.
Marker boards were present on the greenhouse racks during the experimental period.
OpenVSLAM processes these patterns as generic visual features; no ArUco-specific de-

were obtained in this marker-present environment; the system’s sensitivity to visual feature
density is discussed in the Conclusions. The study evaluated three flight path designs to
assess system performance: (1) a linear path, following a straight-line route along each rack;
(2) a zigzag path, alternating between racks; and (3) a loop path, forming a continuous
circuit around all racks, as illustrated in Figure 7. These flight pattern tests provided practi-

environments. The results helped identify the flight path that balanced coverage reliability
and navigation stability.
2.4.2. Setup for Autonomous Navigation Mission
The autonomous flight operation consists of three sequential stages: map creation,
mission planning, and mission execution. In the initial map creation step, the UAV gen-
erates a spatial map of the operational area using Visual SLAM. To ensure accurate real-
world dimensions, a scale factor is calculated by comparing vertical measurements from a
rangefinder to the corresponding SLAM-estimated heights. During the mission planning
step, users manually select key points from the previously generated SLAM map and create
a list of waypoints using the line fitting and resampling method mentioned in the previous
section. In the final mission execution phase, the UAV autonomously navigates along
the predefined waypoints. The mission controller continuously evaluates the difference
between the UAV’s current position (provided by the off-board pose estimator) and the
target waypoint coordinates, adjusting its flight path through PID-based cascaded control
loops. As each waypoint is reached within predefined positional tolerances, the UAV pro-
gresses to the next waypoint, concluding the mission upon reaching the final point. Each
daily mission required approximately 30–60 min of preparation, including edge computer

initialization, network verification, and a SLAM map pre-build process (~10 min), followed
by 4–6 min of flight per 2200 mAh battery.
2.4.3. Flower Counting During Muskmelon Plant Growth
Field data collection was conducted in a muskmelon greenhouse from 30 July to 25
August 2024, spanning the vegetative through flowering stages until fruit set initiation.
The experimental setup comprised three hydroponic racks, with each rack supporting
five plants connected to a shared nutrient delivery system. The automated UAV platform
developed in this study was used to monitor flower development patterns. Daily mon-
itoring missions were conducted as independent flight operations, each requiring fresh
system initialization to ensure no localization drift accumulated between sessions. The
UAV executed predefined linear flight paths at a fixed height of 115 cm above ground level,
systematically collecting imagery data along each greenhouse aisle to capture all plant
racks. Flower counts per plant were quantified through an automated image processing
pipeline, and the temporal flowering data were analyzed using the Gompertz growth
model [45]. To validate system performance, daily manual flower counts were conducted
alongside detailed documentation of flower emergence times and pollination timing when
bees were introduced into the greenhouse. These manual observations served as ground
truth data for evaluating the automated monitoring system’s reliability.

moderate but consistent challenges to pose estimation without compromising worst-case

changes result in the highest RMSE (8.0 cm) despite a shorter path length compared to the
zigzag pattern. In contrast, the zigzag trajectory benefits from longer straight segments
between turns, allowing the SLAM system to maintain feature correspondence and stabilize
pose estimates.

3.3.2. Results of Muskmelon Flower Counting
Muskmelon plant tracking achieved a Multi-Object Tracking Accuracy (MOTA) of
72.4%, with a False Negative Rate (FNR) of 17.96%, a False Positive Rate (FPR) of 8.6%, and

a FPR of 10.0%, and an IDSW rate of 8.7%, prioritizing tracking precision over detection
coverage. To address occlusion and ID switches, the system applies a 20% confidence
threshold and optical flow compensation. An example of the flower counting workflow
is illustrated in Figure 12. The trajectories of flowers 4 and 5 merge into a single cluster

4. Conclusions
This study developed and validated an integrated UAV imaging platform for green-
house monitoring under GPS-denied, compute-constrained conditions. The system inte-
grated edge-based navigation with a dual-camera decoupled strategy: a forward V-SLAM
stream for localization and a side-view RGB stream for phenotyping within the payload
constraints of a sub-1.3 kg platform. In greenhouse mapping, the V-SLAM framework
achieves a translation RMSE of 12.7 cm. Over autonomous missions, comparative analysis
with an independent UWB positioning system confirmed trajectory RMSE of 5.4–8.0 cm at
a mean flight speed of approximately 0.13 m/s. The flower counting pipeline recovered
temporal growth trends (Gompertz R2 = 0.75–0.95) with inflection-point timing aligned
to observed female flower emergence, supporting trend-level longitudinal monitoring
despite per-frame tracking limitations. Operational boundaries characterized through this
campaign include visual feature density dependence, rotation-induced error elevation,
and compute degradation under sustained operation. The platform is deployable on com-
mercially available components and operated with a single-operator setup throughout
the campaign.
Several limitations bound these results. All experiments were conducted in a single
7 m × 10 m greenhouse, and further investigation is required to assess the system’s scala-
bility across different greenhouse configurations. The endurance evaluation was handheld
rather than in-flight. Marker boards on the racks contributed stable visual features to
the SLAM map, though a marker-absent flight remained operational with limited quality
degradation. Flower tracking MOTA of 49.1% with FN-dominant error produces systematic
undercounting (mean −1.63 flowers), and compute throughput degraded from 9 to 6 Hz
during sustained operation.
The primary contribution of this research lies in the engineering integration, implemen-
tation, and practical validation of a constraint-driven, modular architecture for greenhouse
UAV monitoring, where payload, compute, and cost constraints shaped each design choice,
including dual-camera decoupling, edge-offloaded V-SLAM, and offline phenotyping.
A second contribution is operational characterization through a 27-day field campaign
covering navigation performance across trajectory types, sustained daily operability, and
sensitivity to environmental variation. Third, the proof-of-concept flower counting pipeline
recovers temporal growth trends despite systematic undercounting, supporting temporal
trend recovery for longitudinal crop monitoring.
Future research should focus on system improvement and broader validation. Extend-
ing deployment to diverse greenhouse environments and plant species would enhance
applicability, while long-term monitoring studies across multiple growing seasons could
establish reliability. Further refinement of control precision for complex missions and
evaluation under diverse environmental conditions remain essential. Improving object
tracking through motion-model-based re-identification, as demonstrated for dynamic agri-
cultural targets by Zhai et al. [46], could address the current tracking limitations. The
integration of advanced deep learning algorithms and adaptive control mechanisms could
enhance system robustness, including yield-prediction support derived from temporal
flower-count indicators. From a practical standpoint, assessing economic benefits and
addressing maintenance challenges would accelerate commercial adoption. Scaling this
technology across various agricultural settings offers significant potential for advancing
automated greenhouse management systems.
