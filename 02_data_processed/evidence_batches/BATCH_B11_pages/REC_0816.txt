Abstract—Autonomous recovery of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles
(UAVs) in maritime environments is challenged by dynamic
ship motion, texture-poor surroundings, and vulnerabilities
inherent to Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS). To
enable robust UAV state estimation relative to a naval vessel
in GNSS-denied environments, this paper presents a novel
vision-based pose estimation framework leveraging Feature 3D
Gaussian Splatting (3DGS). Adopting STDLoc’s sparse pose
estimation framework, our approach bypasses computationally
expensive dense rendering in favor of direct sparse 2D-3D
matching. We extract 2D features from live monocular imagery
using SuperPoint and match them against a compact set of
view-consistent 3D Gaussian landmarks distilled via a Matching-
Oriented Sampling strategy. The 6-DoF camera pose is then
robustly recovered using a Perspective-n-Point (PnP) solver
augmented with Locally Optimized RANSAC (LO-RANSAC).
Experimental validation on a 1/24-scale naval Yard Patrol (YP)
craft demonstrates centimeter-level translational accuracy (1.66
cm lateral, 2.28 cm vertical mean absolute errors) and near-
degree rotational accuracy (0.90° roll, 1.17° yaw). This method
eliminates the need for physical fiducial markers or precise
CAD models, offering a scalable and highly accurate relative
localization solution for autonomous maritime operations.
Index Terms—Gaussian Splatting, Pose Estimation
I. INTRODUCTION
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) have become indispens-
able assets in modern naval strategies, executing a diverse
range of missions including over-the-horizon reconnaissance,
search-and-rescue, and logistical resupply. A pivotal phase in
these operations is the autonomous recovery of the UAV onto
a surface vessel. Unlike static land-based recovery, maritime
landings are complicated by the continuous 6-DoF motion
of the ship deck, dynamic lighting conditions, and the visual
interference of ocean waves. To ensure safe touchdown, the
UAV requires a robust and precise estimate of its pose relative
to the landing deck.
The views expressed in this paper are those of the authors and do not reflect

of the Navy, the Department of War, or the U.S. Government.
Conventionally, relative positioning relies heavily on Real-
Time Kinematic (RTK) Global Navigation Satellite Systems
(GNSS). While RTK provides centimeter-level accuracy, it
introduces a critical single point of failure. In contested
operational theaters, GNSS signals are highly susceptible
to jamming, denial-of-service, and spoofing attacks, which
can induce catastrophic pose drift during the terminal landing
phase [1]. Furthermore, the requirement for a continuous data
link to transmit RTK corrections between the ship and UAV
creates an electromagnetic signature that compromises the
vessel’s stealth. These vulnerabilities have accelerated the
demand for passive, vision-based navigation systems capable
of operating in GNSS-denied environments.
Vision-based pose estimation generally falls into three
paradigms: marker-based, feature-based, and learning-based
approaches. Marker-based methods, which utilize fiducials
such as ArUco tags [2], offer robust geometric solutions but
impose significant logistical burdens. They require the installa-
tion of large, high-contrast markers on the ship deck—which
may not be feasible on all vessels—and are prone to failure
if the markers are occluded or damaged [3]. Feature-based
methods, such as Visual-Inertial Odometry (VIO), track
natural landmarks to estimate motion [4]. However, maritime
environments are notoriously texture-poor; the sky and water
offer few stable features, and the ship’s appearance changes
drastically with lighting and wetness, leading to tracking
instability.
Recent advancements in deep learning have introduced
direct pose regression [5] and keypoint detection networks
[6]. While these methods improve robustness against envi-
ronmental noise, they typically require massive, manually
annotated datasets or highly detailed Computer Aided Design
(CAD) models to generate synthetic training data. Creating
a photorealistic CAD model of a specific naval vessel is a
labor-intensive, expensive process that does not scale well to
new ship classes.

To address the limitations of geometric reliance and CAD-
dependency, this work leverages 3D Gaussian Splatting
(3DGS) [7] as a novel scene representation for maritime
pose estimation. Unlike implicit representations like Neural
Radiance Fields (NeRFs) [8], which are computationally
heavy to query, 3DGS represents the scene as a collection of
explicit 3D Gaussians. This allows for photorealistic rendering
and, crucially, can be constructed rapidly from a set of 2D
photographs without manual mesh modeling.
In this paper, we propose a method that utilizes a pre-
trained Feature Gaussian [9] representation of the target
vessel to estimate the UAV’s relative pose, as illustrated
in Fig. 1. We adopt the sparse localization stage of the
STDLoc framework, which treats 3D Gaussians not just
as visual primitives, but as semantic landmarks containing
high-dimensional feature descriptors [10]. To achieve robust
performance without the computational overhead of full
rendering, we bypass dense rasterization and instead employ
a direct 2D-3D matching approach. We utilize the Matching-
Oriented Sampling strategy to distill millions of Gaussians
into a compact set of distinct, view-consistent landmarks
[10]. During operation, a SuperPoint [11] network extracts
sparse keypoints and descriptors from the live UAV camera
feed. These features are matched directly to the 3D Gaussian
landmarks to solve the 6-DoF pose via a Perspective-n-Point
(PnP) solver augmented with Locally Optimized RANSAC
(LO-RANSAC) [12] for robust outlier rejection. This approach
eliminates the need for physical markers or pre-existing CAD
files, relying instead on a representation that can be generated
from simple inspection drone footage.
The outline of the paper is as follows. Section II presents
the methodology detailing 3DGS [7], Feature 3DGS [9],
and STDLoc sparse pose estimation [10]. Section III details
hardware, data collection, experimental results, and an analysis
of the pose estimation performance. Finally, Section IV
concludes the paper in the context of ongoing and future
work.
II. METHOD
In this work, we propose a camera localization pipeline
(Fig. 2) that combines 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [7] with
semantic feature fields (Feature 3DGS) [9] to enable efficient
2D–3D matching. We first establish the theoretical foundations
of 3DGS and its extension to Feature 3DGS, followed by
our joint training methodology. The remainder of the pipeline
adopts the sparse pose estimation framework from STDLoc
[10]. Specifically, we describe STDLoc’s Matching-Oriented
Sampling strategy used to distill the dense model into a sparse
set of persistent 3D landmarks. Finally, we detail the live pose
estimation step: SuperPoint [11] extracts 2D features from a
query image to match against these 3D landmarks, robustly
recovering the 6-DoF camera pose via a Perspective-n-Point
(PnP) solver augmented with Locally Optimized RANSAC
(LO-RANSAC) [13].
A. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)
We represent the underlying scene geometry and appearance
using 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [7]. Unlike traditional
implicit neural representations, 3DGS models the scene as an
unstructured set of 3D Gaussians, typically initialized from a
sparse point cloud produced by Structure-from-Motion (SfM)
reconstruction [14]. Each Gaussian primitive is parameterized
by a center position x ∈R3, a 3D covariance matrix
Σ ∈R3×3, an opacity value α ∈R, and a set of spherical
harmonics (SH) coefficients up to third order, which are
evaluated to compute the view-dependent color c ∈R3.
To ensure the covariance matrix remains physically
valid—specifically, positive semi-definite—during gradient-
based optimization, it is decomposed into a scaling matrix
S ∈R3×3 and a rotation matrix R ∈R3×3 such that [7]:
Σ = RSST RT .
(1)
In practice, the rotation matrix R is parameterized by a unit
quaternion q ∈R4, and the scaling matrix S is represented as
a diagonal matrix parameterized by a scaling vector s ∈R3.
The computational efficiency of 3DGS is largely driven
by its tile-based rasterization architecture, which maps 3D
Gaussian ellipsoids into 2D splats that are subsequently
blended across the image plane. To project the 3D covariance
matrix Σ into 2D pixel coordinates, the pipeline uses an
affine approximation. Assuming W ∈SO(3) is the rotational
component of the world-to-camera viewing transformation and
J ∈R3×3 is the Jacobian of the projective transformation, the
resulting 2D covariance Σ′ ∈R2×2 is determined as follows
[7]:
Σ′ = JWΣW T JT .
(2)
This matrix dictates the spatial footprint of the splat in image
space.

onto the 2D image plane and employs a point-based α-

standard 3DGS framework is extended to support semantic

C. Training Methodology
The Feature 3DGS model is trained by jointly optimizing
the radiance field and the feature field. The radiance field
loss Lrgb combines the L1-measure of the photometric loss
L1 and the Structural Dissimilarity (D-SSIM) loss LD-SSIM
between the ground truth training image I and the rendered
image ˆI [7]:
Lrgb = (1 −λ)L1(I, ˆI) + λLD-SSIM(I, ˆI),
(5)
where the weight hyperparameter is empirically set to λ = 0.2.
For the feature field, we denote Ft(I) ∈R256×H′×W ′
as the dense feature map extracted from a training image
I ∈R3×H×W using a SuperPoint encoder [11]. Here, 256
represents the local feature dimensionality, while the coarse
resolution (H′, W ′) corresponds to (H/8, W/8) due to the
encoder’s inherent downsampling factor. To ensure spatial
alignment for the loss computation, we upsample the extracted
ground truth feature map Ft(I) to the target rendering
resolution H × W via bilinear interpolation. The feature
loss Lf is then defined by the scalar L1 distance between the
upsampled ground truth feature map and the rendered feature
map Fs(ˆI) [9]:
Lf = |(Ft(I) −Fs(ˆI))|1,
(6)
where the notation |a −b|1 represents the scalar L1 norm.
Finally, the total loss L is formulated as an equal-weighted
combination of the radiance field loss (Eq. (5)) and the feature
loss (Eq. (6): L = Lrgb + Lf [9].
D. Matching-Oriented Sampling
A fully trained Feature 3DGS scene G contains an over-
whelming number of primitives, making exhaustive 2D–3D
matching computationally infeasible and highly susceptible
to ambiguities. To efficiently select a robust subset of highly
recognizable and distinct Gaussians, we employ a Matching-
Oriented Sampling strategy inspired by STDLoc [10].

gi. We project the center of gi onto every training image I in
which it is visible (denoted as the set Vi) to obtain its 2D pixel
coordinates (ui, vi). We then compute the cosine similarity

To ensure the selected landmarks are uniformly distributed

curated, sparse set of landmarks ˜G is highly distinct and
optimized for cross-modal matching.
E. Sparse Pose Estimation
In the sparse localization stage, given an unseen query
image Iq, we use the SuperPoint network [11] to extract sparse
2D keypoints and their corresponding feature descriptors. To
establish 2D–3D correspondences, we compute the cosine
similarity between these extracted 2D query features and the
3D feature descriptors associated with our sampled landmarks
˜G. For each 2D feature, the 3D landmark with the highest
similarity is selected as a match.
To estimate the six degrees of freedom (6DoF) camera
pose from these correspondences, we utilize a Perspective-
n-Point (PnP) solver. Because the matched set invariably
contains outliers, we apply Locally Optimized RANSAC (LO-
RANSAC) [12] to robustly recover the pose. While standard
RANSAC can struggle with real-world noise—often gener-
ating suboptimal hypotheses from minimal samples—LO-
RANSAC mitigates this issue by triggering an iterative local
optimization step whenever a new maximum number of
inliers is found. By iteratively refining the pose using all
currently identified inliers, LO-RANSAC yields a highly
precise camera alignment with significantly fewer overall
iterations, facilitating efficient pose estimation.
III. EXPERIMENTS
To validate the proposed Feature 3DGS pose estimation
pipeline, we conducted a series of physical experiments using
a scale-model naval vessel. This section details the hardware
platform and data collection procedures used to capture the
empirical training and testing datasets. Next, we outline the
specific software implementation details used to transition the
methodology described in Section II into a functional pipeline.
Finally, we present a comprehensive analysis of the system’s
6-DoF pose estimation accuracy, its robustness to maritime
geometric constraints, and its computational performance.
A. Hardware Overview
Our experimental platform uses a modified DJI Flamewheel
F450 drone frame with a 450 mm diagonal motor-to-motor
distance as shown in Fig. 3. The quadrotor is driven by four
DJI 2312 960 Kv motors and 420 LITE 20A electronic speed
controllers (ESCs), powered by a 5200 mAh 3-cell LiPo
battery. The avionics are centered around a Cube Blue H7
flight controller running open-source PX4 firmware, which is
responsible for low-level flight dynamics and stabilization.
Fig. 3. 450 mm Quadcopter used for data collection.
For sensory perception, an onboard Intel RealSense D435i
records 640 × 360 RGB video, which forms the visual
foundation for both the pose-estimation pipeline and Feature
3DGS scene training. To capture baseline positional data,
a 22-camera Qualisys motion capture setup monitors the
vehicle within an approximately 8 × 7 × 5 m workspace.
This ground-truth telemetry is crucial for validating pose-
estimation accuracy and providing the supervisory signal for
Feature 3DGS training. All sensor feeds are time-synchronized
and logged in a ROS 2 Humble environment hosted on a
companion NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano computer.
All experiments, including training and inference, were
conducted on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX
4090 GPU and an AMD Ryzen Threadripper 7960X CPU.
B. Data Collection
Our dataset centers on a 1/24-scale replica of a United
States Naval Academy Yard Patrol (YP) vessel shown in Fig. 4,
customized with a stern flight deck to emulate future real-
world maritime tests. The data-collection protocol involved
flying the quadcopter in two elliptical patterns around the
model at a close standoff distance of 0.5–1.0 m. The YP craft
was kept near the center of the camera frame, where occasional
partial occlusions occurring due to the tight proximity were
considered acceptable. Fig. 5 shows the flight paths used to
produce two independent sets of posed imagery: 180 frames
for training and 306 frames for testing.

noticeably noisy 3DGS reconstruction. We hypothesize this
degradation stemmed from subtle lever-arm misalignments

During the localization phase, SuperPoint [11] is again

reprojection error margin of 12.0 pixels. The iteration bounds
were set to a minimum of 1,000 and a maximum of 100,000
iterations.

As illustrated by the polar plot in Fig. 7, the longitudinal
(x) error envelope illustrated by the dashed blue convex hull
of all error measurements, mostly exceeds those of the lateral
and vertical axes. This directional imbalance is largely a
consequence of the target vessel’s structural geometry, which
induces two distinct, angle-dependent challenges for the sparse
feature matcher. From broadside and oblique vantage points
(90◦and 270◦), the x-axis runs parallel to the ship’s uniform,
elongated hull. Because this surface lacks prominent, distinct
visual gradients, the SuperPoint encoder [11] struggles to
extract unambiguous keypoints, creating a “sliding” ambiguity
where neighboring longitudinal coordinates yield highly
similar feature descriptors. Conversely, at nose-on or stern-on
angles (0◦and 180◦), the x-axis corresponds to optical depth.
Because our method relies on monocular cues, the minimal
cross-sectional profile of the vessel from these angles provides
inadequate scale constraints, magnifying minor 2D feature
location inaccuracies into more substantial longitudinal depth
shifts during the PnP calculation.
In contrast, the vertical and lateral axes are stabilized
by robust “hard-edge” features. Vertical (z) inaccuracies
are tightly constrained to an MAE of 2.28 cm because the
vessel is framed by sharp horizontal boundaries—specifically,
the waterline and the deck-to-sky transition—which provide
excellent contrast for keypoint extraction and act as strict
“clamps” on vertical positioning. Lateral (y) precision is even
stronger, achieving a 1.66 cm MAE. A lateral camera shift
generates a high degree of feature parallax when viewed from
the bow, and acts as depth to a strongly bounded, flat surface
when viewed from the broadside.
The azimuthal distribution of rotational errors is depicted in
Fig. 8. The varying precision across the orientation angles is
directly linked to the unequal geometric lever arms provided
by the 3D landmark distribution. Roll achieves the highest
accuracy (0.90◦MAE) because the prominent horizontal lines
spanning the length of the ship strongly anchor the roll axis in
the image plane. Yaw also performs exceptionally well (1.17◦
MAE) by leveraging the ship’s overall length; minor yaw
adjustments create pronounced, opposing perspective shifts at
the bow and stern, giving the PnP + LO-RANSAC solver [13]
a wide variance to tightly calculate the heading. In contrast,

IV. CONCLUSIONS AND ONGOING WORK
This paper presents a novel vision-based pose estimation
framework for autonomous maritime UAV recovery in GNSS-
denied environments. By leveraging a pre-trained Feature
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) representation of a target
vessel [9], our method enables robust 6-DoF camera lo-
calization through direct sparse 2D-3D matching. Utilizing
SuperPoint feature extraction [11] combined with a PnP and
LO-RANSAC solver [13], the pipeline successfully filters
ambiguous cross-modal matches without the computational
overhead of dense rasterization.
Experimental validation on a scale model Yard Patrol (YP)
craft demonstrated high precision, achieving centimeter-level
translational accuracy—specifically 1.66 cm and 2.28 cm
mean absolute errors (MAE) along the lateral and vertical
axes, respectively. Attitude estimation also exhibited near-
degree accuracy, highlighted by a 0.90◦MAE in roll and
1.17◦in yaw. Furthermore, our analysis identified critical
geometric constraints inherent to maritime structures, noting
that longitudinal (x-axis) estimations present the primary
source of positional uncertainty due to uniform hull textures
and limited optical depth cues at certain azimuths.
While the current methodology provides highly accurate
spatial resolution, future work must address the computational
bottlenecks associated with inference latency. The current
framework yields an average processing time of 0.21 s per
frame; adapting this system for the aggressive terminal landing
maneuvers of a UAV will require pushing this metric closer
to real-time benchmarks. To this end, ongoing research will
focus on optimizing the feature extraction and matching
phases to ensure deployment viability on Size, Weight, and
Power (SWaP)-constrained edge compute devices, such as the
NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano. Additionally, future initiatives will
transition this framework from scale models to full-scale naval
vessels, validating the system’s scalability and robustness
against the complex motion profiles, weather, and dynamic
lighting encountered in operational maritime environments.
