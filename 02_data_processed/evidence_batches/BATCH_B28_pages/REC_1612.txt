Abstract— This paper furthers the state of the art of visual
localization by harnessing recently developed scene recogni-
tion and understanding machine learning (ML) algorithms to
rethink how vision can be used for navigation, particularly
when combined with a realistic, minimalist, and robust multi-
agent approach. The focus on visual navigation comes as GPS
is increasingly unreliable (jammed, spoofed, or disabled), or
unavailable (indoor, or extraterrestrial environments).
GPS denial/unreliability is an area of active research in gen-
eral, with unmanned parafoil systems being one UAV applica-
tion. The landing locations for parafoil systems must be pre-
programmed manually with global coordinates, which may be
inaccurate or outdated, and offer no in-ﬂight adaptability.
This paper will introduce a novel and minimalist approach to vi-
sual navigation and multi-agent communication using semantic
machine learning classiﬁcation and geometric constraints. This
approach enables localization and landing site identiﬁcation for
multiple communicating UAV systems deployed in GPS-denied
environments.
This is a capability extending beyond single-
agent localization, as well as a novel landing site classiﬁcation
approach.
A mathematical foundation using information theory provides
the technical grounding for the algorithms presented, and sim-
ulation results demonstrate how multiple agents using the pro-
posed algorithms make a signiﬁcant improvement in localization
accuracy and speed over a single agent.
Desirable landing sites for UAVs generally, and autonomous
parafoils in particular, constitute locations where payload sur-
vivability and accessibility are greatest. An on-board semantic
representation and understanding of the operating environment
will allow systems to automatically detect and classify these de-
sirable and undesirable landing sites, enabling successful land-
ing in a much larger class of scenarios than currently possible, in
addition to a fail-safe capability when localization is not feasible.
This paper will further introduce an algorithm enabling multi-
ple agents to reproduce this classiﬁcation of their environment
on-line better and more rapidly than a single agent working
alone, demonstrating the advantage of multiple agents in this
context as well. This has applications to general UAV landing,
whether powered or unpowered, on Earth or in extra-terrestrial
environments.

1. INTRODUCTION
As GPS becomes increasingly unreliable for future applica-
tions, localizing without it becomes more and more crucial,
with computer vision seen as a key way forward.
This
work uses recent advances in machine learning (ML) to add
signiﬁcant capabilities to current autonomous vehicle visual-
navigation, enabling a host of new possibilities in applica-
tions ranging from indoor localization to space exploration.
The algorithms in this paper focus on improvements in visual
navigation made possible by combining two notions: (1) se-
mantically understanding the environment, and (2) leveraging
multiple agents with non-overlapping viewpoints.
Rather
than focus on vision and multiple agents as complicated
substitutes for GPS and a more capable single agent, this
work focuses on demonstrating the true potential of each.
Section 2 provides background with a brief literature review,
highlighting some limitations of current approaches.
The
general approach pursued is motivated and introduced by
example in Section 3. Mathematical foundations and intu-
itions are presented in Section 4.
Sections 5 and 6 apply
the proposed approaches to in-ﬂight imagery for a parafoil
system.
2. BACKGROUND
The problem of GPS-denied localization is far from new,
whether described as the kidnapped or lost robot, or simulta-
neous localization and mapping (SLAM) problem. Drift-free
body-frame odometry is demonstrated in [1] and follow-up
papers e.g. [2], as well as [3], and is a mature and reliable
capability. The state of the art in single-agent data associa-
tion is Fast Appearance-Based Mapping (FAB-MAP 2.0) [4]
showing impressive results for place recognition along streets
and highways. In [5], GPS-denied localization in an urban
environment is achieved using a Gaussian mixture model. A
key idea in both of these works is assuming a discretized
and structured environment map.
However, dealing with
massive maps, different camera viewpoints, branching data
(i.e. free space, rather than street routes), and environments
with severe perceptual aliasing remains an open challenge.
Ensuring multiple agents associate data correctly when over-
lapping landmarks are observed is addressed in e.g.
[6]
[7] using triangulation and consensus, respectively. These
approaches require sufﬁcient and regular observation overlap

Back-end estimation is typically considered for both single

less suitable for a completely lost agent.
Multi-agent statistical consistency (estimators failing to ac-

keeping to accurately track the ﬂow of information and mea-
surements amongst agents, or conservative covariance inter-
section and heuristic approaches. Estimation of every other
agent’s state and receiving labeled measurements from every
other agent at each time step is often required. Changing
numbers of agents, intermittent communications, or realistic
data transmission constraints are rarely considered.
In summary, the primary contribution of this paper beyond
previously reported work is providing a clear example of
multi-agent localization algorithms with realistic communi-
cation constraints, which demonstrate how multiple agents
without overlapping views can be more capable than a single
agent working alone in the scenario where agents are com-
pletely lost in environments with severe perceptual aliasing.
3. MOTIVATION & PROPOSED APPROACH
Minimalist Assumptions
One of the major issues in both localization and multi-agent
work is the hidden assumptions.
Common unstated, but
substantial, assumptions include having already established
a common reference frame (such as knowing global head-
ing), extensive communication capabilities where each agent
knows each other agent’s complete state every time step, or
some kind of central processing location, or framework.
We start with the following minimal assumptions about each
agent’s capabilities:
• Reliable body-frame odometry
• Altitude estimation (scaling)
• Nadir pose estimation (projection)
• Radio communication, RF-ranging (scalar distance to other
agents)
Thus 3 of the 6 degrees of freedom are known (global altitude,
pitch, and roll angles), while global 2-D location (latitude
and longitude) and heading must be estimated. Agents are
fully decentralized, no central processor or pre-existing local
communication network, no beacons. These assumptions are
consistent with current UAV and parafoil system capabilities.
Key Algorithmic Ideas
The following two ideas motivate the proposed approach:
1. Visual localization should be more capable than GPS:
classify environment using semantic scene recognition and
understanding
2. Multiple agents working together should be better than a
single agent: use scalar geometric distance information be-
tween agents and share minimalist, statistically independent
observations
Scene Recognition / Understanding
A major challenge for visual localization is perceptual
aliasing–images of multiple locations look the same, or
images of same location look different.
State of the art
approaches to solving this involve using back-end analysis
to eliminate spurious measurements, which requires already
having a fairly good estimate of one’s location. On the front-
end data association side, high dimensional image descriptors
of various ﬂavors attempt to capture enough unique detail to
disambiguate each location. This demands expensive map
storage, computation, and communication, and assumes all
locations can be visually distinguished uniquely. Approaches
such those detailed in [4] rely on approximation tricks to
structure these high dimensional classiﬁers and Bayesian up-
dates in ways to make the processes more real-time capable,
but do not address the fundamental underlying problems.
These descriptors have no semantic meaning, since there is
no attempt/ability to comprehend imagery: this is navigation
by seeing without understanding.
We propose a different approach predicated on a simple
notion:
multiple images of one kind of terrain may be
indistinguishable from each other, but images of different
terrain–sand and forest–can be uniquely distinguished. We
can think of accepting and exploiting perceptual aliasing,
considering it as a means for image storage compression. If
we use semantically meaningful visual recognition, we can
see and understand the environment, and combined with ge-
ometric constraints (odometry, other agents) we can localize
by matching a sequence of images with metric information to
a known map of the environment.
These ideas can be seen as a method to complement existing
place recognition algorithms, serving as a fallback for cir-
cumstances where no reliable GPS is available, in addition to
providing new capabilities.
Visual Richness
To further describe the type of environments where profound
perceptual aliasing is a structural problem, we introduce a
new concept termed visual richness (VR) deﬁned as follows:
VR: M →[0, 1] where M is a map of an environment. VR
= 0 corresponds to an environment where visual localization
is impossible (e.g. ocean, desert), while VR = 1 signiﬁes an
environment where every location is visually unique (labeled
city map).
The high dimensional descriptor approaches
(whether using a visual bag of words [4] or neural network
layers [26] [27] endeavor to push all environments close to
VR = 1.
But numerous real environments often have VR quite far from
1. Consider an environment of 100 discrete locations catego-
rized into one of two unique categories A and B, distributed
evenly (forest clearing). A noise-free image measurement
narrows one’s location in the environment by half (being
in the forest, or the clearing): VR =
1
2.
Thus scenarios
of structural perceptual aliasing are fundamentally different
from VR →1, and vision alone will always be insufﬁcient
for localization without more information or constraints.

Scene Recognition: Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are state of the art
approaches to semantic machine learning (ML) for object
and scene recognition [31] [32], computationally feasible
by harnessing GPUs. CNNs have been applied to conven-
tional place recognition/pose estimation [26] [27], but rely
on higher dimensional network layers, not the semantically
meaningful output layer–functionally just another high di-
mensional descriptor approach.
Google PlaNET [33] is a
CNN to globally localize, to go from input pixels to location
directly. However, navigation by scene recognition, rather
than speciﬁc place recognition, remains an open problem.
Technically a CNN is a function that takes images (pixels)
as inputs, outputs category probabilities, and is parameter-
ized by convolutional ﬁlters found using stochastic gradient
descent to optimize loss function on training data. Example
classiﬁcations using the Places-CNN model [32] for images
of the same location in Eloy, AZ are shown in Figure
1(a) (satellite imagery) and 1(b) (in-ﬂight imagery), demon-
strating broadly similar classiﬁcation, computed relatively
quickly (∼200ms).
Improving scene recognition via CNNs, or so-called Deep
Learning more broadly, is an active area of research, fur-
ther motivating the use of this capability for visual naviga-
tion. Indeed, substantial developments in real-time semantic
classiﬁcation and segmentation are released regularly, e.g.
[34] with increasingly impressive results. The use of these
tools for navigation and the place recognition problem in a
semantic sense, however, is not widespread. Moreover, larger
questions about the robustness of these methods have been
raised by the vulnerability to adversarial examples [35] which
alter images nearly imperceptibly to the human eye, but
result in classiﬁers profoundly breaking and mis-categorizing
images. These limitations support the considerations of these
tools as inherently noisy and probabilistic, where constraints
using odometry and measurements from multiple agents are
far more robust to overcoming these classiﬁcation failures
than a single agent operating alone.

4. MATHEMATICAL FOUNDATION
The results in Section 3 can be given a mathematical founda-
tion using Shannon entropy [36] [37]. The following result is
elaborated upon in Appendix A, but brieﬂy stated:
Theorem 4.1: In an environment categorized into discrete
sets Sm, where set elements are deﬁned over a metric space,
and agents k ∈K have a vision sensor model (5) with equal
probability of identifying sets correctly p, multiple agents
working together following Algorithm 1 are guaranteed to
each have distributions with entropy less than or equal to the
distribution entropy of a single agent working alone when
p = 1. Furthermore, a single agent cannot perform any better
than in the case of p = 1. Hence multiple agents working
together with p = 1 are guaranteed to reduce entropy as
much, if not more, than the best a single agent can achieve
alone.

be unreliable or unavailable.

7. CONCLUSION
Algorithms for limited multi-agent communication and coor-
dination have been presented: agents broadcast compressed
current, independent, observations.
These algorithms are
robust to intermittent and dropped messages, as agents pro-
ceed with all available information, without concern for
estimating other agents’ states, or keeping track of which
messages originate from whom. This is crucial for parafoil
systems, where an unknown number of agents are deployed in
hazardous environments where malfunctions and interference
are common.
The primary limiting factor in performance is the underly-
ing machine learning techniques used to classify imagery
from different cameras/viewpoints consistently. A network
retrained on satellite imagery could potentially function sig-
niﬁcantly better. The computation of Places-CNN classiﬁer
and approximate nearest neighbor combined are <300 mil-
lisecond operations on a consumer grade laptop (Intel Core i7
with Nvidia 750M GPU), demonstrating real-time feasibility.
Deep learning remains a burgeoning ﬁeld of active research,
and as the accuracy and speed of these networks improve, the
performance of the navigation algorithms presented here will
as well.

The goal of this work has been to demonstrate how emerg-
ing machine learning techniques can make semantic visual
sensing potentially more capable than GPS for navigation, as
well as clearly demonstrate how multiple agents can perform
signiﬁcantly better than single agents with minimal com-
munication and coordination. These minimalist, relatively
computationally inexpensive algorithms provide substantial
information, and can be incorporated within other localiza-
tion frameworks as well.
While the application discussed here has been GPS-denied
guided parafoil systems, these algorithms need not be seen
as limited to that speciﬁc circumstance. Indeed, even in a
scenario with GPS available, a parallel semantic analysis of
imagery could be invaluable in avoiding unmapped obstacles,
dealing with changing conditions on the ground, and as a fail-
safe if visual localization fails. Furthermore, any situation
where GPS is unavailable, whether indoors or extraterrestrial,
could be aided by a semantic approach, enabling algorithms
to search for speciﬁc place signatures, rather than relying on
human operators to identify them, for instance.
In this scenario a top down camera view has been used,
but ground-level imagery could be analyzed this way as
well–both the Places-CNN classiﬁer and general geometric
constraint approach could be adapted to ground-based cam-
era imagery compared to Google street view, for instance.
Autonomous cars traveling through locations with spotty or
inaccurate GPS (tunnels, overpasses) and with limited data
signal to download current maps could continue operating
and navigating safely based on semantic processing of ex-
its, construction signage, and understanding unmapped haz-
ards. Possibilities abound. Computer vision and multi-agent
robotics are promising areas for further enabling autonomous
navigation and exploration; we do well to remember that our
imaginations, rather than the sky or GPS satellites, is the
limit.

Eve Law is a Senior Member of the 
Technical Staff in the Perception and 
Localization Systems group at Draper 
Laboratory. 
Her research interests 
include systems and control theory, and 
multi-agent and GPS-denied visual 
navigation. She holds an S.B. in 
Mechanical Engineering from Harvard 
University and S.M. and Ph.D. in 
Mechanical Engineering from Boston 
University.
Professor John Baillieul focuses on
robotics, the control of mechanical sys-
tems, and mathematical system theory.
His work in the late 1980’s led to sem-
inal papers on motion planning for kine-
matically redundant manipulators. Ear-
lier work on nonlinear optimal control
theory foreshadowed much of the cur-
rent literature on singular Riemannian
geometry. Current research focuses on
extending and applying principles from nonlinear control
theory to complex mechanical systems composed of intercon-
nected rigid and elastic components. Dr. Baillieul has devel-
oped a laboratory system for experiments involving real-time
control of mechanical systems, research fundamental to the
design of lightweight, high-performance manipulator arms
and other advanced robotic devices.
He also investigates
geometric effects in nonlinear rotational mechanics, with po-
tential applications in stabilization and vibration suppression
in rotating machinery, and the theory of control and motion
planning for mechanical systems with relatively few actuators
for the control of a large number of degrees of freedom. A
Fellow of the IEEE, he is the former editor-in-chief of the
IEEE Transactions on Automatic Control. He is also afﬁliated
with Boston University’s Center for Information and Systems
Engineering (CISE)
