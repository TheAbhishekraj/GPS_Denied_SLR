Adaptive Texture-Aware Pixel Selection
for Robust Direct Visual Odometry in UAV Navigation
Jeremias Gaia1, Leonardo Fagundes-Junior2, Carlos Soria1, and Alexandre Brand˜ao2
Fig. 1: Illustration of the proposed visual odometry in scenarios with different texture levels.
Abstract— Direct visual odometry (DVO) is becoming widely
used to estimate camera ego-motion and simultaneously recon-
struct/map the environment from image sequences, particularly
due to its efficient use of photometric information and strong
performance in weak-texture scenarios. However, since DVO
relies directly on pixel intensity patterns for frame-to-frame
alignment, it is inherently sensitive to brightness inconsistencies
and illumination changes, which can degrade pose estimation,
particularly in aerial robotics scenarios involving motion blur,
illumination variations, and texture-poor environments. This
paper proposes an adaptive pixel selection mechanism for DVO
systems. The proposed approach prioritizes regions with strong
structural content, ensuring that the pose estimate remains
constrained by meaningful image gradients, even in low-
texture or highly homogeneous scenarios. By integrating image
texture descriptors, the system dynamically adjusts selection
parameters, according to the scene complexity. This enables the
operation across structured, semi-structured, and unstructured
environments without introducing significant computational
overhead. Experimental validation in both indoor and outdoor
scenarios demonstrates that the proposed method preserves
uniform spatial coverage while suppressing low-gradient and
photometrically unstable regions. The results demonstrate en-
hanced trajectory consistency and greater robustness under
challenging visual conditions, confirming the algorithm effec-
tiveness for UAV navigation in GPS-denied environments.
Index Terms— Mobile Vehicle Visual Odometry, Pose Esti-
mation, Texture Analysis.

I. INTRODUCTION
Autonomous aerial robotics has experienced significant
growth in recent years, driven by applications such as infras-
tructure inspection [1], search and rescue [2], environmental
monitoring [3], and warehouse automation [4]. In many of
these scenarios, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) must op-
erate in GPS-denied or degraded environments, requiring on-
board perception systems capable of reliable state estimation.
Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) provides
a principle-based framework for jointly estimating vehicle
pose and reconstructing the surrounding environment [5],
[6]. These systems typically rely on the fusion of data
from various sensors, such as monocular and stereo cameras,
LiDAR, and inertial measurement units (IMUs) [7], [8].
Among the various SLAM modalities, visual SLAM (V-
SLAM) is particularly attractive for aerial platforms due to
the favorable weight, cost, and energy characteristics of cam-
eras compared to LiDAR systems. When relying exclusively
on camera input, pose estimation is performed through visual
odometry (VO), which refers to the process of estimating
an agent’s egomotion (robot, human, vehicle, etc.) using
input from one or more cameras [9], [10]. Visual methods
are typically categorized into indirect (feature-based) and
direct approaches. Indirect methods rely on sparse feature
extraction and matching, estimate motion by using pixel
intensities and positions, whereas direct methods estimate
motion by minimizing photometric error over selected pixel
intensities, first construct a scene representation prior to
motion estimation.
Direct visual odometry methods such as LSD-SLAM [11],
Stereo LSD-SLAM [12], and Direct Sparse Odometry

(DSO) [13] have demonstrated strong performance in
texture-rich environments taking advantage of photometric
consistency and semi-dense or sparse pixel selection based
on gradient. These approaches directly optimize camera pose
by aligning pixel intensities across frames, avoiding explicit
feature extraction and descriptor matching.
However, UAV deployment introduces additional chal-
lenges: i) high-speed motion causing motion blur; ii) large
viewpoint changes; iii) exposure variations due to illumina-
tion dynamics iv) and frequent operation in low-texture envi-
ronments (e.g., corridors, walls, or agricultural fields). Under
such conditions, conventional gradient-based pixel selection
strategies may either oversample high-gradient clusters or
include poorly conditioned pixels that degrade photometric
optimization. Since evaluating all image pixels is compu-
tationally infeasible in real-time systems, pixel selection
becomes a critical component of direct methods [14]–[17].
In contrast to prior approaches that rely solely on gradient
magnitude or learned priors, this work address this limitation
by proposing a lightweight adaptive pixel selection strategy
designed to improve structural observability in aerial direct
visual odometry, while explicitly measures local texture
richness and dynamically adjusts pixel selection parameters.
Our method identifies pixels that are well-suited for recov-
ering a robot’s trajectory, with potential extensions to di-
rect odometry-based SLAM systems. Our strategy improves
structural conditioning of the photometric optimization while
preserving real-time feasibility for UAV deployment.
The main contributions of this paper are: i) an adaptive
texture-aware pixel selection mechanism for direct visual
odometry; ii) a strategy to preserve spatial uniformity while
prioritizing structurally informative regions; iii) experimental
validation in indoor and outdoor scenarios demonstrating
improved robustness in low-texture and high homogeneous
environments.

Section II introduces the theoretical concepts used to develop
the proposed algorithm, which is described in detail in Sec-
tion III. Section IV presents the results of field experiments,
and Section V provides concluding remarks and directions
for future works.
II. MATERIALS AND METHODS
A. Theoretical Background

to input images, which support the design of the proposed
system.

directions.

B. Maximum Gradients Image
Edges in an image correspond to locations where abrupt
intensity transitions occur, typically indicating structural
boundaries of objects within the scene. These edges are
commonly detected by computing spatial derivatives, which
highlight changes in gray levels. However, since differ-
entiation inherently amplifies high-frequency components,
it can also enhance image noise, leading to spurious or
fragmented edges. Therefore, applying noise suppression
techniques prior to edge detection is essential to preserve
the integrity of meaningful features.
In the context of pose estimation, edges play a critical
role as they denote regions of high structural significance.
Accurately identifying such features enables robust tracking
of camera motion. To this end, we employ a filtering ap-
proach known as the maximum gradients image, designed
to emphasize prominent edges while suppressing weak and
noisy responses. The process begins with the computation
of the gradient magnitude G from the input image. This is
defined as:
G =
q
G2x + G2y,
where Gx and Gy are the gradients in the horizontal and
vertical directions, respectively. The resulting magnitude
image is normalized to the range [0, 255], and a threshold
Thf is applied to retain only regions exhibiting sufficiently
high gradient intensity. This step effectively filters low-
contrast areas that do not contribute meaningful structural
information.
To further refine the edge representation, we apply non-
maximum suppression in two stages: first along the vertical
direction and then along the horizontal. This technique
ensures that only local maxima in the gradient field are
preserved, enhancing the sharpness and spatial accuracy of
the detected edges. Subsequently, morphological erosion is
used to eliminate residual noise and to thin the edge contours,
producing a binary image that highlights only the most
salient edge points.
This entire process is independently applied to each chan-
nel of the input RGB image. Once the maximum gradient
response is computed for each channel, the results are
combined to generate the final maximum gradient image
Gmax. This fusion ensures that edges prominent in any of
the color channels are captured in the final output.
Figures 3(c) and (g) show the limitations of using raw im-
age derivatives without preprocessing, where minor intensity
fluctuations can be incorrectly interpreted as valid edges. In
contrast, Figures 3(d) and (h) demonstrate the effectiveness
of the proposed maximum gradient extraction method in pro-
ducing a cleaner and more structurally meaningful edge map.
III. ADAPTIVE KEYPOINT SELECTION ALGORITHM
An overview of the keypoint extraction algorithm for pose
estimation is presented in Figure 4. The algorithm takes as
input the RGB image and its grayscale counterpart. The RGB
version is used to compute Gmax, while the grayscale image
undergoes Gaussian blur filtering and Canny edge detection
to produce a binary edge image.
A Patch Extractor module divides each image into patches
of size SROI (initially set to Spatch = 16), which are sub-
sequently evaluated by the Patch Selector. At the end of
the process, the number of extracted keypoints is evaluated.
If this number, denoted Npoints, does not meet a predefined
minimum, the algorithm automatically adjusts either SROI or
Thf accordingly.
A. Adaptive Threshold Adjustment (Thf)
The threshold variable Thf directly influences the number
of high-gradient points in Gmax that are eligible for selection
as keypoints. High values of Thf reduce the appearance
of gradients in Gmax, while low values allow low-gradient
regions to emerge. An excessively high threshold can lead to
significant information loss, reducing the number of potential
keypoints. Conversely, an overly low threshold may result in
the selection of low-gradient points, which negatively impact
pose estimation. These trade-offs highlight the need for an
adaptive thresholding mechanism.

B. Adaptive Search Window Adjustment (SROI)
The value of SROI determines the size of the search
window for keypoint extraction. A fixed window size may
lead to several issues:
• An excessively large window may result in too few
extracted keypoints, failing to meet the minimum re-
quirement.
• A window that is too small increases computational cost
and may cause significant delays.
• In regions rich in texture or structure, a large window
may underutilize the information, yielding few key-
points.
• Conversely, very small windows may lead to excessive
keypoint extraction, slowing down subsequent pose es-
timation steps.
To address these issues, the window size is adaptively
adjusted based on image characteristics. As illustrated in the
Patch Extractor block of Figure 4, the algorithm processes
the Gmax, Canny edges, and grayscale versions of the input
image, dividing them into patches of size SROI. Each patch
is further subdivided into four sub-patches of size Spatch/2
to ensure spatial distribution of keypoints. The system is
configured to extract a maximum of two keypoints per patch.

spatial coverage to capture camera motion.
C. Keypoint Extraction
The keypoint extraction module receives corresponding
patches from the Gmax, the Canny edges, and the grayscale
images. Each patch is individually evaluated to determine
whether it meets the criteria for reliable keypoint selection.
First, the grayscale patch is assessed using the SRE metric
defined in Equation (3). If SRE ≥SREmax, the patch
is discarded, as this indicates the presence of excessive
gray-level variations, often corresponding to small or distant
textures, which are unreliable for pose estimation.
Next, the binary patch derived from the Canny edge
detector is analyzed using the LRE metric from Equation (4).
The Canny image is used due to its binarized nature, which
facilitates efficient computation of long edge sequences. If
LRE ≤LREmin, the patch is also discarded.

IV. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS
This section presents qualitative and quantitative results
obtained from evaluating our method in real-world field
experiments using proprietary datasets, as well as on bench-
mark datasets.
To evaluate the proposed method under diverse conditions,
three datasets were collected during field experiments on
the campus of the Facultad de Ingenier´ıa at the Universidad
Nacional de San Juan, covering indoor, outdoor, and mixed
scenarios. As these datasets lack ground-truth measurements,
they are used exclusively for qualitative evaluation. Quantita-
tive evaluation and comparison with a state-of-the-art method
are conducted using the TUM RGB-D benchmark dataset.
An operating range was defined for the number of points
generated by the selection system and used for pose es-
timation. In the experiments, the number of points was
constrained to lie between 2000 and 3600. The lower bound
reflects the minimum required for proper operation of the
pose estimation system, while the upper bound enforces a
relaxation of the adaptive threshold.
A. Qualitative Results
Indoor Experiments were conducted in an office environ-
ment, characterized by hallways with extensive low-gradient
and homogeneous areas. Figures 6 and 7 illustrate dataset
frames corresponding to two representative scenarios.
Figure 6 illustrates an instance from the dataset in which,
as the system moves along the corridor, it transitions from
extracting two points per patch to extracting three. This adap-
tive behavior is visually reflected in a higher concentration of
selected points around structured regions (Figures 6(c), 6(f)),
effectively avoiding low-gradient areas.
Figure 7 illustrates a scenario where the camera moves
toward a wall. As the distance decreases, homogeneous
regions increasingly dominate the frame; however, the system
concentrates point extraction around structural elements,
including window frames and baseboards.
Outdoor environments pose several challenges for visual
systems, including varying lighting conditions, shadowed
areas, foliage, streets, and dynamic objects.
Figure 8 demonstrates how the pixel selection strategy per-
forms under such conditions. Even in high-gradient regions,
such as the contrast between bright sky and dark foliage, the
system reduces the number of extracted points and prioritizes
structured elements such as window frames, tree trunks, and
lampposts.
This behavior results from the Point Extraction module.
Regions dominated by rapid gray-level transitions (i.e., high
SRE and low LRE), such as foliage, are largely avoided
by the algorithm. Nevertheless, due to the trade-off between
performance and computational efficiency, the system may
still select points in suboptimal regions during outdoor
scenarios. Importantly, it consistently maintains a spatially
uniform point distribution and largely avoids low-gradient
homogeneous areas (e.g., sky, walls, grass).

C.
Comparison with DSO
Direct odometry methods select a subset of pixels through
a pixel selection strategy and apply a direct image alignment
process to estimate camera motion. One of the most influ-
ential contributions on the field was made by Engel et al.
in [13], where a Direct Sparse Odometry (DSO) method
is proposed to estimate camera motion by minimizing a
photometric error function derived from the observed pixel
intensities. DSO relies exclusively on image gradient infor-
mation for pixel selection. Initially, the image is partitioned
into 32 × 32 blocks to compute a gradient threshold given
by the median absolute gradient magnitude within each
block. This partition is subsequently discarded, and a new
partition of the image into d × d blocks is performed. The
pixel exhibiting the maximum gradient magnitude above the
previously determined threshold is then selected from each
block.
This section presents a comparative evaluation of DSO
and the proposed system on several sequences from the
TUM RGB-D dataset. Performance is assessed using the
Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE), a metric that quantifies
global trajectory consistency by measuring the absolute dis-
tances between estimated and ground-truth trajectory points
[20]. Table II shows the results obtained for the fr1/plant,
fr2/desk, fr3/structure-texture, fr3/structure-no texture, fr3/no
structure-texture, and fr3/no structure-no texture sequences
of the TUM-RGBD dataset.
While sequences such as fr1/plant and fr2/desk offer rich
visual information, they also involve perspective changes
that challenge visual odometry algorithms. On the fr1/plant
sequence, the proposed approach yields results comparable
to DSO; in contrast, on the fr2/desk sequence, our method
lowers the error from 1.7119 to 0.4344, achieving a 74.6%
reduction in ATE.
The structure-texture family of sequences presents in-
creasingly difficult visual challenges forx the pose esti-
mation system. The conditions for visual pose estimation
degrade progressively from the most favorable scenario
(fr3/structure-texture) to the most challenging environment
(fr3/no structure-no texture).
The fr3/structure-texture sequence contains images char-
acterized by abundant structural and textural cues. The pro-
posed approach shows its best performance on this sequence,
achieving an ATE RMSE of 0.0296 m, which indicates that,
in the presence of rich visual structure and texture, the system
can reliably select informative points for direct odometry
estimation.

During the fr3/structure–no texture sequence, the cam-
era follows a trajectory around a folding screen covered
with white fabric. In this setting, the images contain few
visual cues beyond the structural elements of the screen
itself. Moreover, in several frames, the distinction between
the structure and the background becomes ambiguous in
grayscale images. Under these conditions, the proposed
system outperforms DSO.
In the fr3/no structure–texture sequence, the camera ex-
ecutes a straight trajectory while pointing toward a set of
posters placed on the floor, with the structural elements
removed. Compared to the previous sequence, both the
proposed system and DSO exhibit improved performance due
to the presence of distinctive visual features that facilitate
point extraction.
Finally, the fr3/no structure–no texture sequence presents
a highly challenging visual scenario. A similar trajectory
is performed; however, the posters are removed, leaving a
smooth laboratory floor. In this case, the proposed system
achieves performance comparable to DSO, as it is forced to
operate in an extreme fallback mode (similar to the case in
Figure 5), where points are extracted uniformly across the
image. Although suboptimal, this strategy enables the system
to maintain tracking at the cost of increased estimation error.
V. CONCLUSIONS
This paper proposes a pixel selection system for pose esti-
mation in direct visual odometry applications. The developed
algorithm automatically identifies regions with prominent
structural information—features highly desirable for recon-
struction and SLAM tasks. By incorporating texture mea-
surements, such as Haralick features, the system dynamically
adapts window sizes and filtering thresholds to the navigated
environment. This enhances its robustness and adaptability
across structured, semi-structured, and unstructured settings.
In highly homogeneous environments where structural fea-
tures are scarce or absent, a fallback mechanism is activated.
This operational mode prioritizes pose-tracking stability over
maximum accuracy, thereby preventing catastrophic failures
in feature-deprived scenarios.
Comparative results against a standard state-of-the-art
method indicate that gradient-based pixel selection should
be complemented with additional image characteristics to
enhance point extraction on structural elements. Computa-
tional timing studies confirm that the proposed processing
does not introduce a significant overhead, thereby ensuring
its suitability for real-time deployment on UAV platforms.
