## 2. Problem Statement
"The Leonardo Drone Contest is an autonomous drone competition that aims at finding innovative solutions for drones operating in a Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) denied environment. At the end of a three years cycle of the competition, in this paper a review of the identified system and conclusions made by the DRAFT team from Politecnico di Torino is presented." [p.1]
2022-edition mission requirements [p.2]: "The final goal was to develop a drone and a ground station system capable of reconstructing the 3D environment flown over, tracking moving objects accurately for a minimum time of 10 seconds, performing precision landings, and taking pictures of particular markers (ArUco) hidden between buildings." plus "the presence of unknown obstacles in the race course was also introduced, which had to be handled by the drone with active obstacle avoidance logic" [p.2].

## 3. Motivation
"One of the main challenges with autonomous drones is implementing efficient autonomous drone navigation in Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) denied environment. While GNSS autonomous navigation has been widely discussed [8], GNSS-denied environments provide limitations that need to be overcome to exploit drones' potentialities fully. Many tasks that drones are called to perform cannot make use of GNSS signal because it is degraded (rescue operations) [9] or absent (indoor applications) [10]." [p.2]
"Many solutions have been introduced to overcome the limitations related to GNSS-denied environments [11], [12]: among various approaches, the most promising ones make use of SLAM techniques [13], [14] or ultra-wide-band (UWB) technology [15]." [p.2]
Prior-platform limitation stated by the team: "the developed quadcopter model was optimized in terms of weight and autonomy compared to previous years where a coaxial octa-copter was employed" [p.2].

## 5. Proposed Method
Camera-only SLAM autonomy stack (T265 VIO with two drift corrections, RTAB-Map mapping, A* 3D planning, obstacle avoidance, PID target tracking) on a custom quadcopter — SLAM
"the most promising ones make use of SLAM techniques" [p.2]; the software architecture is "divided into four main modules" [p.3]: Robot System Engineering (RSE: mavros/MAVLink interface, ROS/Gazebo simulation), Deep Learning and Computer Vision (DLCV: ArUco and cell detection), Obstacle Avoidance and Motion Planning (OAMP: "global (Astar 3D ROS service) and local (Astar 3D dynamic) trajectory planning", mission management, tracking and precision-landing control), and SLAM ("sensor fusion and therefore with pose estimation... For the mapping step an open-source ROS package, called RTAB-Map [26], was used") [p.3].
State-estimation design: T265 VIO at ~200 Hz plus two corrections — (1) "a control loop has been applied to perform the correction of the estimated location of the drone wherever it is observed" at a unique known ground feature, and (2) mono-camera ground-pattern tracking: "Whenever a full set of four expected features is extracted, the algorithm tracks them over time to determine a relative drift. This information is then combined with the estimation from the T265 camera" [p.4].

## 6. System Architecture
- Platform: custom quadcopter "Agares" — dimensions H = 0.35 m, W = S = 0.5 m; weight 2100 g; MTOW 2600 g; autonomy 19 min; payload 500 g; flight controller Pixhawk 4 with ArduPilot firmware (Table I) [p.2]
- Sensors (Table II): stereo camera Intel RealSense D455 (forward-facing, main navigation and obstacle-avoidance input, also target detection on obstacles); tracking camera Intel T265 (VIO, ~200 Hz, with its own IMU, MPU6000); mono-camera M120 Raspberry Pi Camera (down-facing, precision landing, VIO drift support); accelerometers/gyroscopes MPU6000 (T265) and ST Micro 16-bit gyroscope (Pixhawk 4) [p.3-4]
- Compute: onboard computer Jetson Xavier NX fed at ~12 V through a Battery Eliminator Circuit from a 14.8 V / 4-cell battery that also powers the ESCs [p.3]
- Communication: mavros/MAVLink between onboard computer and Pixhawk; radio receiver and telemetry retained "for pilot emergency control and data exchange with the flight controller" [p.3]; the ground station interfaces to ROS through a Python node [p.5]
- Control flow: "the main logic flow sees the data coming from the sensors as input to the on-board computer; once the data has been processed, the on-board computer provides the desired speed commands directly to the Pixhawk flight controller" [p.3]
- Latency management: "Computing the state estimate with these two additional algorithms introduces latency in the order of 150 ms to the localization module, because of this it has been decided to decouple the localization from the control problem by feeding the full 200 Hz attitude estimation from the T265 to the controller." [p.4]
- Tracking mode: implemented as an operations mode using the down-facing mono camera, with "a control scheme including of PID family... the chosen gains act on the target position and velocity" [p.4]
- Ground control station: JavaScript GUI with live map (estimated trajectory, obstacle heights by colour intensity, search areas, takeoff pad, waypoints), selectable camera streams, "Mission Flight Plan" uploader with input validation, marker photo gallery, tracking timer, and a sensor-health panel [p.5]

## 7. Experimental Setup
- real_or_sim: BOTH (real competition flights plus software-in-the-loop development)
- environment: Indoor scaled urban arena 20 m x 10 m x 3 m with buildings, unknown obstacles, ArUco markers and a moving ground object [p.2]
- trials/runs: Not reported as a numeric trial protocol; the paper reports one competition result (second place, third edition) and describes the development-validation cycle rather than a statistical evaluation
- baselines: NO_BASELINE in the academic sense — the benchmark is the competition ranking against the other participating Italian universities; the prior-year coaxial octa-copter platform is the internal hardware baseline [p.2]
- datasets: NOT_REPORTED (ROS bags recorded during tests were used to validate SLAM: "SLAM algorithms were validated from recorded ROS bags and experimental testing") [p.5]
- development protocol: Gazebo SITL with the Gazebo D435 plugin for point clouds ("although Gazebo is not a highly photo-realistic simulation system unlike Airsim [27], still allowed us to test and validate the main path planning, target tracking, precision landing, and obstacle avoidance packages with a reduced computational cost"); "SLAM algorithms were excluded from Software In The Loop (SITL) validation, due to the limitations of Gazebo in terms of photo-realistic simulation features"; deployment proceeded "a maximum of one or two code's features at a time on board the real aircraft" [p.5]
- parameters refined between sim and field: "control logic gains, camera calibration and exposure parameters, and aircraft speed limits" [p.5]

## 9. Ablation / Sensitivity
Not reported as a controlled ablation. Component-level evidence exists but is qualitative or operational: the two drift corrections are described individually (unique-feature control-loop correction; mono-camera four-feature ground-pattern drift fusion) without an isolated error figure for each [p.4]; the SITL-to-field workflow is described as parameter refinement only ("it was only necessary to refine certain parameters that differed between the virtual and simulated worlds") [p.5]; SLAM was validated separately from the rest of the stack because Gazebo could not support it photorealistically [p.5].

## 10. Stated Limitations
"experimental tests have identified a drift in the order of magnitude of two meters. This error is not acceptable for accomplishing contest tasks, so in order to compensate for the drifting Visual Inertial Odometry (VIO) estimate, two additional corrections have been implemented." [p.4]
"Computing the state estimate with these two additional algorithms introduces latency in the order of 150 ms to the localization module" [p.4] — the mitigation was architectural (decoupling localization from control), not an elimination of the latency.
"SLAM algorithms were excluded from Software In The Loop (SITL) validation, due to the limitations of Gazebo in terms of photo-realistic simulation features." [p.5]
"although Gazebo is not a highly photo-realistic simulation system unlike Airsim [27]" [p.5] — simulator fidelity acknowledged as a constraint.
"Although VIO still has weaknesses as concerns the state estimation problem, the loop closure suggested in this paper allows to reduce the localization error." [p.6]

## 15. Notes
- Evidence character: this is a competition-system report, so its numbers are platform and process figures (drift magnitude, latencies, rates, mass budget) rather than a statistically reported accuracy evaluation. The strongest quantitative datum for the survey is the uncorrected T265 VIO drift of "the order of magnitude of two meters" in an indoor arena [p.4].
- Two independent drift-correction mechanisms are documented in enough detail to replicate: (1) discrete correction at a unique known ground feature, (2) continuous relative-drift estimation from a four-feature ground pattern observed by the down-facing mono camera, fused with T265 output [p.4].
- Engineering trade-off worth citing: correcting VIO costs ~150 ms, so the team decoupled a 200 Hz uncorrected attitude estimate (for control) from a slower corrected localisation (for planning) — a pattern relevant to the VIO cluster of this review [p.4].
- The SITL development loop is explicitly non-photorealistic and therefore unable to validate SLAM; SLAM was instead validated from recorded ROS bags and real tests — a methodological caveat for any claim that the whole stack was simulation-validated [p.5].
- Platform detail is complete for reproduction: airframe size/mass/payload/endurance, flight controller and firmware, all sensor part numbers, onboard computer, and power architecture (14.8 V 4S, BEC to ~12 V) [p.2-3].
- Reference-list oddity: reference [3] (Foehn et al.) appears with a mismatched title ("Dynamics modeling and analysis of a flexible-base space robot..."), an apparent citation error in the source — flagged here rather than silently propagated [p.6].
- The same T265 sensor appears in REC_0043 (simulated) and here (real, with a measured ~2 m drift) — a useful cross-check for the synthesis on commercial tracking-camera reliability.

## 18. Best Combination & Accuracy
### 18.1 Best sensor combination
Intel RealSense D455 stereo (forward) + Intel T265 tracking camera (VIO, ~200 Hz) + down-facing Raspberry Pi M120 mono-camera (landing + drift correction), with Pixhawk 4 (ArduPilot) IMU data and Jetson Xavier NX compute [p.2-4]
### 18.2 Best algorithm
T265 VIO fused with two drift corrections (known-feature control-loop correction; four-feature ground-pattern relative-drift estimation), RTAB-Map for 3-D mapping, A* 3D global/local planning with obstacle avoidance, PID-family tracking and precision-landing control, under a decoupled 200 Hz control / ~150 ms localisation architecture [p.3-4]
### 18.3 Best accuracy (metric + value + unit + [p.N])
NOT_REPORTED as an absolute figure; the quantified evidence is the uncorrected T265 drift "in the order of magnitude of two meters" that the corrections bring within contest-acceptable limits, plus a second-place competition outcome [p.1, p.4, p.6]
### 18.4 Baseline comparison at best configuration
No academic baseline; the comparison is the competition ranking (second among the participating university teams) and the team's own previous coaxial octa-copter platform, which the quadcopter improved in weight and endurance [p.1-2]
### 18.5 Conditions of best accuracy
Indoor scaled urban arena 20 m x 10 m x 3 m, a known unique ground feature plus a structured ground pattern, unknown obstacles present, moving-object tracking for at least 10 s, precision landing and ArUco photography tasks [p.2, p.4]
### 18.6 Trade-offs
Correcting VIO drift costs ~150 ms of localisation latency, resolved by architectural decoupling rather than faster estimation; the rich sensor payload (three cameras + tracking camera) must fit a 500 g payload and 19 min endurance budget on a 2100 g quadcopter [p.2, p.4]
### 18.7 Generalization limits
Demonstrated only in the 20 x 10 x 3 m indoor contest arena [p.2]; the corrections depend on structured ground patterns and a unique known feature, which do not exist in arbitrary environments [p.4]; SLAM was not validated in simulation, so the full stack's sim-to-field transfer is only argued for planning/control [p.5]; the extension to eVTOL air mobility is a claim, not a demonstrated result [p.5]
