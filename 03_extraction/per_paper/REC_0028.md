## 2. Problem Statement
"Navigation of an unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) to reach a desired waypoint with provable guarantees in global navigation satellite system (GNSS)-denied environments is considered. The UAV is assumed to have an unknown initial state (position, velocity, and time) and the environment is assumed to possess multiple terrestrial signals of opportunity (SOPs) transmitters with unknown states (position and time) and one anchor SOP whose states are known. The UAV makes pseudorange measurements to all SOPs to estimate its own states simultaneously with the states of the unknown SOPs." [p.1]

## 3. Motivation
"Today's UAV navigation systems essentially rely on global navigation satellite system (GNSS) signals. However, it is well-known that GNSS signals can become unreliable in environments where UAVs conduct missions: GNSS signals get highly attenuated in deep urban canyons and under canopies [1] and are susceptible to jamming and malicious spoofing [2]. To alleviate these shortcomings during GNSS outages, UAV navigation systems typically supplement GNSS receivers with additional sensors (e.g., inertial measurement units (IMUs) [3], cameras [4], and lasers [5]). However, after prolonged GNSS unavailability periods, the UAV's position estimation error and estimation uncertainty could accumulate to unacceptable levels, compromising the safety and success of the UAV's mission. While the accumulation rate of UAV positioning error could be reduced by incorporating additional sensors into the UAV's sensor-suite, this could violate cost, size, weight, and power (CSWaP) constraints." [p.1]
"A more elegant and CSWaP-efficient approach is to detect and map existing features in the unknown environment and to prescribe the UAV's trajectory to minimize the UAV's states uncertainty, which is estimated using measurements drawn from the mapped features [6]." [p.1]

## 4. GPS-Denied Context
MIXED

Note: the paper enumerates several distinct GNSS-degradation mechanisms (urban canyon attenuation, canopy attenuation, jamming, spoofing) rather than one, hence MIXED. The field experiment itself is an open-air cellular-SOP scenario in which GNSS was available but deliberately not used for navigation: the ground-truth trajectory was "parsed from the UAV's onboard navigation system log file, which records position from its integrated GNSS-aided inertial navigation system (INS)" [p.12].

## 5. Proposed Method
Greedy multiobjective motion planning (MOMP) over EKF-based radio SLAM with cellular SOP pseudoranges — SLAM
"The waypoint navigation problem is formulated as a greedy (i.e., one-step look-ahead) multiobjective motion planning (MOMP) strategy, which guarantees that the UAV gets to within a user-specified distance of the waypoint with a user-specified confidence. The MOMP strategy balances two objectives: i) navigating to the waypoint; and (ii) reducing UAV's position estimate uncertainty." [p.1]
"The problem of navigating with unknown SOPs is termed radio SLAM [26] or variations thereof [31]." [p.2]
Cost functions derived three ways — probabilistic (Markov inequality) and information-theoretic (Kullback-Leibler divergence, 2-Wasserstein metric): "It was shown that all cost functions are equivalent under certain conditions" via Theorem IV.1 [p.7-10, p.14]. The adopted cost is the Markov form J = w·J1 + (1-w)·J2 with J1 the Euclidean distance to the waypoint and J2 the A-optimality trace of the position covariance, and w set by an indicator function derived from the χ² stopping condition (48/53) [p.9-10].

## 6. System Architecture
- Platform: Quadrotor UAV; experimental platform is "an Autel X-Star Premium UAV ... equipped with an Ettus universal software radio peripheral (USRP)-E312R to sample cellular CDMA signals" [p.12]; planar (2-D) motion model assumed, with extensions to 3-D declared straightforward but "poor estimability of the UAV's vertical position" expected without an altimeter [p.4]
- Sensors: SOP pseudorange receiver (USRP-E312R tuned to 882.75 MHz cellular CDMA, Verizon Wireless) [p.12]; no camera/LiDAR/IMU used as a navigation aid in the proposed loop — the algorithm replaces the GNSS receiver with an EKF over SOP pseudoranges [p.4]; the UAV airborne platform also carries "a GNSS receiver, IMU, barometric pressure sensor or altimeter, magnetometer, etc." according to the generic control-system description [p.3]
- Compute: The MOMP optimisation is intended to run online on the UAV's onboard processor ("It is desired that the UAV be able to solve the optimization online on its on-board processor. In order to meet this requirement, a greedy approach is adopted") [p.9]
- Communication: Terrestrial cellular CDMA SOP downlink signals (Verizon Wireless, 882.75 MHz) as the navigation signal source; no dedicated ranging infrastructure [p.12]

## 7. Experimental Setup
- real_or_sim: BOTH (500-run Monte Carlo simulation plus a field experiment)
- environment: Simulation — "Consider an environment comprising four SOPs and a specified waypoint... One anchor SOP is available, and the remaining three SOPs are unknown" [p.10]. Field experiment — "The experiment was conducted in Colton, California, USA." [p.12]; "an environment was first built in simulation based on the real field experimental environment" [p.12]. The archived text gives no urban/rural/forest classification of the site, so no specific environment enum is asserted.
- trials/runs: Simulation — "A Monte Carlo study is conducted using 500 runs"; "If the UAV did not reach the waypoint within 200 s, the mission was stopped and recorded as failure." [p.10]. Experiment — three cellular SOPs (one anchor, two unknown), trajectories flown manually by a piloted UAV, EKF applied in post-processing [p.12].
- baselines: (i) naive strategy (fly straight to the waypoint), (ii) nonadaptive MOMP, (iii) adaptive MOMP [p.10]. The experiment compares naive vs adaptive MOMP only [p.12].
- datasets: No public dataset. Experiment produced UAV trajectories (prescribed and estimated) and cellular CDMA pseudorange recordings: "the profiles of the pseudoranges were compared with the distance profiles to each SOP" for offline data association [p.12].
- experiment chain: "Signals from three cellular SOPs were acquired and tracked via the Multichannel Adaptive Transceiver Information eXtractor (MATRIX) software-defined received (SDR) [70], producing pseudoranges to all SOPs for the entire duration of the flight." [p.12]; anchor SOP clock "solved for off-line by subtracting the true distance from the SOP's pseudoranges" [p.12]

## 9. Ablation / Sensitivity
Two distinct comparisons. (1) Strategy ablation: naive vs nonadaptive MOMP vs adaptive MOMP over 500 Monte Carlo runs, where "the nonadaptive MOMP strategy performed the worst in all metrics" and "did not declare mission success for any of the runs", and where "without the indicator function, the naive and nonadaptive MOMP never meet condition (48), which is necessary for (42), leading to poor performance" [p.10-12]. (2) Theoretical sensitivity: the equivalence of the three cost functions is bounded and numerically validated over a sampled covariance matrix with a constant lower bound (Theorem IV.1) and a time-varying upper bound (Lemma IV.1) [p.7-9]. Further observation: "the covariance matrix in the EKF tends to grow as the UAV is flying around due to the poor SOP geometry around the target" [p.11].

## 10. Stated Limitations
"However, because of the poor estimability around the target, the UAV cannot bring its position uncertainty down in the allotted time." [p.11]
"Note that in some runs that used the naive strategy, the filter diverged and the UAV never reached the waypoint, but the filter reported mission completed. This is due to the naive approach failing to consider the uncertainty in the UAV's position estimate, causing the UAV to declare mission complete when the actual position is unacceptably far from the waypoint." [p.12]
"The MOMP strategy studied in this article requires an indicator function to switch between the two components in the cost function, when using greedy motion planning." [p.14]
"In addition, the cost function of MOMP is generally nonconvex, which upon employing numerical optimization solvers, could result in convergence to local optima, in addition to requiring involved computations which could be infeasible for real-time implementations." [p.14]
"For simplicity, this article assumes a planar environment." [p.4]

## 11. Future Work
"Future work can extend this framework to adaptive weighting of the two components of the cost function or adopt a receding horizon trajectory optimization strategy to consider both components at the same time. ... Applying a convex information gathering cost (e.g., innovation-based metrics [32]) to MOMP could be the subject of future work." [p.14]

## 15. Notes
- Venue is a top-quartile aerospace journal (IEEE TAES), and the article is unusually rigorous for this corpus: three cost-function derivations with a theorem and two lemmas, plus a field experiment.
- Table II (simulation metrics) and Table IV (experiment metrics) numeric cells are absent from the archived page text; per Rule E4 they are recorded as TABLE_ONLY with the page, not reconstructed. Any manuscript use of those values needs a direct PDF/table check.
- The framework treats the UAV's own control system as a black box: "no modifications are needed for the inner control loop; and 2) the proposed motion planning algorithm automatically computes the desired trajectories that are input to the inner control loop." [p.4]
- State vector includes receiver clock bias and drift alongside 2-D position/velocity; SOP states are 4-dimensional (position and time states), which is what makes the environment only weakly estimable [p.4, p.10].
- Experiment caveat: trajectories were flown manually and processed offline rather than in closed loop onboard, so the experiment is a replay-of-truth evaluation rather than a live closed-loop demonstration [p.12].
- Counter-intuitive finding relevant to the review: flying the shortest path can be self-defeating — the naive filter declared success while the true position was outside the tolerance [p.12].
- The GNSS-denied premise is partly constructed in the experiment (GNSS was available and used only for ground truth), which is typical of SOP navigation studies but should be reported transparently.

## 18. Best Combination & Accuracy
### 18.1 Best sensor combination
Cellular CDMA SOP pseudoranges only (three SOPs: one anchor, two unknown) via USRP-E312R at 882.75 MHz with the MATRIX SDR; no GNSS, camera, LiDAR or IMU in the navigation solution [p.4, p.12]
### 18.2 Best algorithm
Adaptive greedy MOMP with the Markov-inequality cost J = w·J1 + (1-w)·J2, indicator-function weighting (Eq. 53), and the chi-squared stopping criterion from Lemma IV.2 (Eq. 48), with an EKF fusing UAV and SOP states [p.9-10]
### 18.3 Best accuracy (metric + value + unit + [p.N])
Mission success rate 95 % versus 36 % for the naive strategy over 500 Monte Carlo runs; exact final position RMSE and final distance to waypoint are TABLE_ONLY (Tables II and IV) in the archived page text [p.1, p.11-13]
### 18.4 Baseline comparison at best configuration
Naive straight-line strategy — 36 % success, with "only about 30% of these missions were actually successful" among those it self-declared complete, declaring completion about 60 s sooner; nonadaptive MOMP — worst in all metrics and never declared success in 500 runs [p.1, p.11-12]
### 18.5 Conditions of best accuracy
Four SOPs (one anchor with known states) in simulation; initial UAV state and three SOP states unknown with large initial covariance; 200 s mission budget; tolerance d = 5 m; epsilon = p = 1e-4 for the cost-equivalence validation [p.8, p.10]
### 18.6 Trade-offs
Uncertainty-reducing manoeuvres cost time: the naive strategy completes (or claims completion) about 60 s sooner but fails the mission, while adaptive MOMP succeeds at the cost of a longer trajectory; nonconvex optimisation and indicator-function design add complexity [p.11-12, p.14]
### 18.7 Generalization limits
Planar 2-D dynamics only [p.4]; one known anchor SOP assumed available [p.1, p.10]; simulation uses a stylised four-SOP geometry [p.10]; field experiment relied on manual flight and offline processing rather than closed-loop onboard MOMP [p.12]; the anchor SOP clock was solved offline [p.12]
