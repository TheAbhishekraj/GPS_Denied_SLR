Abstract—Autonomous UAV inspection in industrial reﬁneries
demands precise landing on pipelines under GNSS-denied condi-

integrates LiDAR-based localization with a novel multi-sensor
framework for pipeline perception and tracking. The proposed
method processes dual 2D laser scans and fuses them with depth
data through a temporal ﬁlter. This enables consistent mapping
and tracking of pipelines, effectively rejecting spurious detec-
tions and maintaining a reliable scene representation. The system
is validated both in controlled indoor experiments and through
fully autonomous landing missions in a real reﬁnery, demonstrat-
ing robust performance and accurate alignment with the target
infrastructure.

II. AERIAL ROBOT DESCRIPTION
The aerial robot presented in this work is an evolution of
related work from [10]. Instead of remaining attached to the
pipe during inspection, the new system’s primary role is to
autonomously deploy and retrieve various inspection crawlers,
signiﬁcantly enhancing its operational scope and versatility.
The aerial robot, shown in Fig. 2, is a custom-built plat-
form designed for autonomous operation in cluttered, GNSS-
degraded environments such as reﬁneries. Its mechanical design
features tilted rotors, forming a fully-actuated conﬁguration
managed by a custom ﬂight controller. This allows the UAV to
generate lateral forces and maneuver horizontally without sig-
niﬁcant attitude changes, which is advantageous for maintaining
stable sensor orientation and enhancing safety during the ﬁnal
landing approach on pipelines. An integrated VectorNav VN-
100 Attitude and Heading Reference System (AHRS) provides
inertial measurements.
It is equipped with a multi-modal sensor suite, detailed in
Fig. 2, for robust localization and perception, with all data
processed in real-time by an onboard Intel NUC 10 computer
(8 GB of RAM). An Ouster OS1-64 3D LiDAR serves as the
primary navigation sensor. For pipeline perception, the system
uses a combination of an Intel RealSense D435 depth camera
and dual Hokuyo UST-10LX 2D lasers. A Leica prism is also
mounted to acquire ground-truth data during experiments. The
coordinate frame shown in the ﬁgure represents the UAV’s body
axes directions, with RGB corresponding to XYZ.
III. SOFTWARE ARCHITECTURE SYSTEM
A. GNSS-Denied Navigation
Given the presence of large infrastructures in reﬁneries, elec-
tromagnetic interference may lead to degraded or completely
unavailable satellite coverage, rendering GNSS-based naviga-
tion impractical. To achieve safe autonomous drone operations,
a comprehensive localization system is proposed.
1) Tightly Coupled LiDAR-Inertial Odometry: This com-
ponent leverages data from the UAV’s AHRS and a 3D Li-
DAR to perform tightly coupled LiDAR-inertial odometry. A
streamlined version of the LIO-SAM architecture [11] is used,
adapted to operate without GNSS and magnetometer data, and
initialized from a zero-origin reference frame. Other modiﬁca-
tions include gravity calibration, continuous pose output without
loop-closure corrections, and customized sensor transform han-
dling to accommodate NED/FRD IMU frames. Pose estimates
are computed at the AHRS rate (200 Hz), providing smooth,
high-temporal-resolution state information for downstream nav-
igation modules.
2) Map-Based Localization: To correct accumulated drift
and enable global navigation, a map-based localization module
is used. It aligns the UAV’s current LiDAR observations with
a pre-built high-deﬁnition map generated using a Leica Total
Station. This module builds upon a customized version of the
Direct LiDAR Localization (DLL) algorithm [12], leveraging
an initial pose guess and non-linear optimization to iteratively
reﬁne the UAV’s global position within the map frame. The
algorithm is executed once at the beginning of the mission and
subsequently at low frequency during ﬂight, enabling long-term
drift correction. Inspection areas and ﬂight plans are deﬁned
directly on this map, allowing for accurate mission execution
and repeatability across different sessions.
B. Pipeline Detection and Mapping
Autonomous UAV landing on pipelines requires their precise
3D pose estimation. Reﬁneries present a challenging environ-
ment due to the presence of multiple, closely spaced pipelines.
The diverse materials used in these pipelines can create re-
ﬂective surfaces that degrade sensor readings, necessitating a
system designed to operate effectively under such conditions.
To mitigate these effects, the system combines geometry-based
ﬁltering (clustering, curvature/size constraints, robust cylinder
ﬁtting) with association gating and temporal consistency checks
in mapping/tracking to reject inconsistent detections and retain
only persistent hypotheses.
This system must accurately identify each individual pipeline
and track its characteristics over time. The complete scheme
will determine the 3D position, direction, radius, and length of
each pipeline captured by the sensors as shown in Fig. 3. Highly
accurate direction estimation is particularly important for proper
alignment of the UAV’s landing gear, ensuring a safe and smooth
landing.

1) Cylinder Detection Using a Depth Point Cloud: Cylinder
parameters are extracted from the 3D point cloud using a ro-
bust, iterative process, building upon our previous work [13].
First, Euclidean clustering employing a nearest-neighbor search
segments the point cloud. Clusters that are too small (below a
minimum point count) or too large (above a maximum point
count) are discarded. For each remaining cluster, a RANSAC-
based algorithm ﬁts a cylindrical model.
The algorithm iteratively estimates cylinder parameters, in-
corporatingpointnormals(estimatedusingalocalneighborhood
search) to enhance ﬁtting accuracy. Candidate cylinders are
evaluated based on the root-mean-square error (RMSE) between
the data points and the ﬁtted model. Those exceeding a prede-
ﬁned distance threshold are rejected. Furthermore, geometric
constraints are applied: only cylinders with a radius within
a speciﬁed minimum and maximum range are accepted. This
ﬁltering process yields a set of pipeline candidates.
2) Cylinder Detection Using a Single 2D Laser Scanner: In
this section, a method to obtain a 3D cylinder estimation from 2D
scan points is presented. The problem consists of retrieving the
cylinder direction, position, and radius, given the curve depicted
by the scan points.
Since obtaining the cylinder’s parameters from 2D scan points
alone is a high-dimensional problem, the estimation process
is simpliﬁed by leveraging existing information. Speciﬁcally,
two key assumptions are made to constrain the optimization
problem: a known pipeline radius obtained from a pre-existing
map, and the approximate 3D direction from prior depth camera
estimations, which serves to resolve the ambiguity introduced
in the optimization process.

IV. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION
A. Setup and Methodology
The system is evaluated in two settings to assess both con-
trolled and real-world performance: a VICON-equipped indoor
testbed (6-DoF ground truth) and an outdoor reﬁnery with a
Leica total station (3-DoF position ground truth).
First, LiDAR odometry is evaluated using the Absolute Pose
Error (APE) and the Relative Pose Error (RPE). Estimated and
ground-truth trajectories are rigidly aligned at the beginning,
allowing long-term drift to be consistently measured. Second,
pipeline perception accuracy is evaluated for three outputs:
raw depth-camera detections, raw dual-laser detections, and
the ﬁnal fused tracked estimations. For this purpose, a set of
complementary metrics is employed. Each is compared against
the ground-truth pipe axis using the geometric segment distance
δ (17) and the angular deviation θ (18). Also, the detection
Availability (A) is reported, deﬁned as the percentage of time
each component provides a valid estimate, which reﬂects the
continuity and reliability of the output.

affectedbyaccumulateddrift. Toisolateperceptionfromnaviga-

are aligned to ground truth using the instantaneous UAV offset.
Indoors a full 6-DoF pose correction is applied. Outdoors a 3D
translation is applied. After this alignment, perception metrics
reﬂect the sensing and tracking quality, independent of accumu-
lated odometry drift.

V. CONCLUSION
This work presented a complete and robust software archi-
tecture for autonomous UAV landing on industrial pipelines
in complex, GNSS-denied environments. The proposed system
effectively addresses the dual challenges of maintaining accu-
rate global localization and achieving high-precision, relative
perception of the target infrastructure.
The main contribution lies in a novel pipeline mapping and
tracking module that fuses sparse detections from a complemen-
tary sensor suite, comprising a depth camera and dual 2D laser
scanners. Jointly processing dual laser scans eliminates single-
scan geometric ambiguity. Additionally, the tracker’s temporal
ﬁltering and state management algorithms effectively rejected
spurious detections and maintained a consistent, high-ﬁdelity
representation of the environment’s pipelines.
The system was rigorously validated through controlled in-
door experiments and fully autonomous missions in a real-world
reﬁnery. Results demonstrate that the tracker achieves less than
3 cm geometric accuracy and angular precision of about 1.5◦,
even during the ﬁnal landing phase.
Future work will focus on extending the perception module to
handle non-horizontal and curved pipelines, and on evaluating
its generalization across different materials, diameters and more
complex scenarios.
