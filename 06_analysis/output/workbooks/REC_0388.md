Multi-UAV Cooperative Navigation Based on
Multisource Information Fusion: A Review
Fujun Song , Qinghua Zeng , Rui Zhang, Xiaohu Zhu, Xiaoyu Ye , and Zongyu Zhang
Abstract—Unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) swarms are
increasingly deployed in both civilian and military domains,
where high-precision navigation in Global Navigation Satel-
lite System (GNSS)-denied environments remains a critical
challenge. Cooperative navigation (CN) has emerged as
a promising paradigm by leveraging intervehicle informa-
tion exchange and multisource information fusion (MSIF) to
enhance swarm navigation performance. Although numerous
surveys have summarized multisensor integration and state
estimation methods for single-platform UAVs, comprehen-
sive reviews dedicated to CN remain limited. This article
presents a structured framework for CN that integrates multi-
source information acquisition, cooperative localization (CL)
techniques in GNSS-denied environments, and multisource
fusion algorithms. On this basis, we provide a systematic
review of CN from three perspectives: relative measure-
ment information, CN architectures, and swarm cooperative
optimization algorithms. Furthermore, fusion algorithms are
categorized into four classes, namely, mathematically derived, graph-based, filtering-based, and learning-based
approaches, with their development trends systematically analyzed. The synergistic features of multisource information
are then discussed, with emphasis on information selection, fault tolerance, and resilient fusion. Finally, the hierarchical
structure of MSIF is highlighted, revealing the evolutionary trend from feature-level toward decision-level strategies. This
survey aims to present a holistic understanding of UAV CN, outline key technical challenges, and shed light on future
research directions in this rapidly evolving field.
Index Terms— Cooperative navigation (CN), multisource information fusion (MSIF), multiple unmanned aerial vehicles
(UAVs).

The research topics and challenges of CN and integrated
navigation exhibit strong similarities, as UAV swarms com-
monly encounter localization difficulties in GNSS-denied
environments. In 2019, Science Robotics highlighted that the
deployment of micro-UAV swarms is primarily constrained
by the lack of autonomous cooperative relative localization

Technology identified “high-precision intelligent navigation
for unmanned systems in satellite-denied environments” as
one of the top ten engineering challenges. A distinguishing
feature of CN lies in its dependence on intervehicle infor-
mation sharing, with an increasing diversity in the types
and combinations of multisource information. For instance,
Li et al. [6] proposed a distributed estimation framework
that integrates ranging, vision, and intermittent position mea-
surements. This method constructs both direct and indirect
geometric constraints among UAVs by exploiting relative
distances and cooperative observation features. Furthermore,
Jarraya et al. [7] introduced a dynamic model-aided navigation
(DMAN) method tailored for small-scale multirotor UAVs,
which can provide temporary navigation support when GNSS
position and velocity measurements are unavailable or unre-
liable. To address these challenges, CN leverages intervehicle
measurement sharing, combined with dedicated algorithms or
strategies, to achieve multisource information fusion (MSIF),
thereby enhancing the overall navigation performance of UAV
swarms. As an emerging paradigm, the fundamental scenario
of CN is illustrated in Fig. 1. In GNSS-denied environments,
UAV swarms can effectively suppress the temporal drift inher-
ent in INS-derived position errors by cooperatively fusing
estimated signal indicators [e.g., time of arrival (TOA)] with
the positional information of neighboring UAVs [8].
In recent years, the rapid advancement of CN technologies
has facilitated their widespread adoption across both military
and civilian applications. Research on multiplatform CN pri-
marily encompasses two dimensions: cooperative architectures
and navigation methods [9], [10]. Regarding cooperative archi-
tectures, CN is commonly categorized into leader–follower and
parallel configurations. Navigation methods primarily address
multisensor integration, information processing, and algorithm
design, and are generally classified into three fundamen-
tal approaches: centralized, hierarchical [11], and distributed
schemes [12]. Currently, relative information measurements
in CN predominantly rely on angle-of-arrival (AOA), rang-
ing [13], and vision-based techniques [14]. In addition, prior
geometric configurations and inter-UAV information sharing
within a swarm are widely employed as critical information
sources [15]. Consequently, CN constitutes a multidisciplinary
and complex technology that can be analyzed from three per-
spectives: relative measurement information, CN architectures,
and swarm cooperative optimization algorithms. Innovations in
navigation methods drive the evolution of cooperative archi-
tectures, while the intrinsic requirements of these architectures
further stimulate advances in navigation methods.

II. CLASSIFICATION OF MULTISOURCE
INFORMATION IN UAV SWARMS
CN for UAVs involves the integration and calibration of
individual navigation information through intervehicle com-
munication links, thereby enhancing the overall navigation
accuracy of the formation [24]. In UAV swarm localization
scenarios, effectively exploiting MSIF is particularly critical
for achieving accurate estimation of position, velocity, and
attitude [25]. As illustrated in the graphical abstract, this
work categorizes and summarizes multisource information,
including external information such as sensor measurements
(as shown in Fig. 2) and carrier model-based data, as well as
system-level features such as topological structure information.
In this context, CN depends on two essential information
sources: external data (e.g., sensor measurements and UAV
carrier information), which form the basis for state estimation
and control, and topological structure information, which
provides the communication infrastructure and transmission
pathways.

B. Model-Aided Information
Compared with exteroceptive sensors, model-based nav-
igation does not require physical sensors and relies on
platform-intrinsic model constraints. It can update the nav-
igation state when external information is unavailable or
unreliable. Model-aided information provides two main advan-
tages: zero additional cost and continuous availability.
1) Kinematic Information: Direct kinematic information con-
verts platform motion into measurements that feed the
navigation filter. Its primary feature is that knowledge of
the INS-carrying platform or its environment can be directly
transformed into measurement information to support the navi-
gation filter [63]. Examples include zero-velocity updates [64],
zero-heading updates [65], and relative position updates [66],
which are widely used in mobile robotics, pedestrian navi-
gation, and UAV applications. In CN, direct methods such
as zero-velocity updates are less common because they rely

C. Geometric Information
Geometric information is one of the core elements for
achieving CL in UAV swarm systems, primarily determined

2) Hierarchical CN: In traditional leader–follower archi-
tectures, the follower’s navigation relies entirely on the
leader [92]. If the leader fails, system reliability is compro-
mised, making single-leader–follower structures unsuitable for
large-scale UAV formations [93]. To address this limitation,
Song et al. [94] proposed a hierarchical CN structure, dividing
UAVs into high-precision and low-precision layers based on
onboard sensor accuracy. Low-precision UAVs acquire CN
information from high-precision UAVs via radar and laser
sensors and construct distance-angle-based cooperative models
to enhance their positioning accuracy [95].
However, continuous leader–follower cooperation encoun-
ters reliability challenges when leaders fail [96]. Hierarchical
CN dynamically layers UAVs based on estimated position-
ing accuracy, enabling cooperative recovery in the event of
node failure [97]. Flexibility is a key feature of hierarchi-
cal cooperative fusion (HCF) algorithms. While increased
connectivity can improve system performance, integrating
low-accuracy or faulty measurements may degrade follower
UAV positioning [96]. Furthermore, using all received coop-
erative information increases computational complexity due
to variations in estimation precision and multipath effects in
wireless environments.
3) Parallel Structure: In parallel CN, all nodes are equipped
with sensors of similar precision and mutually optimize
their positioning through cooperative observations. This mech-
anism introduces correlations in state estimation between
nodes, which are often challenging to model accurately in
practice.
Covariance intersection (CI) is the main approach for par-
allel structure. Julier and Uhlmann [98] first proposed CI,
which avoids the need for internode cross-covariance estima-
tion while enabling CP. Carrillo-Arce et al. [99] developed
a CI-based model: if a node lacks relative observations, it fuses
local sensor data via EKF; if relative observations exist,
local estimates are first computed, then CI is applied to fuse
estimates with neighboring nodes. This process maintains con-
sistency and has an overall computational and communication
complexity of O(N).
Parallel structure treat all UAVs equally; each UAV acts as a
host, and failures in one or more UAVs do not critically affect
others [100]. However, correlations between local estimates
are difficult to model, leading to conservative covariance
bounds in CI methods, limiting CP accuracy. Nonlinear mea-
surements, such as relative distances, are also challenging to
integrate. Therefore, more efficient CP strategies are needed
for complex environments.
4) Cooperative Structure Maintenance and Reconfiguration:
In practical applications, UAV cluster topology dynamically
changes with flight environments and mission objectives,
increasing positioning uncertainty [101]. Under fixed forma-
tions, Inalhan et al. [102] modeled multi-UAV control with
decision systems and established discrete optimal solutions
with global convergence under identifiability and linear
independence constraints. Thrun [103] proposed a Monte
Carlo-based CN algorithm that updates probability distribu-
tions using relative pose information. Jo et al. [104] exploited
differential GPS for relative distance measurements within
formations.
To address UAV heterogeneity in positioning accuracy,
high-precision UAVs can serve as reference platforms to
enhance low-precision UAVs’ navigation using inter-UAV
relative distance information [105]. Further studies [106]
developed multiplatform INS error correction methods and
extended EKF-based relative navigation, enabling joint esti-
mation across the formation. However, these approaches often
assume Gaussian-distributed sensor errors and rely on pairwise
observations, limiting full utilization of cooperative informa-
tion. In conclusion, the central challenge lies in designing
a CL method that effectively integrates multiple sources of
information.

III. CLASSIFICATION OF CL METHOD UNDER
GNSS-DENIED ENVIRONMENTS
Based on a systematic review of multisource information,
this chapter examines a range of representative CN approaches
and establishes a conceptual framework for the subsequent
discussion of specific fusion algorithms. Mitrovic et al. [107]
proposed a comprehensive classification scheme for UAV
navigation, dividing existing methods into five categories:
satellite navigation, INS, terrestrial navigation, geomagnetic
navigation, and vision-based navigation. From the perspec-
tive of measurement modalities, navigation systems can be
further categorized as relative distance-based, relative bearing-
based, relative heading-based, and other types. However, this
taxonomy remains limited. As illustrated in Fig. 5, many com-
monly used sensors exhibit multifunctional characteristics and
therefore cannot be strictly confined to a single classification
category.
In GNSS-denied environments, UAV systems can acquire
relative information from the environment, onboard sensors,
and inter-UAV measurements through various means. Fig. 6
illustrates that these approaches can be broadly categorized
into absolute positioning and relative positioning. Furthermore,
based on the type of information utilized, they can be further
refined into three classes: radio frequency information, visual
information, and dynamic model information. This classifica-
tion facilitates more rational system modeling and the design
of navigation strategies.
A. Classification Based on Localization Methods
1) Relative Localization: RL primarily relies on initial states
and historical observations to estimate the current position,
involving a higher proportion of local measurement infor-
mation. As a typical CL approach, RL aims to infer UAV
positions within local coordinate frames, demonstrating strong
adaptability and practicality in GNSS-denied environments,
such as indoor or complex outdoor scenarios. RL meth-
ods integrate multiple localization strategies, including dead

reckoning, simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM)
[108], optical flow [109], and visual-inertial odometry (VIO)
[110], thereby enabling flexible and high-accuracy localization
solutions in the absence of absolute position information.
Dead reckoning techniques estimate incremental displace-
ments of an agent in real time to update its position.
Representative approaches include INS [111], visual odometry
(VO) [112], and recently developed SLAM algorithms [113].
SLAM provides location estimates while simultaneously con-
structing environmental maps; however, cumulative errors are
inevitable. As flight duration increases, localization errors
accumulate, significantly impacting mission accuracy and
reliability.
In addition, RL also includes CL strategies based on other
agents [114], where GNSS-denied nodes rely on assistance
from multiple agents with accurate localization capabilities.
Han et al. [115] proposed a particle filter (PF)-based approach
that reduces the number of cooperating agents to one, albeit at
the cost of significantly higher computational resources. Sim-
ilarly, vision-based methods [116] and ranging sensor-based
schemes [117] have been developed to determine UAV-anchor
relative positions or by measuring the direction of arrival
(DOA) between GPS-enabled UAVs and GNSS-denied ones.
Furthermore, by integrating joint DOA–TDOA estimation
techniques, MIMO radar-based methods enable UAV swarms
to maintain robust relative positioning even under GNSS-
denied conditions [118].
As a critical component of swarm navigation, RL is
particularly important when interagent distances are small.
In such contexts, RL sensors typically provide more accu-
rate and cost-effective information than absolute localization
approaches [82]. The deployment of efficient inter-UAV RL
algorithms ensures robust CN in complex environments.
Beyond GNSS, existing UAV swarm localization systems
successfully leverage technologies such as cooperative SLAM
(C-SLAM) [119], UWB ranging [120], and motion capture
(MoCap) systems [121], although these methods are primarily
limited to indoor applications. Leveraging these measurements
within CN algorithms significantly enhances overall system
accuracy in GNSS-denied environments.
2) Absolute Localization: In GNSS-denied environments,
absolute localization plays a crucial role in determining UAV
positions with respect to the global Earth-centered coordinate

B. Classification Based on Information Type
1) Radio-Based CN: Radio navigation is one of the essen-
tial means to realize CN for aerial swarms. This technique
provides capabilities such as ranging, angle measurement, and
velocity estimation, thereby enabling high-precision determi-
nation of position, velocity, and attitude. Moreover, it features
fast response and all-weather operability. Commonly adopted
radio navigation methods for swarm CN mainly include satel-
lite navigation [135], sonar [136], radar [137], and lidar-based
approaches [136].
However, these sensors are often expensive and sometimes
bulky, which limits their applicability in outdoor environments.
For instance, sonar systems have a restricted operational
range [138]. Active laser-based systems, such as LiDAR [139],
represent a highly promising sensing technology, as they
can operate under any visibility conditions (i.e., both day
and night, unlike cameras) and directly provide 3-D mea-
surements of the surrounding environment. Nevertheless, the
cost of LiDAR remains relatively high, and its deployment
in certain applications may cause system overload. Fur-
thermore, LiDAR contains moving components, which may
introduce additional measurement errors [140]. To address
this issue, Hedgecock et al. [80] proposed the relative GPS
tracking method, which relies on predetermined base station
receiver positions as intermediate references and subsequently
resolves the spatial positions of nearby nodes through rela-
tive motion comparisons. To overcome the GPS limitations,
to enhance the accuracy of UAVs operating in close formation,
Gross et al. [141] developed a robust relative navigation
method that integrates carrier-phase differential GPS, INS,
and peer-to-peer radio ranging, combining UWB ranging with
multisource data fusion.
In addition, researchers have increasingly focused on CL,
which estimates positions using relative distance and/or
angle information between UAVs, thereby improving the
fault tolerance of navigation systems [142]. Relative dis-
tance measurements can be obtained via UWB radio or
optical systems [143]. Moreover, with the development of
radio direction finding (RDF) technology [144], relative angle
measurements between UAVs have also become feasible,
enabling higher accuracy CL. Extensive studies have addressed
UWB/INS CL. Most UWB-based systems typically assume
prior knowledge of at least two auxiliary node positions [145].
Gentner et al. [146] proposed an SLAM-based localization
scheme that enables pedestrian positioning using three UWB
nodes without prior auxiliary node information, achieving
centimeter-level accuracy; however, it still requires prede-
ployment of three nodes. Liu et al. [147] introduced an
indoor autonomous robot localization framework integrating
INS with UWB signals and employing the Sage-Husa adaptive
filter to enhance both accuracy and robustness, demonstrating
high performance even with only two deployed UWB nodes.
Fallon et al. [148] proposed a CN method for autonomous UVs
(AUVs) using a single maneuvering surface vessel to obtain
azimuth estimates.

GNSS-challenged environments, where UGV motion planning

the benefits of integrating vision-inertial, LiDAR-inertial, and

critical for enhancing robustness and accuracy in UAV local-

3) Model-Based CN: UAV dynamic models have been
widely applied in various fields, including control strategy
design, navigation performance enhancement, sensor substi-
tution, sensor fault diagnosis, and action decision-making via
learning [168]. Consequently, in GNSS-integrated navigation
systems, dynamic models can also serve as replacements for
inertial measurement units (IMUs), enabling attitude estima-
tion in the absence of IMU data [169]. In the so-called
“model-aided navigation” approaches, researchers introduce
accurate INS dynamic models. Koifman and Bar-Itzhack [170]
were among the pioneers in this area, proposing a complete
dynamic model for fixed-wing UAVs as an alternative to GPS
information. As shown in Fig. 6, this approach highlights
a bidirectional correction mechanism between the inertial
system and the dynamic model. Specifically, the model not
only provides predictive information for navigation but is also
updated by observational data to refine its own parameters,
thereby further improving estimation accuracy and enhancing
system robustness [171].
However, there is a prevailing consensus in current research
on UAV state estimation: estimators based on dynamic mod-
els can adapt in real time to variations in rotational and
translational drag parameters during flight, while providing
smooth and lag-free estimates of inertial acceleration [74].
Such estimators do not require expensive sensors or exter-
nal measurement sources, enabling online estimation of
model parameters and offering a feasible pathway for
large-scale deployment of high-performance, low-cost UAV
systems [176].
In the context of CN within data link systems, numerous
studies have focused on achieving high-precision, model-
assisted navigation for multi-UAV systems. For instance,
Yang et al. [128] proposed a CN approach for multiple
UAVs that integrates relative distance information with magne-
tometer measurements. This method leverages magnetometer
outputs to construct the UAV kinematic model, thereby
enhancing positioning accuracy. For a minimal cooperative
unit composed of a leader UAV with known position and
follower UAVs with unknown positions, Zhu et al. [69]
introduced a CP algorithm based on follower motion vector
features to achieve precise pose estimation.
Moreover, Zhou et al. [178] developed a multiagent naviga-
tion approach under the random finite set (RFS) framework,
combining the probability hypothesis density (PHD) and car-
dinalized PHD (CPHD) filters. This algorithm redefines the
virtual potential field construction mechanism, enabling agents
to autonomously adjust their paths according to relative veloc-
ities for obstacle avoidance. Our team conceived a method
that exploits the consistency properties in formation CN, using
a leader UAV aerodynamic prediction model to enhance the
navigation accuracy and robustness of follower UAVs under
GNSS-denied conditions.

However, most existing model-assisted navigation research
primarily focuses on state-level observation fusion, lacking
feature-level fusion strategies commonly seen in visual nav-

be directly used as observable measurements; their temporal

Therefore, future research should emphasize the integration

capability and advance multisource UAV navigation from
traditional state fusion toward feature-driven fusion.

B. Graph-Theoretic Optimization Algorithms
Graph-theoretic optimization methods primarily model the
relationships among all targets within a certain range as a
mathematical graph structure. Starting from each node in the
graph, information flow to other nodes can be analyzed, and
specific algorithms can be designed to optimize the graph
structure, thereby providing an intuitive representation of
abstract problems.
1) Factor Graphs: As shown in Fig. 12, weight factor graphs
can be regarded as an optimization method based on prob-
abilistic graphical models. Due to their high efficiency in
fusing multisource information, they have been widely applied
in fault diagnosis [195], target tracking [196], and robotic
navigation [197]. Leveraging this capability, various factor-
graph-based algorithms for CL have been proposed [198].
These methods typically construct a global optimization cost
function, jointly modeling onboard inertial measurements and
received cooperative information at the current time. However,
they often do not fully consider the differing contributions of
various cooperative information sources in state estimation.
To enhance cooperation performance and trajectory tracking
accuracy in multi-UAV systems, Guerrero et al. [199]
employed Newton–Euler dynamic modeling and a hierarchical
swarm structure to abstract the multi-UAV system into an
information graph, building a nonlinear control framework for
cooperative control. This method effectively integrates system
dynamics with communication structure to achieve coordi-
nated control among multiple vehicles. Shames et al. [200]
proposed a graph-theoretic approach for localization of mobile
agents in a 2-D plane. The method assumes known internode
distances and relative angles, with the overall structure satis-
fying rigidity constraints, enabling CP among multiple agents.
To address the decline in state estimation accuracy caused
by weakened distance constraints in conventional graph
optimization, Zhu et al. [201] proposed a weighted factor-
graph-based CL method guided by navigation performance
evaluation. By assessing the actual contribution of coop-
erative information to navigation performance, low-quality
information sources are filtered out, preventing them from
influencing the final position estimate and improving both
estimation accuracy and computational efficiency. However,
although relative-distance performance evaluation has been
extensively studied in WSNs, it remains less explored in multi-
UAV CN.
Wang et al. [202] proposed a factor-graph-based
resilient CL (FG-RCL) method for multirobot systems, fusing
visual sensor and UWB measurements to achieve precise state
estimation while effectively mitigating limited field-of-view
issues inherent in visual sensors.
2) Bayesian Network (BN): Classical approaches, including
the extended KF (EKF), PF, and optimization-based tech-
niques, typically rely on centralized processing architectures,
requiring a central processor to compute all agent positions,
making them unsuitable for distributed systems and vulnerable
to single-point failures [11]. To address these limitations,
BN methods have been introduced.
BN is an efficient inference technique based on a Bayesian
framework and operates on factor graphs, and their effec-
tiveness has been extensively validated. As a distributed
inference mechanism, BN enables state estimation in networks
without central control, offering strong scalability and robust-
ness [203]. Li et al. [204] used graph theory to study the
localization method of mobile agents in 2-D space. Consider-
ing the time-varying characteristics of the network topology,
Wymeersch et al. [205] proposed a distributed CN algorithm
based on the network factor graph. The algorithm can handle
a variety of scenarios and can achieve accurate and robust
positioning with little communication.

goal-driven navigation tasks. The Voronoi local maps map
semantic subgoals to specific spatial locations through online
construction, enabling efficient path planning.

C. Filter-Based Optimization Algorithms
1) KF and EKF: Numerous filtering-based fusion algorithms
have been proposed, with performance dependent on the
application scenario and modeling approach. In Kalman-filter-
based fusion, system architectures are typically classified as
centralized or hierarchical/distributed, depending on whether
raw measurements are directly used [11]. In centralized fusion,
all sensor measurements are transmitted to a fusion cen-
ter, which constructs high-dimensional measurement vectors
for state estimation, achieving theoretically optimal global
accuracy [209]. However, this requires high computational
and communication resources, limiting real-time performance
and scalability. Distributed fusion allows subsystems (agent
nodes) to perform local estimation independently, later com-
bining results into a global state. This approach reduces
computational and communication load, facilitates sensor fault
detection and isolation (FDI), and enhances system sur-
vivability and robustness in dynamic environments. Despite
various improvements and extensions of the KF, no univer-
sal method simultaneously achieves optimal performance in
computational complexity, error control, and noise modeling
accuracy. In practical applications, achieving low computa-
tional overhead, nonaccumulating errors, and accurate mod-
eling of complex noise is challenging, leaving ample research
opportunities.
To address the limited accuracy of Kalman-filter-based
fusion methods under highly nonlinear system or observation
models in CN scenarios [213], several improvements have
been proposed, such as distributed EKFs [214], UKFs [215],
and cubature KFs (CKFs) [216], improving fusion accuracy
and robustness. For example, Liu et al. [217] proposed a
CN algorithm using ranging and velocity measurements, with
EKF estimating follower state errors. Results show significant
improvements in positioning accuracy compared to single INS
or ranging-only methods.
However, Kalman-filter-based swarm fusion methods typ-
ically
rely
on

practice. When relative observations exist, the computational
complexity of fusing N nodes can reach O(N 4), severely
limiting scalability. In addition, these methods only use the
previous state and current observation, underutilizing historical
information and constraining accuracy in large-scale coop-
erative systems. Trujillo et al. [140] proposed a localization
method fusing UWB ranging with inertial observations using
EKF for target state estimation. Although only a single aux-
iliary node is required, prior knowledge of the target node’s
position and heading is assumed. Jung et al. [218] developed
a quaternion-error-state EKF based on IMU propagation for
distributed CN, improving both accuracy and scalability.
While Bayesian-filter-based fusion methods achieve high
positioning precision, their computational complexity grows
with time. Common practice uses a fixed-time window, yet the
relationship between positioning accuracy and window length
remains underexplored. For micro-UAVs with limited onboard
resources, optimizing fusion computation while maintaining
high accuracy, exploiting distributed computation, and mini-
mizing communication remains a critical challenge.
2) Invariant EKF (IEKF): IEKFs differ from conventional
EKF by defining state variables on matrix Lie groups [219].
Traditional EKF represents states in vector space, modeling
attitude via direction cosine matrices or quaternions, while
position and velocity reside in Euclidean space. In contrast,
IEKF models position and velocity within the Lie group, more
closely integrating system geometric properties.
Due to the high maneuverability of UAV swarms, their flight
states often exhibit pronounced nonlinear characteristics at
certain operating points. Under such conditions, the traditional
EKF is prone to estimation bias and even divergence, which
can severely degrade filtering performance and consistency.
However, the core advantage of IEKF lies in invariant error
propagation, decoupling error dynamics from the current state
and rendering the state transition matrix independent of the
trajectory. Consequently, IEKF maintains numerical stability
and estimation accuracy even with initial state errors, outper-
forming EKF and UKF in robustness and consistency.
This invariance extends to observation modeling: if the
observation model is left-invariant or right-invariant, the mea-
surement matrix does not depend on the state estimate,
ensuring Kalman gain remains trajectory-independent. In mul-
tisensor systems, left-invariant observations include acoustic
positioning [220], GNSS position measurements [221], and
velocity measurements, while right-invariant observations
often come from DVL or wheel odometry. In these scenar-
ios, centralized right-invariant EKF (RIEKF) is commonly
used. However, for large state estimation errors, RIEKF
performance may be limited due to trajectory-dependent mea-
surement matrices. Conversely, left-invariant EKF (LIEKF)
performs better under such conditions. As shown in Fig. 13,
Hwang et al. [222] proposed a federated filtering frame-
work where different subfilters handle different observation
types: LIEKF for left-invariant measurements and RIEKF
for right-invariant measurements. Since all subfilters update
with trajectory-independent Kalman gains, the overall sys-
tem achieves significantly improved positioning accuracy and
robustness compared to centralized architectures.
3) AKF: Within the Kalman filtering framework, the current
state estimate and its uncertainty depend on information from
subsequent observations. KFs can correct raw sensor measure-
ments affected by noise to produce estimates closer to the
true values, thereby improving sensor output accuracy [223].
However, KF performance is highly sensitive to the system
model dynamics and the accurate characterization of process
and measurement noise statistics [224].

determine precisely. Traditional KF approaches typically fix
one covariance matrix (e.g., Q or R) and adaptively tune the
other to improve filtering performance. To address this, [225]

4) Unscented Kalman Filter: The UKF is widely employed

modeling characteristics. Unlike the traditional EKF, which
linearizes system dynamics, UKF utilizes the unscented

sampling-based nonlinear approach. By selecting a set of
representative sigma points in the state space and propagating
them through the system dynamics, UKF can estimate the
mean and covariance of state variables with higher accuracy,
thereby outperforming EKF in state estimation [231].
In standard UKF, the process and measurement noise
covariance matrices ( Q and R) are typically fixed. However,
in real-world nonlinear systems such as UAVs and AUVs,
uncertainties and time-varying noise in dynamic environ-
ments are common [232]. If noise statistics are unknown or
inaccurately estimated, UKF performance may significantly
degrade [233]. To address this, adaptive noise covariance
adjustment mechanisms and robust estimation concepts have
been introduced to enhance the filter’s capability against
time-varying noise and outlier measurements [210]. Li et al.
[234] proposed a data-fusion-based adaptive UKF for multi-
UAV systems, achieving optimal CL among platforms.
Similarly, Khaghani and Skaloud [235] applied UKF to
rotorcraft VIO systems, enabling high-precision navigation
under high-speed flight conditions.
Despite its advantages in estimation accuracy, UKF entails
high computational complexity. Similar to PFs, real-time
implementation on embedded platforms remains challenging.
For large-scale cluster drones, the significant demand for
computing resources is a practical limitation factor for the
deployment of algorithms.

navigation solutions [211].

algorithm stability to some extent, inherent instability in
multiagent systems remains unresolved.
To address these challenges, Kang et al. [255] modeled
multiagent joint optimization tasks as a partially observable
Markov decision process (POMDP) and, considering resource
constraints, energy limitations, and collision avoidance, pro-
posed a multiagent proximal policy optimization (MAPPO)
algorithm. This method requires relatively low exploration,
achieving efficient training while maintaining good navigation
performance.
In relative navigation systems under complex environments,
to handle possible failures of range sensors, some studies have
proposed enhancing system robustness via ensemble learning.
For instance, Sheng et al. [256] employed the AdaBoost
algorithm to combine multiple weak learners into a strong
learner, enabling robust fault state prediction.
3) Deep Learning: Recently, DL techniques have made sig-
nificant breakthroughs in CV and have been widely applied to
UAV positioning, remote sensing perception, and vision-based
autonomous navigation [257]. With rapid advances in image
processing and CV, DL-based visual navigation research has
expanded across various platforms [258], [259]. Its core lies
in extracting navigation-relevant features from preprocessed
image data. Golroudbari and Sabour [260] reviewed end-to-
end DL applications in autonomous navigation, covering not
only localization but also obstacle detection, scene understand-
ing, path planning, and control.
In GNSS-denied conditions, geomagnetic navigation is con-
sidered an important alternative. Cuenca et al. [261] proposed
a DL-based geomagnetic navigation framework designed
for GPS-degraded environments, demonstrating robust and
adaptive navigation performance. Leveraging DL’s strong rep-
resentation capabilities, image sensor data can be directly
mapped to control commands for end-to-end UAV autonomy.
Amer et al. [262] used onboard RGB camera images to train
CNNs to generate steering commands, achieving vision-based
autonomous navigation.
AI methods, with strong feature learning and efficient
function approximation capabilities, demonstrate significant
potential in UAV navigation. Current research mainly focuses
on two directions: 1) bio-inspired navigation mechanisms,
constructing a “UAV brain” model with spatial cognition to
achieve end-to-end integration of vision, perception, and local-
ization; and 2) learning-driven navigation systems that rapidly
identify navigation states, diagnose anomalies, and recon-
struct systems in complex, dense swarm flight environments,
enhancing adaptability to dynamic environments. Integrating
AI deeply with navigation systems not only improves accu-
racy and robustness but also reduces modeling complexity
and adaptation costs. Therefore, intelligent CN in complex
environments is expected to be a key direction for future UAV
system development [258].
V. ANALYSIS OF KEY ISSUES AND PROSPECTS BASED
ON MULTISOURCE INFORMATION CHARACTERISTICS
Building upon recent research advances, this study further
analyzes and discusses the key challenges in CN technolo-
gies characterized by multisource information. On one hand,
we examine the resilient design of fusion strategies, empha-
sizing the selection, switching, and fault-tolerant mechanisms
associated with multisource data, multiple signal types, and
various subsystems. We explore the emerging trend of infor-
mation fusion evolving from the data level to the feature and
decision levels, thereby underscoring the growing potential of
multilevel fusion frameworks in enhancing CN performance.
A. Multisignal Source Selection
1) Limitations: As illustrated in Fig. 16, in complex scenar-
ios involving heterogeneous swarm flight, providing accurate
and reliable navigation information is fundamental for UAV
formation control. In CN, the commonly used GNSS receivers
can achieve high-precision measurements and positioning in
open environments. Nevertheless, multipath effects and NLOS
reception [263] can significantly degrade the performance
of GNSS-based relative navigation systems, manifesting as
reduced accuracy, integrity, and continuity, and in some cases,
even signal denial [10]. Furthermore, due to constraints in
cost, payload, endurance, and size, a single UAV cannot carry
multiple high-precision sensors simultaneously [264]. These
issues present significant challenges to CN in multi-UAV
systems.
As summarized in Section II, the information fusion process
among multiple UAVs can be categorized into three naviga-
tion modes: multileader, single-leader, and distributed [265].

Among them, the single-leader [6] and multileader modes are
more common. The CN algorithms in these modes are similar
in principle to multisatellite positioning, making the algorithms
relatively simple and well-established [95]. In distributed CN
algorithms, the suppression of pure INS positioning errors
theoretically depends on both the number and quality of
UAVs in the formation [266]. High-precision positioning in
all three modes relies on the optimal performance of reference
nodes. In addition, the relative configuration among nodes is
a critical factor that can significantly reduce or even diverge
the positioning accuracy of individual nodes [267].
To enhance UAV state estimation accuracy while reduc-
ing computational complexity, cooperative systems require
evaluation of cooperative information performance and data
fusion based on geometric or probabilistic approaches. Con-
sidering the optimal spatial geometric configuration (OSGC)
between follower and leader UAVs, the relative distance
information of leader UAVs with known positions can be
used as observation vectors to achieve high-precision CL for
follower UAVs. Causa et al. [150] proposed a multi-UAV
formation geometry for CN in the GPC challenge environ-
ment. When the precise positions of leader UAVs are known,
the aforementioned distance-based CL method selects leader
nodes based on spatial geometry, but does not fully assess
the impact of relative distance performance on system posi-
tioning accuracy. Traditional distance-based CL algorithms
often ignore CN performance information, and poor-quality
cooperative data can degrade follower UAV state estimation
accuracy [201]. By leveraging the Fisher criterion and rel-
ative entropy, cooperative UAV node positions and related
distance information are comprehensively evaluated. The con-
tributions of selected cooperative nodes’ positions and distance
measurements to tracking UAV state estimation are weighted
to achieve improved accuracy. Using only two-hop measure-
ments based on TOA and AOA, a WLSs method is proposed
to distribute the computation of specific target nodes in 3-D
environments. Moreover, the impact of TOA and AOA mea-
surement errors on positioning accuracy is analyzed based on
geometric relationships [87].
Whether in leader–follower or hierarchical cooperative
frameworks, the problem essentially reduces to the selection
of reference UAVs. Rigid selection schemes in CN can lead to
inefficiencies in the reference UAV group. To enhance system
robustness and accuracy, multireference CN is necessary. How-
ever, existing studies have primarily focused on filtering signal
sources based on relative distance and directional information
to select the optimal reference for the target UAV, which
remains insufficient.
2) Prospects: In summary, CN relies on high-precision
UAVs within a formation to act as reference signal sources,
while UAVs with lower positioning accuracy are designated
as targets to be localized, obtaining navigation information
from surrounding reference UAVs via data links. Tradi-
tional leader–follower CN achieves high-precision positioning
primarily by designating high-end, well-equipped UAVs as
references. In scenarios where high-configuration UAVs are
not explicitly assigned, it becomes challenging to identify
which UAV maintains high accuracy under signal denial con-
ditions or leader failures, thereby complicating leader–follower
distinction. Moreover, most formation CN algorithms empha-
size the use of cooperative information to assist absolute
navigation, while relatively less attention is paid to relative
navigation [88]. Previous multisensor fusion-based coopera-
tive relative navigation methods for UAV formations share a
common limitation: the relative positions between UAV pairs
are estimated only using the multisensor information of those
two UAVs, resulting in low utilization of multi-UAV infor-
mation and limited improvements in the accuracy, integrity,
and continuity of relative navigation. Evidently, formation
CN must address three critical issues: 1) reliability of high-
precision layers; 2) multireference navigation optimization;
and 3) relative navigation within the formation.
B. Resilient Fusion of Multisource Information
1) Limitations: Traditional sensor fusion frameworks mainly
focus on individual sensor combinations to enhance envi-
ronmental adaptability, often neglecting cooperative operation
among sensors. The improvement of multi-UAV CN accuracy
also depends on effective MSIF. Relative sensor noise filtering
and measurement fusion can be implemented using EKF.
In existing CN algorithms, EKF-based methods [268] or
equivalent information filter approaches are widely used due
to their computational efficiency and accuracy. However,
their theoretical consistency cannot be guaranteed. Notably,
although InEKF and Lie group theory have demonstrated
remarkable performance in robotic pose estimation, they also
face challenges in sensor validity detection, which is similarly
critical for CN.
Current solutions in the literature generally follow three
strategies: 1) fusion of information from other nodes, such

that rely on numerous if-else structures for sensor validity
detection, it is crucial to maximize the residual value of sen-

During switching among different sensor or measurement
submodels, the interacting multiple model (IMM) framework
provides an effective solution and has been widely applied in
target tracking and localization [277]. In IMM, model switch-
ing refers to instantaneous transitions between submodels,
as shown in Fig. 17. In CIMM [278], the transition proba-
bility matrix (TPM) π is manually tuned, performing well
in relatively stable systems. However, as Johnston and Krish-
namurthy citebib:294 point out, the accuracy of IMM-based
ISIU strategies is highly sensitive to the TPM π, representing
a Markov chain. Inappropriate transition probabilities can
significantly affect fusion results and hinder proper submodel
selection. Therefore, a more scientific approach to determining
the transition probabilities is required, as π should not be
considered a constant matrix [280].
2) Prospects: In previous studies [281], we investigated
navigation algorithms with dynamic model correction, focus-
ing on multisource sensor reliability and interactive resilient
fusion frameworks. Due to the low cost and compact size of
MEMS-IMUs, integrating multiple IMUs into a system can
enhance both robustness and accuracy [282]. This approach
reduces overall noise levels, making inertial measurements
more representative of actual values and consequently improv-
ing system accuracy [283]. Previous research has also
investigated high-grade IMUs [284], and experimental results
using multiple MEMS-IMUs show that employing four IMUs
can improve navigation performance by 30%–50%. Theoret-
ically, the optimal weighting of each IMU depends on its
measurement noise variance. Most studies assume identical
noise characteristics for IMUs of the same type and assign
equal weights for fusion [285]. Estimating the online noise
variance of MEMS-IMUs [286] as a performance metric and
incorporating it into a multisource resilient fusion framework
is therefore of significant research value.
As illustrated in [287], based on a simplified, dimensionless
sensor reliability evaluation scheme, we proposed an improved
multisource resilient fusion algorithm for redundant relative
navigation using IEKF as a submodel. We also designed
an ES-RIEKF-based multirate full-source relative navigation
framework, which accounts for both interactive measurement
fusion and prediction stage interactions. Sensor reliability
metrics are employed to design the multimodel transition prob-
ability matrix, thereby reducing the sensitivity of submodel
weights.
Future work should explore the integration of conven-
tional perception sensors, such as cameras, LiDAR, and
millimeter-wave radar, with UAV external auxiliary informa-
tion, including kinematic and dynamic models, as well as
topological information. In such a multisource fusion sys-
tem, the transition probabilities between different subfilter
models should adaptively change according to the system
state. For current cooperative systems, an updated sensor
reliability evaluation framework [288] can guide the design of
the filter transition probability matrix. Moreover, the resilient
fusion framework should analyze redundant information in
previously unconsidered submodels, exploring its impact on
performance and potential benefits for inertial navigation,
ultimately enabling highly perceptive environmental awareness
and precise prediction of navigation states.
C. Fault-Tolerant Approaches Based on Multisubsystems
1) Limitations: The FKF offers several advantages [289]:
1) higher accuracy; 2) good fault-tolerance performance; and
3) a relatively simple global information fusion algorithm
with low computational cost. The primary strength of FKF

lies in its ability to distribute and adjust information between
subfilters and the main filter according to the information
allocation principle. With the development of UAV swarm
localization, fault-tolerant CN based on federated filtering
has become a research focus [290], mainly in centralized
and distributed architectures [291]. Centralized CN requires
transmitting measurements from all UAVs to a central station
for collective processing [292], but faces limitations such as
high computational burden and reduced fault tolerance.
To address inconsistencies in conventional distributed
EKF-based synchronization, research has explored IEKF,
a nonlinear observer based on Lie group theory. Unlike
standard EKF, IEKF defines state variables on matrix Lie
groups and maintains linearization accuracy even with inaccu-
rate initial states, showing superior precision and consistency
across various UAV applications [16]. Another critical aspect
in FKF design is selecting an appropriate adaptive federated
information distribution coefficient, which directly affects the
structure, characteristics, and filtering accuracy [293]. How-
ever, in complex operational environments, sensors may be
affected by external disturbances, increasing measurement
errors [294]. To address this issue, extensive research has
been conducted on the allocation of subfilter information
weights in federated filters [211]. Gao et al. [295] proposed
a Markov distance-based adaptive federated KF, introducing
adaptive fusion and allocation coefficients during informa-
tion fusion when subsystems malfunction, thereby improving
robustness. Wang et al. [296] developed an INS/GNSS/DVL
integrated navigation algorithm using a robust, strongly adap-
tive federated KF. Similarly, Guo et al. [297] introduced an
observability-sharing factor considering both system character-
istics and state estimation performance for nonlinear sampling
in integrated navigation, followed by a robust integrated nav-
igation framework with distributed federated filtering.
In practice, sensor noise may also vary over time, lead-
ing to unknown or time-varying noise covariance matrices.
In conventional federated filters, the state noise covariance
matrix (SNCM) and measurement noise covariance matrix
(MNCM) for each subfilter are usually treated as constant, ren-
dering them unsuitable for dynamic noise [298]. To adaptively
accommodate inaccuracies in measurements [299], AFKFs
are required. Existing AFKF approaches—whether based on
innovations or subfilter failure probabilities [300]—suffer from
high uncertainty due to assumptions in subsystem fault prob-
abilities. Advanced adaptive methods, such as multimodel
adaptive KFs [301] and NN-based adaptive KFs [302], perform
poorly in estimating SNCM and MNCM and cannot be readily
extended to federated frameworks. Therefore, current AFKF
methods cannot fully address covariance estimation under
unknown or time-varying noise, and adaptive strategies for
federated filters require further development.
VB approaches have become increasingly popular in adap-
tive filtering [303]. For example, [304] introduced a VB-based
filter to estimate states under inaccurate inputs in complex
marine conditions, deriving augmented state vectors, pre-
diction error covariances, and measurement noise matrices
more precisely. Wang et al. [2] proposed a VB-based adap-
tive federated KF for joint state estimation, achieving high
reliability and precise positioning under strong UAV maneu-
vers. However, direct fusion of unprocessed measurements into
the main filter can degrade accuracy. Chang et al. [305] further
proposed VB-ESKF, which adaptively estimates process and
measurement noise covariance, enhancing filter adaptability
for LiDAR-INS integrated navigation systems.
However, conventional federated filter information alloca-
tion strategies are insufficient for estimating swarm motion in
complex environments. For high-dimensional nonlinear sys-
tems, conventional EKF may fail to achieve sufficient accuracy
and stability; PF [306] and UKF [307] provide better state
estimation at the cost of increased computational complexity.
In distributed federated filters, the information distribution
principle among subfilters fundamentally affects integrated
navigation accuracy and fault tolerance. Chen et al. [198]
proposed a hybrid CN method combining factor graphs and
KFs for UAV swarms, integrating GNSS and ranging infor-
mation for improved distributed filter position estimation.
Tang et al. [308] developed an information-geometric multi-
source fusion approach for real-time UAV swarm localization,
effectively suppressing abrupt errors and achieving rapid posi-
tioning. Zhang et al. [309] introduced an adaptive fault-tolerant
federated filtering method, fusing follower UAV states, leader
UAV positions, and inter-UAV range measurements for CL.
Moreover, cooperative systems often neglect external auxiliary
information, such as aerodynamic coefficients for fixed-wing
UAVs, which are difficult to measure directly.
2) Prospects: Our previous work integrated aerodynamic
information from leader UAVs and employed LSTM-based
aerodynamic model predictions for navigation correction [20].
Building upon the aforementioned multisource framework,
each type of information can be regarded as a subsystem
supporting fault-tolerant navigation through advanced filtering.
Specifically, in cases of inaccurate main-node initialization or
deviated state estimates, we proposed a multinode federated
structure that leverages multisource information from a single
UAV to achieve fault-tolerant processing, which was shown
in Fig. 18. By adopting an IEKF with multiple subnodes, the
system can compensate for dynamic subnode failures, thereby
extending the concept of single-UAV multisource fusion to
multi-UAV multisource fusion, and substantially improving the
fault tolerance and robustness of CN.

D. Different Levels of MSIF
1) Limitations: As shown in Fig. 19, the architectures
of MSIF can be categorized into data-level, feature-level,
and decision-level fusion [311]. Classical information fusion
theories are mainly based on statistical inference and math-
ematical estimation methods, typically applied to handle
incomplete data, including heterogeneous data types, low
reliability, or partial information [312]. For radar and cam-
era fusion strategies, three main approaches are commonly
employed: data-level fusion, feature-level fusion, and decision-
level fusion [313].
Data-level fusion is performed at the lowest data layer
and directly combines raw sensor measurements, such as
radar and camera data [314]. In navigation systems, data-level
fusion primarily addresses information uncertainty [315], data
heterogeneity [316], and data correlation [317]. This approach
preserves the original measurement information, achieving
high accuracy, reliability, and low noise. However, it suffers
from large data volume, poor real-time performance, and is
limited to homogeneous data. Although data-level processing
is easy to implement, its weak robustness necessitates carefully
designed fusion algorithms, which may introduce new errors
and uncertainties—a common challenge in our research.
Feature-level fusion uses extracted features from sensor
data as inputs, producing either refined features or higher
level data, such as decisions [318]. Feature extraction typ-
ically includes radar-derived position and velocity, as well
as visual features like image edges and textures. Combining
heterogeneous sensor features at this level leverages their
complementary advantages, enhancing feature accuracy and
richness [319]. In navigation, incorporating carrier dynamic
features to capture motion characteristics influenced by spe-
cific environments, tasks, and scenarios is also an effective
multisource CN strategy. For example, Yang and Gao [320]
proposed an adaptive KF approach, which quantifies the
contribution of dynamic model information and observation
model information to PNT fusion results, enabling multisource
integration of dynamic and navigation information [321].
Fig. 19. Three types of fusion architectures and their comparison [311].
At the micro-level, multisource navigation systems contain
diverse sensor devices and inherent dynamic subsystems, lead-
ing to heterogeneous information evolution patterns. Accurate
characterization of sensor and dynamic system information
features can enhance system perception accuracy. Detailed and
comprehensive feature extraction allows processing of both
similar and heterogeneous sensor data, significantly reducing
data volume but potentially increasing information loss.

feature-level fusion. It integrates preliminary decisions derived

To address low accuracy caused by threshold limitations in
traditional decision-level methods, Wang et al. [321] proposed

navigation systems typically convert their state behaviors
into cognitive representations understandable by navigation

subsequently expressed in machine-readable form, ultimately
providing intelligent PNT services [322].
However, traditional information fusion methods rely on
specific constraints, and inappropriate thresholds or settings
may lead to missed or false decisions. Simple NNs often fail to
effectively capture both local and global information, resulting
in unsatisfactory performance when fusing at the boundaries
of ambiguous or overlapping information regions. More-
over, most existing studies focus on conventional navigation
methods and rarely incorporate model-derived feature priors,
limiting their capability to perform intelligent decision-making
for complex, cross-scenario CN.
2) Prospects: At the feature level, the focus is primarily
on intelligent dynamic modeling, multisensor resilient fusion
methods, and optimal node selection, aiming to enhance the
detectability and reconfigurability of CN information and to
enable effective feature extraction and evaluation of mul-
tisource navigation data. Ultimately, a dynamic evaluation
standard for MSIF can be established. Facing complex and
heterogeneous information sources, especially in CN sys-
tems, such an evaluation framework allows the system’s
reliability to adapt to temporal and environmental variations.
For example, the weights of individual information sources
can be dynamically adjusted based on real-time assessment
results, improving both the accuracy and robustness of the
state estimation. Furthermore, the fusion results can be fed
back into the navigation task evaluation process, allowing
dynamic adjustment of source weights according to fusion
accuracy, thereby realizing closed-loop control of the fusion
process.
Research can also focus on the self-learning capability
of carrier-borne multisource information, emphasizing the
reliability and completeness of navigation system decision-
making [323]. By constructing different scoring matrices
according to task-specific weights and importance and per-
forming weighted fusion, an intelligent and trustworthy
autonomous multisource navigation system can be realized
with dynamic iterative optimization [324]. Given continuously
changing environments, unpredictable faults and disturbances,
and dynamically evolving navigation tasks, DL can leverage
NNs to regress and fit sensor-predicted results, mitigating
the limitations of traditional methods and handling incon-
sistent data [325]. To this end, imitation learning can be
employed, integrating CN information features into the learn-
ing framework. By collecting navigation databases and training
NNs, the system can generalize to other scenarios, enabling
intelligent decision-making and dynamic iterative optimization
of navigation behavior in complex environments.
VI. CONCLUSION
Building upon a comprehensive review of related literature,
this article systematically analyzes the fundamental concepts
and research significance of multi-UAV CN, with a particu-
lar focus on navigation methods based on MSIF. From the
perspectives of “multisource information” and “information
fusion,” the study provides a thorough classification of multi-
source information and, on this basis, summarizes the technical
types employed in CN. Subsequently, the main research
progress of MSIF algorithms is systematically reviewed.
Considering current research trends, this article further
analyzes and envisions the key issues in CN technology
based on multisource information characteristics. On one hand,
the resilience of fusion strategies is discussed with respect
to the selection, switching, and fault-tolerant mechanisms
among multisource data, multiple signal sources, and multiple
subsystems. On the other hand, attention is given to the
evolution of information fusion from the data level to feature
and decision levels, highlighting the potential of multilevel
fusion to enhance navigation robustness and intelligence.
