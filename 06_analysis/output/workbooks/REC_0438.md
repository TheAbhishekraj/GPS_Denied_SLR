Abstract— This paper presents the development of an object 
path following algorithm for a quadcopter using on-board vision 
and localization. This development aims to increase capabilities 
of autonomous unmanned aerial vehicles (UAV), with the use of 
object path following. By following an object’s path, reliance on 
obstacle avoidance strategies can be reduced, under the 
assumption that the object’s path is free of static obstacles.  
Relative position of the target has been estimated using on-
board processing and monocular vision. On-board localization 
has been achieved through dead-reckoning, where velocity 
estimates are calculated from optical flow. Horizontal positional 
estimates with a standard deviation of 6.5cm have been achieved 
experimentally with this method. The use of optical flow allows 
for localization in GPS-denied environments.  
Both the position and path following can successfully run in 
moderate winds and variable lighting. The UAV can station-
keep to within 40 cm of desired position. The object’s path can 
be followed with an rms error of 28 cm and maximum deviation 
of 78 cm. 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) are now being utilized 
for a wide range of tasks, in particular for search and rescue, 
videography, surveillance, and asset monitoring. This is 
mainly due to their compactness and high degree of 
maneuverability. Through the improvement of UAV 
autonomy, these and other potential tasks have become much 
more readily available to users. 
The goal of the present work is to develop a system able to 
autonomously track the path of a dynamic object. For any form 
of autonomous flying, obstacle avoidance is a necessity, in 
order to protect the UAV, and humans, from potential harm. 
Path-tracking is of interest due to its inherent obstacle 
avoidance, from the fact that the target was able to take its path 
so the UAV should be able to as well. Dynamic obstacles 
would not be avoided by this method. This algorithm is 
intended to be used to follow a human at the same height (2D 
path-tracking), like when following and filming from a 3rd 
person perspective. 
The problem of automated UAV object following has been 
the subject of research at institutes and companies across the 
world. Another area of interest is object recognition algorithms 
and the use of integrated visions systems for sensing the 
environment around the object. Pestana et al. [1] use vision as 
a sensor on-board a UAV and a ground station to perform the 
complex image processing. Limiting the operation of the 
autonomous UAV to within transmitting distance of a ground-
station severely limits the possible applications. Lin et al. [2] 
describe a vision system to follow a ground target using a 
 
 
UAV. They recommend basing a colour detecting algorithm 
on hue, saturation and value (HSV) colours. This method of 
characterizing colours leads to better object detection in 
changing light conditions. Kendall et al. [3] performed object 
detection at a frequency of 8.1Hz through the use of a 
Raspberry Pi for image processing and its accompanying 
camera module. Charmed Labs has created an open source 
embedded colour vision sensor called CMUcam5 [4]. Its on-
board object detection algorithms and hardware had been 
optimized to enable tracking at 50Hz. 
Teulière et al. [5] describe a system in which a quadcopter 
follows a ground target using a colour-based tracking with on-
board vision. Their system was able to deal with occlusions of 
the target by assuming the target velocity remains the same. 
This worked for most cases but doesn’t account for larger 
acceleration or orientation changes. Compensation due to 
attitude changes of the craft were required as the camera was 
fixed. Their aim was to fly directly overhead the target. 
Pestana et al. [1] used image-based visual servoing to maintain 
a horizontal offset from the target they were tracking. They 
successfully followed a target for a period of 45 seconds. Lai 
et al. [6] used a leader-follower arrangement to allow one 
UAV to follow another. A relaxed formation was used to allow 
them to avoid obstacles whilst still remaining in range to each 
other. 
Grabe et al. [7] utilized optical flow to estimate velocity of 
a quadcopter, which allowed them to supplement or replace 
inertial measurement unit (IMU) data for closed-loop control 
of the craft. This computation was completed using only on-
board processors, enabling control of the craft in a body-
centered frame. 
Instead of attempting to maintain a set distance from a 
tracked object, targets can be created along a desired path for 
the UAV to reach. A system of waypoint following [8], allows 
the UAV to travel to each position at a time offset from the 
object. Waypoint following has been widely used in the UAV 
industry, however this usually entails the use of global 
positioning system (GPS). GPS has limitations in availability 
and accuracy, such as in urban environments or indoors, where 
there is no line of site to satellites or reflections of signals are 
present. GPS position estimation causes waypoints to be 
positioned in the range of meters apart. The inclusion of other 
types of sensing technology, such as vision, allows for the 
waypoints to be situated closer together. This allows for 
smoother, and thus more accurate, path following.  
This paper presents an on-board solution to localization 
and visual tracking of a target. Localization is performed 
through dead-reckoning of velocity estimates. These estimates

II. EXPERIMENTAL HARDWARE 
Experimental tests were carried out on a custom built 
quadcopter craft, as shown in Fig. 1. Its frame is based on a 
DJI Flame Wheel F450. A quadcopter was chosen for this task 
due to its high maneuverability compared to other UAV [9]. A 
MaxBotix XL-EZ4 sonar was used to provide accurate altitude 
measurements. An on-board Pixhawk [10] flight controller 
with IMU was used to implement both the high and low level 
controllers.  
For craft position information used by the controller, a 
PX4Flow [11] optical flow sensor estimated craft velocity for 
use in dead-reckoning. These calculations were performed on-
board the flight controller using the ArduCopter extended 
Kalman filter (EKF) [12].  
To provide visual sensing, a Pixy camera was used, which 
employs hue based object detection to provide object position 
and size information to the controller. A red ball (210mm 
diameter) was used as the object to track. This enabled the Pixy 
camera to provide data at up to 6m. When closer than 1m the 
camera’s performance is diminished significantly. Given the 
intended separation distance between the craft and object of 
3m this is not an issue. A two degree-of-freedom camera 
gimbal was used to stabilize the camera during rolling and 
pitching of the craft. This removed the need for software 
compensation and maintained the full field of view forwards. 
No work has been done to solve the problem of occlusions as 
of yet, although some other work presents potential solutions 
to this [5, 6]. 
As the weight of processors decreases, general feature 
tracking will become much more readily available for use in 
lightweight systems, removing the need for a specific target 
type. On-board dynamic obstacle avoidance strategies will 
also become easier to implement, further extending UAV 
capabilities.

IV. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE 
A. Localization 
Both velocity and position information is required to 
perform the intended tasks. With the intention of running 
without the use of GPS, an optical flow sensor was used to 
gather velocity data. This data was provided as velocities for 
both pitch and roll in the body frame. When combined with 
IMU and sonar range data the UAV’s velocities in 𝑥𝑒 & 𝑦𝑒 can 
be found using (5). 
Position information was found using the velocity data 
through dead-reckoning at 100Hz. With dead-reckoning, 
position change is estimated through integration of discrete 
velocity measurement. The disadvantage of this method is drift 
caused by accumulation of small errors, although this is 
minimized due to the accuracy and high frequency of the 
measurements. For larger time periods, a method to correct the 
drift would likely be required. 
B. Controller design 
The following controllers were designed to maintain the 
position of the UAV in the Earth frame, the heading of the 
UAV to face the target object, and the height of the UAV 
above the ground. These controllers enable the UAV to hold 
its position accurately, however, with changes to the position 
reference input, object following can be achieved. 
To maintain altitude whilst the other controllers perform 
path following and object tracking, the ‘AltHold’ control mode 
from ArduCopter has been used. A fusion of on-board 
barometer and a sonar range-finder data are used to estimate 
the UAV’s altitude. 
1) Yaw tracking controller 
To maximize usage of the camera’s field of view (FOV), a 
yaw tracking controller maintains the craft’s movement 
towards the target. This regulates the angle offset found from 
the image to zero, achieved through proportional control of the 
yaw rate sent to the on-board low level controllers (Fig. 5).  
Only a proportional gain is required as the low level controller 
sets a heading point from the given yaw rate.  
Tuning of this controller was performed indoors, with a 
static target and the Ziegler-Nichols [13] oscillation method. 
Further fine tuning resulted in a higher gain than that found by 
this method, providing a faster response to disturbances caused 
by the coupled dynamics of the quadcopter.  
2) Position controller 
The position controller maintains the UAV’s position in 
the 𝑥𝑒, 𝑦𝑒 plane of the Earth frame. The use of an optical flow 
sensor allows for the UAV’s velocity and position to be 
accurately measured. These measured values can then be used 
to control the position of the UAV through a nested control 
loop design. This entails controlling the roll and pitch of the 
UAV depending on both the distance error and the current 
velocity. 
The controller, shown in Fig. 6, controls the UAV’s 
velocity to move its position asymptotically towards the 
desired position. Commands are passed directly to the low 
level controllers (contained within the UAV plant) that 
command the individual motors to achieve the set roll and 
pitch angles. 
The proportional-integral controller acts to change the 
UAV’s velocity to match that of the desired velocity. The 
proportional gain acts to allow for the UAV to quickly respond 
to changes in the desired velocity, while the integral gain acts 
to eliminate steady state errors that are caused by system 
uncertainties and by disturbance inputs such as wind. 
A maximum rate change limit of ±20 degrees per second 
is applied to the roll and pitch angle commands, enforcing 
smooth motion of the UAV. This was added as larger

To tune these controllers a moving set point was used,

simultaneously. First, the gains were increased until motion

was fixed and the integral gain tuned for fast elimination of the 
steady state error. This error occurs when switching into the 
position controlled flight mode, due to small amounts of 
positional drift. 
3) Object path following algorithm  
By extension of the position controller, object path 
following can be achieved. This is performed through the 
storing of positions that the tracked object has visited, and 
using these positions to move the UAV along the tracked 
object’s path. Position targets are determined by the path 
following algorithm and fed directly into the positional 
controller. The path following algorithm is split into a 
waypoint storage and a position target algorithm. 
The 2D Earth frame position of the tracked object is stored 
as waypoints in a circular queue, as shown in Fig. 7. This 
circular queue is updated with a new position at 10Hz to allow 
for smooth motion of the UAV between points rather than 
large jumps in the positional targets. The size of the circular 
queue was chosen to store only 50 positions, which is 5 
seconds of positional data. 
The initial target position is set to the UAV’s own position 
when the path following algorithm is started. This forces the 
UAV to maintain its own position until the tracked object has 
moved from its starting point. The craft will only travel to 
waypoints that are at least 3m from the target and will start 
travelling to the next waypoint once within 50cm of the current 
waypoint. After the circular queue is full (5 seconds without 
reaching current waypoint) the waypoint will be skipped. The 
capacity of the circular queue was chosen to reduce the chance 
of the target exceeding the detectable range. A more robust 
method of remaining in range of target will need to be 
investigated in future work.  
 
V. RESULTS 
A. Yaw tracking controller 
Once tuned the yaw controller was able to achieve zero 
steady state error, and a maximum error of 1.5 degrees, whilst 
indoors. Further testing of the yaw controller was completed 
outdoors. With the added disturbances of moderate wind (up 
to 28km/hr, from surface wind reports) and variable lighting 
conditions, the controller was capable of maintaining heading 
to within ±6 degrees with a standard deviation of 2.2 degrees. 
Although object yaw tracking performance was poorer than 
that achievable indoors, it was sufficient to ensure the object 
remains in the FOV. As such, further tuning of the yaw 
controller was not undertaken. Also of note is the potential 
decrease in performance of the position and height controllers 
(due to coupling of axes) if the response time of the yaw 
controller was improved. 
B. Localization 
The results from indoor testing were not quantified due to 
lighting and surface pattern issues. The localization of the 
UAV was tested outdoors to check the accuracy of the dead-
reckoning. This was completed by walking the UAV between 
measured points; 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5m apart 10 times each. Each 
trial was completed over a one minute period with an average

D. Path following algorithm  
When testing the path following, the UAV was able to 
closely track the object’s path with a maximum error of 78 cm 
and an rms error of 28 cm. This performance was measured 
using a similar method to that of the position controller. A 
video frame of the test can be seen in Fig. 10, with the target 
and UAV’s paths are marked in green and blue respectively, 
and distance to path is plotted in Fig. 11. The point of 
maximum error could be attributed to two different causes: the 
high wind during this point in the trial, and/or the shaded area 
(in the upper left of the frame) in which the object travelled 
through, possibly resulting in an incorrect range estimation.  
 
VI. CONCLUSIONS 
 
Accurate on-board localization of the UAV has been 
obtained through the use of an optical flow sensor and an IMU. 
The Pixy vision system has been characterized to enable object 
position and distance to be measured in a range of lighting 
conditions. A positional controller was developed and tested 
which can hold the UAV to within 40 cm of its initial position, 
in variable wind and lighting conditions without the use of 
GPS. A control algorithm has been developed which can 
effectively follow an object’s path utilizing the Pixy vision 
system and the positional controller. During a single outdoor 
test in light winds, the rms horizontal tracking error was 
recorded to be 28 cm. Future work will include more rigorous 
testing of the path following algorithm, investigating the 
effects of object speed and radius of curvature of the path. An 
alternative ground truth measurement based on real time 
kinematic (RTK) GPS, allowing for cm-level accuracy may be 
used. This will also provide data at a much higher frequency, 
enabling relevant dynamics for tuning to be captured.
