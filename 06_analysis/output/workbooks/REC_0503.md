Decimeter-Accuracy Positioning for Drones
Using Two-Stage Trilateration in a
GPS-Denied Environment
Yu-Er Chen, Student Member, IEEE, Huan-Huan Liew
, Ju-Chin Chao, and Ruey-Beei Wu
, Fellow, IEEE
Abstract—This study proposes a high-accuracy two-stage tri-
lateration method that enables real-time positioning in global
position system (GPS)-denied areas, such as unmanned aerial
vehicles (UAVs) under bridges and targets in vegetation or
indoors, where automated drone operation is difﬁcult, and there
are few localization methods. This method uses ultrawideband
(UWB) with high-ranging accuracy and a high transmission
rate as the positioning sensor. The drone and the target are
equipped with multiple UWBs, and the relative distance is calcu-
lated through the signal transmission between the UWB devices
so as to determine the position of the target in a ﬁxed coordinate
system. In practice, this method uses the UWB anchors on the
ground to locate the UAV moving in the air, and the altitude
error can be reduced from 8–10 m to less than 1 m. The moving
UAV can also be used as the anchor to locate ﬁxed objects on
the ground in areas with poor GPS, and the relative position
error is reduced from 2.02 to 0.35 m, and the relative altitude
error is reduced from 3.39 to 0.32 m. This study alleviates the
insufﬁcient accuracy of the trilateration method when the UWB
anchors are in the coplanar space, so as to improve the reliability
of the positioning and navigation of the UAV in the GPS-denied
area.
Index Terms—Drones, global position system (GPS)-denied
environment, trilateration, ultrawideband (UWB), wireless posi-
tioning.
I. INTRODUCTION
D
RONES, or unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), are air-
craft without a human pilot that can ﬂy autonomously or
under the guidance of a remote control. Until the early 2000s,
drones were complex systems with few nonmilitary applica-
tions. Modern advances in hardware and software technology
allow the development of smaller, easier-to-control, and lower-
cost systems, and their use is expected to increase in the
near future [1]. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA)
projects that by 2022, the number of drones registered in its
database will be 3.8 million units [2].

Drones are used in various ﬁelds due to their high mobil-
ity and ﬂexibility [3]. A key technology for drone systems
is the detection and high-accuracy localization of stationary
or moving targets [4]. These vehicles are used for real-time
monitoring of road trafﬁc, wireless coverage, remote sens-
ing, search and rescue operations, delivery of goods, security
and surveillance, precision agriculture, and civil infrastructure
inspection [5].
Most drones rely on the onboard inertia measurement unit
(IMU) for navigation, but its accuracy is susceptible to cumula-
tive errors caused by measurement drift. Traditionally, global
position system (GPS) measurements can be fused with the
IMU to compensate for the accumulated error, and if high
precision is required, the real-time kinematic (RTK) can also
be used. But GPS signals are not available everywhere, and in
hostile areas, buildings, and bodies of water, they can degrade
or become completely unavailable. In areas with no GPS cov-
erage, RTK positioning is not possible, so the position cannot
be sent accurately.
In such a GPS-denied environment, positioning requires a
secondary sensor modality to estimate the drone’s position and
its linear velocity. Vision-based systems are a natural solution
to this challenge and most studies follow the simultaneous
localization and mapping (SLAM) and visual odometry (VO)
techniques [6]. Based on lasers, lidars, RGB and RGB-D
cameras, and stereovision, these methods typically use an
unscented Kalman ﬁlter [7], extended Kalman ﬁlter (EKF),
Gaussian particle ﬁlter [8], sliding window observer [9], or
image processing [10]. An example of these algorithms for
indoor teleoperation is using direct VO using an RGB-D sen-
sor [11]. A summary of some current vision-, laser-, and
LiDAR-based techniques for localization in GPS-denied envi-
ronments is provided in [12]. An example of the deployment of
drones is the exploration of vision- and marker-based localiza-
tion methods in a mobile post-earthquake monitoring system,
where a downward-facing LiDAR and a gimbal-mounted
camera are integrated with an octo-rotor platform [13].
Recently, extensive research has been undergone to improve
positioning accuracy with more advanced machine learning
methods. White et al. [14] proposed a convolutional neural
network (CNN) approach to recover the true ﬂight trajectory
from the entire synthetic aperture radar (SAR) image data. In
addition, a positioning method for a pair of tethered vehicles
(i.e., ﬁeld robot and multirotor) can be developed to achieve
an accuracy of around 0.22 m, using the catenary shape of the

types of cracks in bridges. Furthermore, the techniques are
computationally challenging, and for most aerial drones with
limited payload capability, their functionality will be severely
compromised.
There are other RF signal-based positioning schemes suit-
able for GPS-denied areas, such as proximity positioning using
Bluetooth low energy (BLE) or ﬁngerprinting using WiFi [19].
BLE and WiFi rely on a narrow spectrum spanning typically
20 MHz or less per channel in the ISM band, are susceptive
to complex dynamic environments, and cause large position-
ing uncertainties through tri-tessellation. In practice, higher
positioning accuracy can be achieved using proximity detec-
tion, but there is a tradeoff in deploying a huge number of
BLE devices. For WiFi ﬁngerprinting, the received signal
strength (RSS) ﬁngerprints of the scene need to be collected
ﬁrst, and the model is built through the ofﬂine training phase.
Previously, vast RSS measurements were required in a large
range, but the positioning accuracy was generally 3–5 m.
This study uses ultrawideband (UWB), covering 500 MHz
per channel for measurements, which can provide much supe-
rior performance in positioning accuracy. The UWB capable
of decimeter accuracy is good enough to perform indepen-
dently without the fusion with IMU in the proposed method
of this article. For example, Ho et al. [20] proposed a UWB
indoor positioning system for the customer pathway tracking.
In range-based positioning systems, where UWB anchors are
nearly coplanar, the altitude difference between the tag and the
reference plane can cause signiﬁcant errors for 2-D and 3-D
positioning. A two-stage estimator using altitude information
from an additional sensor, data such as barometric sensors or
altitude estimates can alleviate this problem [21]. The study
used a method that allows drones to locate targets using a
two-stage approach without the need for visual information or
other sensor data.

II. THEORY
A. Statement of Problem
Trilateration involves measuring the distances between the
device under test (DUT) and four or more anchors of trans-
mitters. Consider four UWB modules mounted on four tripods
in a square, as shown in Fig. 1. The four UWB modules are
D0, D1, D2, and D3, respectively, disij is the measured distance
between Di and Dj., and disij is the ground truth. Anchors are
used to locate the position of a moving drone including alti-
tude. The altitude difference can cause signiﬁcant errors in
3-D positioning [21].
Trilateration is a classical problem with available solutions,
e.g., [22] and [23]. In previous work, most papers applied the
least-squares solution with cosine law to ﬁnd the initial guess
and iteratively used the Taylor algorithm [23] to solve nonlin-
ear equations. However, the iteration in the Taylor algorithm
might consume a lot of computational time. In cases where the
altitude range covering the anchors is much smaller than the
longitudinal coverage, conventional trilateration cannot accu-
rately determine the altitude [22]. The two-stage trilateration
proposed in this study can be performed without resorting to
iteration and can address the above shortcomings.

V. CONCLUSION AND DISCUSSION
This study proposes a 3-D positioning technique using
UWB modules, which can be used for the tracking and control
of drones in GPS-denied areas. Two application scenarios are
proposed and demonstrated. In scenario one, four UWB mod-
ules placed on the ground are used to calculate the position of
a moving drone in an environment without GPS coverage. In
scenario two, a single UWB on a moving drone is used to cal-
culate the positions of stationary tags on the ground. This study
utilizes the distances measured by UWB modules and adopts a
novel two-stage trilateration method to accurately calculate the
longitudinal coordinates and altitude of the DUT. Numerical
analysis shows that this method can achieve decimeter-level
accuracy of 3-D positioning of drones.
The proposed method does not require any GPS coor-
dinates to obtain accurate relative positions between UWB
