A B S T R A C T
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) localization in Global Navigation Satellite Systems-denied environments 
faces significant challenges due to sensor limitations, environmental factors, and the absence of absolute 
positioning references. Visual-based methods degrade under sparse textures and illumination changes, while 
traditional sensor fusion approaches suffer from cumulative drift and poor adaptability to dynamic conditions. 
We propose the Two-Stage Residual Extended Kalman Filter (TSR-EKF), a hybrid framework that incorporates 
Machine Learning (ML)-based residual corrections into a probabilistic state estimator. The core novelty lies in 
a clear two-stage residual learning strategy: the approach employs eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) for 
coarse bias removal from terrain-induced and inertial errors, followed by Dense Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks 
(DenseKAN) for fine nonlinear residual refinement. These corrections are fused with Inertial Measurement Unit 
velocities and terrain-referenced measurements within an Extended Kalman Filter framework without altering 
its standard probabilistic structure. We conducted experimental validation using seven UAV flight trajectories 
over the Taif region, Saudi Arabia. These tests were performed in high-fidelity Gazebo simulations using 
actual terrain Digital Elevation Models (DEM). The results demonstrate clear and measurable performance 
gains. The TSR-EKF achieves root-mean-square error (RMSE) reductions of 57.4% to 98.9% compared to 
traditional EKF methods while maintaining sub-6m positioning accuracy and real-time computational feasibility 
with processing times under 2.1 ms per update. The framework exhibits exceptional robustness to sparse 
measurement conditions, making it suitable for mission-critical UAV operations in contested environments. 
Overall, these results highlight TSR-EKF as a practical and effective solution that combines Kalman filtering 
reliability with ML adaptability for robust autonomous navigation.
1. Introduction
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) have rapidly evolved from ex-
perimental platforms to indispensable tools in mission-critical opera-
tions, including military reconnaissance, border surveillance, disaster 
response, and search-and-rescue efforts (Othman et al., 2025; Tanveer 
et al., 2022). In these applications, accurate geolocalization is not 
merely a technical feature but a mission-enabling capability for auton-
omy, coordination, and operational success. Particularly in defense and 
security scenarios, high-precision localization under degraded or de-
nied Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS) is crucial for sustained 
intelligence gathering, high-value target tracking, and secure airspace 
navigation. Recent global conflicts have revealed critical vulnerabilities

A foundational methodology in UAV geolocation is the family of 
Kalman filtering algorithms, which provide a principled probabilistic 
framework for recursive state estimation. While the classical Kalman 
Filter (KF) is optimal for linear Gaussian systems, UAV applications 
often involve nonlinear dynamics and non-Gaussian noise. To address 
these complexities, the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) performs lin-
earization via Jacobians, supporting real-time onboard fusion of in-
ertial, visual, barometric, LiDAR, and range data. In more nonlinear 
or uncertain regimes, the Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF) and Particle 
Filter (PF) provide improved performance via sigma-point propaga-
tion or probabilistic sampling. These Kalman-based estimators under-
pin various localization strategies, including visual-inertial odometry, 
terrain-referenced matching with Digital Elevation Models (DEMs), and 
LiDAR-inertial fusion. Their recursive formulation, uncertainty propa-
gation, and compatibility with multi-sensor fusion make them essential 
components in both absolute and relative localization systems (Raković 
et al., 2020; Wan et al., 2022).
Localization techniques are generally categorized as absolute or 
relative. Absolute approaches, such as Terrain Referenced Naviga-
tion (TRN), Terrain Contour Matching (TERCOM), and Sequential 
Terrain Aided Navigation (SITAN), align onboard altimetric or LiDAR-
derived profiles with DEMs, digital surface models (DSMs), or digital 
terrain models (DTMs). Digital Scene Matching Area Correlation (DS-
MAC) improves localization by correlating onboard imagery with high-
resolution satellite maps, though it remains sensitive to low-texture 
regions (Carr and Sobek, 1980; Turek et al., 2021; Borra, 2025).
Relative localization methods estimate incremental motion from on-
board sensors. Techniques such as Visual-Inertial Odometry (VIO) and 
Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) combine data from 
cameras, inertial measurement units (IMUs), and LiDAR to estimate 
UAV trajectories in a local frame (Wan et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2022). 
Kalman filter variants (EKF, UKF) and optimization- or sampling-based 
approaches are commonly used to mitigate drift. Despite advances, 
these systems still face cumulative errors during long-duration flights, 
particularly without loop closures or periodic absolute corrections.
Modern trends emphasize hybrid frameworks and learning-
augmented techniques. Hybrid systems integrate absolute and relative 
cues within factor graphs or Kalman filter formulations and increas-
ingly incorporate opportunistic signals such as ultra-wideband (UWB), 
cellular towers, Wi-Fi signals, or geomagnetic anomalies. In parallel, 
learning-based methods embed Machine Learning (ML) modules into 
the estimation pipeline. Residual learning architectures introduce adap-
tive corrections to compensate for bias and nonlinear residuals that 
classical models miss.
A notable real-world demonstration is the DARPA Subterranean 
Challenge, organized by the Defense Advanced Research Projects
Agency. In this competition, Team CERBERUS deployed a heteroge-
neous fleet of UAVs and legged robots equipped with visual–inertial 
odometry, LiDAR-based three-dimensional mapping, and photorealistic 
feedback (Tranzatto et al., 2022). These robots used an iterated EKF 
to fuse multimodal pose estimates and achieved robust navigation in 
GNSS-denied underground environments (Kottege et al., 2024).
Visual localization presents additional promise and challenges. Ab-
solute visual localization aligns onboard images with geo-referenced 
maps, satellite views, or 3D models. Datasets like AnyLoc and
AnyViewLoc report errors below 5 m using transformer-based encoders 
and cross-view matching networks (Keetha et al., 2023; He et al., 2023). 
However, performance degrades under lighting variation, occlusion, 
and seasonal changes, and the process often requires expensive feature 
extraction. Relative visual methods, including Visual Odometry (VO) 
and SLAM, offer robust short-term tracking but accumulate drift over 
time. To reduce this, visual–inertial SLAM systems often embed EKF or 
UKF within the frontend to fuse IMU data with visual keypoints in real 
time (Tabassum et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2025; He et al., 2024).
Despite progress, environmental diversity and real-time constraints 
remain significant obstacles. Recent methods incorporate learned fea-
tures, semantic segmentation, and adaptive filtering to enhance robust-
ness in complex scenes. Hybrid systems now combine visual data with 
terrain-referenced localization, inertial measurements, and alternative 
signals to reduce drift while maintaining global consistency (Elhassan 
et al., 2024; Chen and Jiang, 2025).
Furthermore, ML-enhanced estimators have emerged as a com-
pelling alternative. Deep neural networks, recurrent models such as 
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), and 
transformer encoders have been applied to both absolute and relative 
localization tasks. In visual systems, learned models either regress 
global coordinates from images or perform robust feature matching. In 
relative pipelines, learning-based odometry methods integrate attention 
modules and robust descriptors to improve performance in texture-
poor or dynamic settings (Lin and Lai, 2025; Natarajan et al., 2025). 
Recent research has emphasized sensor fusion strategies that embed 
Artificial Intelligence (AI) into recursive filters. For example, DeepUKF-
VIN introduces a neural adaptation module into the UKF, using camera 
and IMU data to perform online tuning of process and measurement co-
variance matrices. This results in improved accuracy in GNSS-degraded 
settings (Ghanizadegan and Hashim, 2025).
Collectively, these advances mark a pivotal transition toward intel-
ligent and adaptive localization. By integrating data-driven estimators 
within classical filters such as EKF, UKF, and PF, modern hybrid archi-
tectures provide resilience against sensor drift, enable self-tuning under 
uncertainty, and ensure robust operation in contested or degraded 
environments (Oda et al., 2025).
However, existing GNSS-denied localization methods concentrate 
on three families with distinct limitations. Vision-centric VO/SLAM and 
image-retrieval terrain-aiding can be accurate at low altitudes, but they 
degrade with sparse texture, weak parallax, illumination changes, and 
the need for dense, up-to-date map imagery (Minervini et al., 2025). 
LiDAR-centric terrain-aided navigation achieves strong absolute fixes 
where relief is favorable, yet it often requires heavy computing and 
high-fidelity maps, losing accuracy over flat terrain. Finally, end-to-
end learned filters improve short-term accuracy but frequently modify 
the Bayesian recursion, add latency, and reduce interpretability for

2. Related works
Recent research on UAV geolocalization in GNSS-denied environ-
ments has advanced significantly, driven by growing demands for au-
tonomy, resilience, and precision in contested or degraded operational 
settings. A wide range of methodologies has been explored, includ-
ing terrain-referenced localization, ML-enhanced estimators, recursive 
filter-based sensor fusion, and non-visual navigation techniques. Many 
of these approaches aim to improve robustness and real-time perfor-
mance by integrating data from heterogeneous sources such as LiDAR, 
IMUs, barometers, cameras, and DEMs. Increasingly, traditional estima-
tors like the EKF and UKF are being augmented with AI components, 
enabling more accurate state estimation in the presence of nonlinearity, 
drift, and sensor noise. These advances underscore a broader trend to-
ward combining physically grounded models with data-driven residual 
learning to enhance localization accuracy in environments where GNSS 
signals are unavailable or unreliable.
Terrain-Aided Navigation (TAN) remains a foundational approach 
for absolute localization when GNSS is unavailable. Classical TAN 
methods such as TERCOM and SITAN use elevation profiles or con-
tour maps to align onboard sensor measurements with preloaded ter-
rain models (Vaman, 2012). Lili Wang et al. (2025) demonstrated 
centimeter-level precision by fusing LiDAR and IMU data within a 
hybrid framework, albeit with high calibration and computational re-
quirements. To address resource limitations, Michaelson et al. (2024) 
proposed neuro-inspired terrain encoding, achieving ∼2.5 m (3𝜎) posi-
tion accuracy with reduced memory overhead. However, the success 
of TAN remains tightly coupled with the availability and fidelity of 
terrain data, limiting its performance in unstructured or low-resolution 
environments.
Learning-augmented methods have emerged as powerful alterna-
tives or complements to traditional model-based filters. El Sabbagh 
et al. (2023) introduced a cascaded neural architecture combining Non-
linear Auto Regressive model with eXogenous inputs (NARX) network 
and Feed-Forward Networks (FFN), achieving a 98% improvement in 
velocity and position estimation during GNSS outages. Taghizadeh and 
Safabakhsh (2023) developed an attention-based hierarchical LSTM 
(AHLSTM) to improve long-horizon predictions. However, such models 
require substantial computational resources and remain difficult to 
deploy in real time on embedded-class hardware. Wan et al. (2022) 
enhanced UAV localization by integrating frequency-domain terrain im-
age matching into a SLAM framework, achieving an average 0.45 m er-
ror over a 33.8 km flight path. Deep learning has also enabled improved 
visual-semantic perception, as in the work of Ouyang et al. (2024), 
where convolutional architectures enhanced UAV scene understanding 
for autonomous decision-making.
Recently, Yang et al. (2025) introduced a DINOv2-based visual 
localization method tailored for low-altitude urban UAVs. Their model 
leverages global image-to-map retrieval using pretrained DINOv2 em-
beddings and refines estimates via dense feature matching against 
satellite views. Unlike traditional place recognition techniques, this 
method handles severe appearance variations, low-altitude perspec-
tives, and urban clutter, achieving a mean absolute error below 5 m. 
However, it depends on dense aerial image coverage and high-quality 
visual databases, which can limit deployment in less-mapped or cloudy 
regions. Moreover, the use of transformer-based architectures may 
hinder their application on lightweight UAVs due to onboard processing 
constraints.
Hybrid sensor–fusion frameworks, often centered on Kalman-filter 
variants, have proven effective in GNSS-denied environments. The EKF, 
UKF, and PF remain standard choices for recursive fusion, each offering 
trade-offs in non-linearity handling and computational cost (Kadri, 
2025). Gallo and Barrientos (2022) incorporated barometric data into 
inertial fusion to reduce vertical drift, whereas Xiaoyu et al. (2024) 
fused LSTM networks with a right-invariant EKF (RIEKF) to achieve 
drift-free localization for fixed-wing UAVs. DeepUKF-VIN (Ghanizade-
gan and Hashim, 2025) embedded a neural covariance tuner inside the 
UKF for Visual–Inertial Navigation (VIN), underscoring a recent trend 
toward coupling AI-driven corrections with probabilistic filters.
Wang and Zhang (2024) advanced this trend with a multi-step 
pseudo-measurement Kalman filter (MPKF) that continuously evalu-
ates Inertial Navigation System (INS)/GNSS filter performance. The 
MPKF monitors the statistical distance between innovation-based and 
signal-agnostic noise estimates. When this distance exceeds a tun-
able threshold, the filter discards corrupted GNSS updates and in-
jects pseudo-measurements derived from previously reliable INS states. 
Tested with a tactical-grade NovAtel ISA-100C IMU and dual-frequency 
GNSS on urban roads, the method cut 3D position, velocity, and atti-
tude RMSE by up to 48%, 50%, and 38%, respectively, relative to a 
tightly coupled EKF. However, its efficacy declines under heavy-tailed 
multipath, and it depends on (i) unbiased historical estimates and (ii) 
environment-specific threshold tuning.
Non-visual localization methods have also gained prominence, es-
pecially in visually ambiguous or low-light environments. Ahmed et al. 
(2024) proposed a radar-inertial-barometric fusion system using Fre-
quency Modulated Continuous Wave (FMCW) radar to derive forward 
velocity and fusing it via both EKF and PF algorithms, achieving up 
to 99.8% error reduction in controlled tests. Bodi et al. (2023) lever-
aged reinforcement learning with LiDAR and Deep Deterministic Policy 
Gradient (DDPG) control policies for multi-UAV formation in GNSS-
denied areas, maintaining sub-meter formation accuracy. However, 
these methods are constrained by sensor-specific limitations—radar

While SLAM and camera-based localization techniques have demon-
strated considerable success in structured indoor spaces and low-
altitude outdoor flights, their efficacy substantially declines in high-
altitude scenarios. As UAVs operate at higher altitudes, the spatial 
resolution of visual features captured by onboard monocular or stereo 
cameras decreases due to the increasing distance between the sensor 
and the ground. This reduction in spatial resolution significantly limits 
the number of distinctive and trackable keypoints required for reliable 
feature-based visual odometry and loop closure, which are fundamental 
components of SLAM systems. Additionally, high-altitude perspectives 
often introduce uniform, repetitive terrain textures, such as those 
found over agricultural fields, deserts, or water surfaces, which lead 
to perceptual aliasing and failures in image correspondence.
Environmental conditions at higher altitudes, such as haze, vari-
ation in illumination, and atmospheric scattering, can substantially 
degrade aerial images and increase uncertainty in pose estimation. 
Visual-inertial navigation systems attempt to mitigate these degrada-
tions by fusing camera measurements with inertial data, but their 
performance remains highly dependent on precise calibration and tight 
synchronization. Even small timing misalignments between the visual 
and inertial data streams may accumulate over long flight durations and 
lead to substantial drift, as shown by recent GNSS-denied visual-inertial 
methods (Gallo and Barrientos, 2023). 
Global visual localization techniques that rely on image-to-map 
correspondence, such as drone-to-satellite matching or cross-view geo-
localization, face further difficulties when operating at high altitudes 
or under different imaging conditions. Scale ambiguity, viewpoint in-
consistency, and appearance differences between UAV imagery and 
reference satellite maps significantly complicate reliable matching, es-
pecially when satellite imagery is outdated or does not reflect current 
scene semantics (Yao et al., 2024). 
These observations confirm that purely vision-centric localization 
becomes increasingly fragile at higher altitudes and under varying en-
vironmental conditions. As a result, alternative sensing modalities, such 
as LiDAR, barometric altimetry, radar, and terrain-based priors, gain 
importance. A recent survey of GNSS-denied UAV navigation confirms 
that no single modality can satisfy all operational requirements in 
all environments, thus recommending hybrid sensor fusion strategies 
that combine complementary sensors and algorithms to ensure robust 
localization in diverse conditions (Jarraya et al., 2025b).
Existing GNSS-denied localization methods concentrate on three 
families. Vision-centric VO/SLAM and image-retrieval terrain-aiding 
can be accurate at low altitude, but they degrade in the presence of 
sparse texture, weak parallax, illumination changes, and the need for 
dense, up-to-date map imagery. LiDAR-centric terrain-aided navigation 
achieves strong absolute fixes where relief and calibration are favor-
able, yet it often requires heavy computing and high-fidelity maps and 
can lose accuracy over flat terrain. End-to-end learned filters improve 
short-term accuracy but frequently modify the Bayesian recursion, add 
latency, and reduce interpretability for real-time embedded use.
In response, we propose the TSR-EKF, a non-visual, DEM-aided 
framework that keeps the standard EKF propagation intact and treats 
a machine-learned position as an external measurement. The key idea 
is to form an elevation proxy from barometric altitude minus LiDAR 
ground range, align it to a pre-mission DEM, and let learning correct 
only the residual error in that proxy. An XGBoost first removes low-
frequency, context-dependent bias using planar translations, simulated 
elevation, and IMU velocities. A DenseKAN then models the remaining 
nonlinear residual; outputs are standardized and clipped (±3𝜎) before 
fusion to preserve filter consistency.
This design yields three practical advantages over prior work: (i) 
absolute cues without reliance on imagery, dense LiDAR maps, or 
transformer-scale perception; (ii) modular integration that preserves 
EKF structure, uncertainty propagation, and real-time performance on 
embedded CPUs; and (iii) robustness at high altitude and in texture-
poor scenes where keypoint matching, visual odometry, and loop clo-
sure are unreliable (see Table 1).
3. Proposed TSR-EKF framework
UAVs operating in GNSS-denied environments employ onboard 
sensing and prior terrain knowledge to achieve accurate pose estima-
tion. Fig. 1 illustrates the complete TSR-EKF framework architecture. 
The diagram shows the integration of sensor inputs, including an 
IMU, barometer, LiDAR, and DEM. These inputs feed into a two-stage 
residual correction process using XGBoost and DenseKAN, resulting 
in a final refined position output. During the training and evaluation 
phases, a pre-aligned DEM provides elevation labels corresponding to 
the measured geographic coordinates. The localization task is formu-
lated as a probabilistic state estimation problem, in which an EKF 
integrates multi-sensor data, augmented by sequential, data-driven 
residual learning to enhance accuracy.
In this context, the TSR-EKF is proposed as a hybrid localization 
framework optimized for real-time UAV navigation in GNSS-denied 
environments. The framework employs a sequential refinement strategy 
that incrementally reduces estimation errors by integrating model-
based filtering with data-driven residual learning. Specifically, the TSR-
EKF architecture augments a baseline EKF with a two-stage residual-
correction module that addresses both coarse and fine measurement 
biases prior to assimilation. This hybrid design enhances localization 
robustness under conditions of significant drift and sensor degradation. 
The system consists of three main pipelines:
1. Baseline estimation (EKF): The Extended Kalman Filter fuses 
IMU-derived velocity data with terrain-relative altitude mea-
surements to perform dead-reckoning, maintaining a continuous 
state estimate of position, velocity, and terrain elevation.
2. Coarse correction (XGBoost): The gradient-boosted decision 
tree model, trained on multimodal sensor and terrain features, 
compensates for large-scale residual biases uncorrected by the 
EKF.
3. Fine correction (DenseKAN): The Dense Kolmogorov–Arnold 
Network models high-frequency, nonlinear residuals beyond the 
coarse correction stage, enabling precise fine-grained adjust-
ments.
By applying these corrections in sequence prior to EKF measurement 
assimilation, the TSR-EKF stabilizes the update process, reduces inno-
vation magnitudes, and enhances overall state estimation accuracy in 
challenging GNSS-denied conditions.

3.2. Extended Kalman Filter (EKF)
In the proposed TSR–EKF, UAV localization is formulated as a 
probabilistic state-estimation problem in which the system evolves 
according to a constant-velocity motion model and is observed through 
a measurement model derived from onboard sensors. The EKF provides 
two essential functions: (1) model-based state prediction with Gaussian 
uncertainty propagation, and (2) correction of this prediction using 
new sensor data. To enhance robustness in GNSS-denied conditions, the 
standard EKF is augmented with learned residual-based corrections that 
generate bias-reduced pseudo-measurements prior to assimilation.

3.3. XGBoost-based coarse position estimation in TSR–EKF
XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting) is a powerful ensemble learn-
ing algorithm based on gradient-boosted decision trees. It builds an 
additive model by sequentially training weak learners (decision trees) 
that correct the errors of previous ones, effectively reducing bias and 
variance to yield highly accurate results. Compared to standard boost-
ing, XGBoost introduces regularization and system optimizations (such 
as sparsity-aware tree splitting and cache-friendly block structures) that 
improve generalization and computation speed. This makes it well-
suited for real-time applications. In particular, XGBoost can handle 
heterogeneous input features and capture complex nonlinear relation-
ships while maintaining low inference latency (Hakkal and Ait Lahcen, 
2024; Liu et al., 2021). These characteristics are crucial for UAV 
navigation, where data come from multiple sensors and the mapping 
from sensor readings to position errors may be highly nonlinear.
In the proposed TSR–EKF, XGBoost serves as the first-stage residual 
learner, producing a coarse, bias-reduced position estimate before the 
DenseKAN stage fine-scale correction. XGBoost is selected for its ability 
to model complex nonlinear dependencies across heterogeneous UAV 
sensor channels, its resilience to noisy measurements, and its efficient 
inference. In the TSR–EKF pipeline, the XGBoost stage operates in the 
following four steps:
• Step 1 – Feature Vector Transformation: Transform the raw 
EKF measurement vector through a deterministic mapping into a 
normalized feature set that preserves essential sensor information 
while eliminating redundant or collinear inputs. This preprocess-
ing yields a compact and physically consistent representation, 
improving numerical stability and facilitating efficient learning.
• Step 2 – Model Training: Train a multi-output gradient boosting 
regression model using historical UAV flight data to capture the 
relationship between the constructed feature set and the target 
position residuals.
• Step 3 – Residual Bias Prediction: Use the trained XGBoost 
model to predict the coarse residual bias between the EKF prior 
position estimate and the true position, providing a first-level 
correction to the raw state estimate.
• Step 4 – EKF Integration: Integrate the bias-compensated posi-
tion into the EKF pseudo-measurement vector, thereby reducing 
innovation magnitude and improving stability and drift-resilience 
in GNSS-denied environments.

3.4. DenseKAN fine residual estimation in TSR–EKF
KAN models are neural architectures inspired by the Kolmogorov–
Arnold representation theorem, which states that any multivariate 
continuous function can be decomposed into a finite sum of uni-
variate continuous functions applied to linear combinations of the 
inputs (Allogmani, 2025). As emphasized by Longlong Li et al. (2025), 
KANs constitute an effective alternative to traditional multilayer per-
ceptrons (MLPs) because they replace fixed scalar weights and node 
activations with learnable univariate basis functions on edges. This 
edge-based functional parametrization enables more flexible and inter-
pretable modeling of complex nonlinear behaviors. Allogmani (2025) 
further show that the spline-parameterized transformations in KANs of-
fer smoother, more expressive, and data-adaptive mappings than stan-
dard MLP activations, allowing KAN architectures to capture nonlinear 
structures and fine-scale variations that MLPs often fail to represent.
DenseKAN is the dense-layer formulation of the Kolmogorov–Arnold 
Network (Jayakumar et al., 2025). It preserves the topology of a 
conventional fully connected layer while replacing each scalar weight 
with a compact B-spline-based univariate function. This functional 
parametrization enhances the model’s ability to adapt to local ge-
ometric variations and high-frequency residual patterns. Although a 
standard MLP could be used for residual prediction, DenseKAN provides 
clear advantages within the TSR–EKF framework.
The second stage of the TSR–EKF refines the coarse position es-
timate produced by XGBoost by learning high-frequency, nonlinear 
residuals that tree ensembles cannot fully capture. DenseKAN is em-
ployed in this stage to generate data-adaptive corrections that integrate 
smoothly into the EKF update process while preserving the stability 
and interpretability required for state estimation. The following steps 
outline the DenseKAN stage:
• Step 1 – Network Input and Feature Construction: Concatenate 
the 6D feature vector with the coarse–corrected position from 
the XGBoost stage, apply scaling, and prepare it for DenseKAN 
processing.
• Step 2 – DenseKAN Layer Formulation: Replace standard dense 
layer weights with learnable univariate functions expressed
through B-splines, SiLU activations, and affine terms.
• Step 3 – Fine Residual Prediction: Train the DenseKAN to pre-
dict standardized fine-scale residuals between the coarse position 
and the ground-truth position.
• Step 4 – Refined Position Estimate and EKF Integration: De-
standardize the predicted residuals, apply robust clipping and 
gating, and integrate the refined position into the EKF pseudo-
measurement vector.

We evaluate the proposed TSR-EKF, which fuses a coarse position

dual-stage residual learning, inference times remain well within real-

2.05 ms for the complete TSR-EKF pipeline, corresponding to real-
time viability ratios (V_RT) of 0.0298 to 0.0620. These metrics confirm 
feasibility for deployment on embedded UAV systems operating at 
standard control frequencies (30–100 Hz).
Computational trade-offs and anticipated embedded performance. 
All timing results were obtained in a Google Colab cloud environment 
using an Intel Xeon CPU at 2.20 GHz with 12–26 GB of RAM, Python 
3.10, and TensorFlow 2.15.1. On this CPU-only platform, the EKF-

the full TSR–EKF (XGB+KAN→EKF) requires 0.98–2.05 ms per up-

roughly 0.7–1.8 ms but still use only 2.98–6.20% of a 33.3 ms control 
cycle at 30 Hz. This overhead must be weighed against the substantial 
localization gains, where RMSE is reduced by 57.4–98.9% relative to 
EKF-ONLY across all trajectories.
To gauge feasibility on embedded platforms, we compare these 
figures with the published specifications of NVIDIA Jetson Orin NX 
and AGX Orin modules, which support up to eight CPU cores with 
maximal CPU frequencies of approximately 1.98 GHz (Orin NX 16 GB) 
and 2.19 GHz (AGX Orin 32 GB) under 10–40 W power (NVIDIA Cor-
poration, 2024). Since our TSR–EKF latency is already below 2.1 ms 
per step on a single Xeon core, and both XGBoost and DenseKAN are 
intentionally kept lightweight, we expect comparable per-step runtime 
on Jetson-class SoCs configured in high-performance modes, with the 
option to further reduce latency by pruning tree depth or decreasing 
the number of spline bases at the cost of some accuracy. A complete 
empirical evaluation of specific embedded boards and ultra-low-power 
autopilot hardware is left for future work.

probabilistic model across all trajectories.

4.9. Comparative study
In the third step, we conducted a comparative evaluation to position 
the proposed TSR–EKF against alternative learned-residual strategies 
under challenging GNSS-denied outdoor conditions. Two additional 
EKF-based variants were included for comparison: EKF+MLP, which 
uses a static multilayer perceptron to estimate residual corrections, and
EKF+LSTM, which incorporates a temporal LSTM-based estimator. In 
both models, only the position-correction term is learned while the 
EKF velocity update remains unchanged, ensuring a fair and controlled 
comparison. All experiments were performed on the unified OS-RFODG 
dataset with identical DEM maps, sensor streams, initialization set-
tings, and flight trajectories. Under these consistent conditions, the 
TSR–EKF demonstrates a robust, accurate, and computationally effi-
cient solution for real-time non-visual UAV localization in GNSS-denied 
environments. 
(1) MLP residual model. The MLP residual estimator applies a static 
nonlinear mapping from the EKF feature vector to a 3D correction 
term. It consists of two fully connected layers with ReLU activations 
and a linear output layer. Although computationally efficient, its lim-
ited depth restricts its ability to capture sharp, high-frequency terrain 
discontinuities characteristic of DEM-induced residuals. 
(2) LSTM residual model. The LSTM estimator introduces temporal 
memory by processing features as a sequence (Ziyi Wang et al., 2025). 
The architecture comprises a single LSTM layer with 64 hidden units 
followed by a fully connected output layer. While it models smooth

4.10. Framework limitations and future considerations
Despite the demonstrated performance, the framework is subject to 
several inherent limitations, which outline important avenues for future 
investigation:
Computational constraints. While the TSR-EKF maintains real-time 
feasibility, the dual-stage approach requires approximately 4–7× more 
processing time than EKF baseline methods. A key direction for future 
work is migrating both lightweight residual-learning stages to em-
bedded accelerators (eg, Jetson-class GPUs, edge TPUs) and exploring 
compression methods such as pruning, quantization, and low-rank fac-
torization to ensure deterministic execution during flight. Furthermore, 
profiling-based scheduling and model distillation will be considered to 
guarantee bounded latency.
Training data dependency. The effectiveness of both the XGBoost 
and DenseKAN components depends on representative training data 
that captures the characteristics of the operational environment. Per-
formance may degrade when deployed in terrain significantly dif-
ferent from training conditions, necessitating periodic retraining or 
adaptation. Future work will investigate adaptive residual-learning 
mechanisms capable of online refinement, and domain generalization 
techniques that improve cross-region and cross-terrain robustness.
Sensor requirements. The framework’s terrain-aided approach
requires reliable LiDAR and barometric measurements, making it de-
pendent on sensor availability and environmental conditions that may 
affect these measurements (e.g., heavy precipitation for LiDAR, atmo-
spheric disturbances for barometry). Future work will explore extend-
ing the learned residual pipeline to additional non-visual modalities, 
such as magnetometer.

5. Conclusions
This work presents a novel TSR-EKF framework for UAV local-
ization in GNSS-denied environments. The proposed approach inte-
grates XGBoost for coarse residual correction and KAN for fine residual 
refinement within a conventional EKF, combined with terrain-aided 
navigation using DEMs. Experimental validation across seven diverse 
UAV trajectories demonstrates substantial performance improvements, 
achieving RMSE reductions of 57.4% to 98.9% compared to EKF-ONLY 
approaches while maintaining sub-5.93 m positioning accuracy. The 
framework operates in real time, with processing times under 2.1 ms 
per update, and exhibits exceptional robustness to sparse measurement 
conditions. The key contributions include a dual-stage residual learning 
architecture that effectively decomposes localization errors, successful 
integration of ML within Kalman filtering frameworks, and compre-
hensive experimental validation demonstrating real-time feasibility for 
embedded UAV deployment. The TSR-EKF establishes a new perfor-
mance standard for GNSS-denied UAV localization, enabling mission-
critical operations in contested environments. While TSR-EKF demon-
strates strong performance, its practical deployment is still constrained 
by computational overhead, region-specific training requirements, and 
reliance on reliable LiDAR–barometric sensing. Future work will there-
fore focus on deploying the residual-learning stages on embedded 
accelerators, enhancing cross-region generalization through adaptive 
residual-learning mechanisms, and integrating additional non-visual 
modalities to improve robustness under degraded sensor conditions.
