Abstract
We present a novel approach to use moun-
tain drainage patterns for GPS-denied navigation of small
unmanned aerial systems, such as the ScanEagle, utiliz-
ing a down-looking ﬁxed focus monocular imager. Our
proposal allows for extension of GPS-denied missions in
mountain areas, with no assumption of human-made geo-
graphic objects. We leverage the analogy between mountain
drainage patterns, human aeteriograms, and human ﬁnger-
prints, to match local drainage patterns to parallax occlusion
maps of geo-registered radar returns (GRRR). The GRRR
data may be loaded on-board the aircraft pre-mission to avoid
the need for a scanning aperture radar during the mission.
For recognition purpose, we represent a given mountain area
with a set of spatially distributed mountain minutiae, i.e.,
details found in the drainage patterns, so that conventional
minutiae-based ﬁngerprint matching approaches can be used
to match real-time camera images against template images
in the training set. We use medical arteriography processing
technique to extract the patterns. The minutiae-based repre-
sentationofmountainsisachievedbyﬁrstexposingmountain
ridges and valleys with a series of ﬁlters and then extract-
ing mountain minutiae from these ridges/valleys. Our results
are experimentally validated on actual terrain data and show
the effectiveness of minutiae-based mountain representation
method.

1 Introduction
Unmannedaircraftsystems(UASs)offerfascinatingresearch
areas. An UAS is deﬁned as an aircraft with no on-board
human pilot. The use of UASs have been attracting much
attention among geometrics and geospatial professionals
recently. The applications of such UASs are diverse, ranging
from scientiﬁc exploration and data collection, to provision
of commercial services, military reconnaissance, and intel-
ligence gathering. Other areas include law enforcement, and
even entertainment.
Currently, most UASs are drones which rely on a substan-
tial degree of human remote control. Recent versions have
built-in control and/or guidance systems to perform speed
and/or ﬂight path stabilization and simple prescribed naviga-
tion functions such as way-point following [2]. For an UAS
to replace the human pilot and ﬂy in all kinds of environ-
ments, the current state of the art in UAS autonomy falls
short of the necessary capabilities. Global positioning sys-
tem (GPS) is a critical sensor for UAS navigation due to
its accuracy, global coverage, and small hardware footprint.
However, GPS is subject to interruption or denial due to sig-
nal blockage or RF interference, such as through canyons
or under forest canopy. When GPS is unavailable, position,
velocity and altitude (PVA) performance from other iner-
tial and air data sensors is not sufﬁcient, especially for small
UASs. Therefore, a key issue of UAS design is how to enable
them to operate during GPS denial.

speciﬁed using concepts of hybrid navigation, fusing in real-
time all available navigation data, such as radar altimeters,

2 Vision based navigation
Recently different image-based navigation algorithms have
been developed to address GPS outage for UASs. Since most
UASs already include a camera as standard equipage, vision-
based navigation does not require much additional hardware
or payload. Of course video cameras can also be “put out” by
some adverse weather conditions, such as heavy cloud cover,
but considering the advantages just described, vision-based
navigation is becoming an attractive alternative/supplement
to GPS. In practice a challenge for vision-based navigation
is how to control error accumulation.
2.1 Related work: landmark based approach
An earlier work on landmark based navigation is that of
Rodriguea and Aggarwal [5]. In this approach, each suc-
cessive image pair is used to reconstruct a 3D terrain map
through stereoscopic analysis, and then the position and
heading of the aircraft are estimated by matching this recon-
structed terrain map to a pre-stored, 3D digital elevation map
(DEM). In a sense, this approach is a two-dimensional exten-
sion of the DTS approach.
A more recent work that uses this approach is that of Sim
et al. [6], which explores practical implementation and
system integration issues. In this approach, the system is
composed of two parts: relative position and absolute posi-
tion estimation. Relative position estimation recursively
computes the current position of an aircraft by accumulating
relative displacement estimates extracted from two succes-
sive aerial images. The position error by relative position
estimation is compensated by absolute position estimation.
The absolute estimation employs two approaches: (1) match-
ing aerial image directly to reference images when the
aerial image contains distinct geometric (artiﬁcial) structures
such as roads, building; (2) matching recovered elevation
map (REM) with pre-stored DEM information in moun-
tain regions without artiﬁcial structures. Since reconstruction
and matching are performed each time that a position esti-
mate is updated and there are no integration operation, the
approaches in [5] and [6] do not have error accumulation.
However, the terrain reconstruction process itself is poten-
tially error-prone (since stereopsis is often a difﬁcult inverse
problem), and this can cause position estimation error.
Recently, some novel image-based navigation algorithms
have been developed. These approaches compare real-time
camera images against template landmarks, instead of DEM
information, in storage to achieve precise navigation. For
example, Michaelsen et al. [7,8] developed a testbed to utilize
known constructive features and patterns of salient man-
made objects from handbook on infrastructure construction
or thesauri. The declarative knowledge is presented as pro-
duction system, and after the instances of most primitive
object types are extracted from aerial image, the hypothe-
sis driven parser is used to control the search.
Celik et al. [1] developed a system called “Meta Image
Navigation Augmenters (MINA)” to use real-time camera
images in conjunction with XML based open source map
data to augment UAS navigation. MINA considered the
transportation layer of GIS from OSM (OpenStreetMap).
In this system, visual signiﬁcance of objects, with pre-
cise position in 3D world coordinate system, are analyzed,
and only visually distinguishing objects are rendered for
navigation augmentation purpose. The features of visually
distinguishing objects are pre-stored in the training sets as
landmarks. During a ﬂight in GPS-denied environments,
matching approaches that work with geometric shapes and
parametric curves including weighted K-nearest neighbor
classiﬁer, thin plate spline transform and principal compo-
nent analysis, are applied to match aerial images against
templates in the training sets. The matching result is used to
augment UAS navigation. This system is proved to work well
in urban areas. It has no accumulation error. However, this
approach could not be directly extended to terrain areas, as
the representation and matching methods for artiﬁcial struc-
tures cannot be applied to terrain.

2.2 Alternative approaches
Merhav and Bresler [9,10] proposed two position estimators
for UAS navigation. In the ﬁrst position estimator infor-
mation is obtained by integrating instantaneous velocity
estimates, which are obtained from inter-frame displacement
of the input image sequence and the airplane’s height read-
ings. In the second estimator the position information is
obtained from an extended Kalman ﬁlter (EKF), with the air-
plane’s position and velocity as its states and the inter-frame
displacement measurements as observation data. Using a the-
oretical analysis, Merhav and Bresler show that the naive
estimator suffers from error accumulation while EKF, which
“locks on to the available terrain information”, does not.
Merhav’s and Bresler’s paper however, does not report exper-
imentalresultsoftheirestimatorsonvideodata.Furthermore,
the motion model in their formulation is simple: the motion
is assumed to be only a translation and parallel to the ground
(leading to constant image motion ﬁelds).
Lerner et al. [11] propose an approach that uses the DEM
directly to generate a constraint between the camera’s pose
and ego-motion. This leads to a set of nonlinear equations
from which, in principle, one can solve for UAS’s pose and
for 3D motion. The focus was on expressions of error perfor-
mance measures using Monte Carlo simulations, since these
measures as a functions of various parameters are too com-
plex to evaluate analytically.
In other works [12–14], researchers adapt a technique,
known as SLAM (simultaneous localization and mapping),
to vision-based navigation. SLAM was originally developed
for a robot to estimate its own position (i.e., localization)
and the positions of a set of landmarks (i.e., mapping).
This is usually achieved through an EKF that uses range
measurements, from the robot to the landmarks, as obser-
vation data. Since vision sensors generally do not provide
range information directly, additional information is needed
to produce the needed range measurements. For example, the
technique in [12] and [14] requires man-made ground land-
marks with knowns sizes, while the technique in [13] requires
the knowledge of some ground landmarks (with known 3D
coordinates). However, these techniques are not scalable to
large area, because they use EKF and is n2 computationally
expensive.
Recently, Zhang et al. [15] proposed an approach that uses
the terrain DEM directly to estimate the position and orien-
tation of UASs. In this approach the position and orientation
estimation problem is formulated as a tracking problem and
solved by using an extended Kalman ﬁlter (EKF). For the
EFK, the UAS’s position, orientation, velocity and angular
velocity are deﬁned as states, and the small number of visu-
ally salient pixels (feature points) from the aerial images are
deﬁned as observation. The state and observation models of
the EKF are established based on an analysis of the imaging
geometry of the UAS’s video camera in connection with a
DEM of the area of the ﬂight, which helps to control estima-
tion error accumulation. One problem with this approach is
that one needs to consider complex aircraft model. Typically
the dynamics of the helicopter is described using a conven-
tional six-degree-of-freedom rigid body model, and therefore
EKF requires high computational overhead for derivations.
3 A novel approach: nature’s ﬁngerprint
Based on the discussion in the previous section, we know that
speciﬁc type of vision-based navigation algorithm, which
match the aerial image (recovered elevation map) against
landmarks (digital elevation map) pre-stored in databases
to estimate position of UAS, are attractive as they have no
error accumulation. For terrain matching, one alternative is to
match the recovered terrain elevation map against pre-stored
digital elevation model information. Another alternative is
to store vector terrain map data in database and generate
life-like appearances of terrain dynamically during ﬂight.
The aerial images can be matched directly against map data-
rendered terrain images via feature extraction and matching
processes to estimate position of UAS. The terrain recon-
struction process itself is potentially error-prone. This is
because stereopsis is often a difﬁcult inverse problem, and
this can cause position estimation error. We therefore propose
a novel approach that utilizes the aerial images combined
with open source map data to augment UAS navigation dur-
ing ﬂight over terrain area in GPS-denied environment.
A solution to augment UAS navigation in GPS-denied
terrain area is decidedly a minutaie-based approach due to
the way nature behaves. The following principal observations
of the features of nature has led to this train of thought.
1. Acts-of-nature type terrain features which present them-
selves as “detectable” to a machine vision system using
some generalizable method, are that of those shaped by
bathymetry. Water leaves a signature that has a random
probability distribution such that it can be statistically
analyzed. This cannot always be precisely predicted.
Common denominator in such problems, where heuris-
tic methods are involved in matching, is that exhaustive
approaches (e.g., correlation), and are impractical.
2. The positive artifacts of ﬁltering video signals that
contain water-shaped terrain using convolution kernels
yield results that visually resemble minutiae formations.
Spectral ﬁlters for example do not have that type of char-
acteristics (and for the same reason would not work well
with nature as they do with human-made scenes).
Observation (1) rules out correlation based approaches
because the search would have to be brute force. Correla-

(A) a processed image (from aerial image) to be matched;

ﬁnding a satisfactory correspondence matching between the

and come up with the most likely matching result in a prob-

3.1 Minutiae
Minutiae are the minor or incidental details of the terrain that
are ambiguous on their own. They have spatial characteristics
thatcanbeusedtouniquelyidentifyaparticulargeo-location.
These details are found in drainage patterns formed by water,
and captured in the bathymetry layer of GIS system. Note that
we are not assuming presence of water; only that it had once
been at that location. Some ridges date as far back as the ice
age, and the water that shaped that area is long gone. On the
other hand, a small creek or river which still has water ﬂow
is still perfectly applicable. Typical minutiae type examples
are shown in Fig. 1 and described below. The list is not meant
to be inclusive:
a. Ridge endings: A point at which a ridge stops.
b. Bifurcations: A point at which one friction ridge divides
into two friction ridges.
c. Dots: An isolated ridge unit hose length whose length
approximates its width in size.
d. Islands: A ridge slightly longer than dots, occupying a
middle space between two temporarily divergent ridges.
e. Enclosures: A single friction ridges that bifurcates and
rejoins after.
f. Spurs: A bifurcation with one short ridge branching off
a longer ridge.
g. Bridges: A connecting friction ridges between parallel
running ridge.
Fig. 1 Minutiae examples, including ridge endings, bifurcations, dots,
islands, enclosures, spurs, bridges, trifurcations, and crossings
Fig. 2 One example of ridge bifurcation (the orientation value is equal
to the angular direction of the speciﬁc ridge from the East)
h. Trifurcations: A point at which one friction ridge divides
into three friction ridges.
i. Crossings: A point at which two ridges cross each other.
Each minutiae m is described as a quadruple m =
{x, y, θ, T }, where (x, y) represent the minutiae coordinates,
θ represents the speciﬁc drainage pattern orientation at the
minutiae point, and T represents the minutiae type (e.g., end-
ings or bifurcations), as illustrated in Fig. 2. The quadruple
minutiae information is later employed by the matching algo-
rithm to measure the similarity between terrain areas.
Pliability of the underlying rock to water, deposition pres-
sure of water, slippage, roughness of the substrate are just
some of the various factors that determine how water forms
drainage patterns. Drainage patterns govern how minutia
are formed such that no two terrains are exactly alike. Our
approach is designed to bring out these signature features,
while ﬁltering out minor changes in impressions of the same
area recorded at different times with different ambient light-
ning, orientation, or seasonality.
Although minutiae are readily present in most terrain, they
are not in an immediately usable form due to the ambient
lighting, color, shadows, and many other factors that they are
usually hidden within. As minutiae appear as minor details in
drainage patterns, we ﬁrst employ a series of ﬁlters: wavelet

3.2 Wavelet transform
Wavelettransformisatime-frequency-transformation,which
decomposes images into different frequency layers. One
important advantage of wavelet transform is that by mod-
ifying one or multiple frequency layers and applying reverse
transform, images can be low-pass, band-pass or high-pass
ﬁltered.
It is well known that the slow varying frequency compo-
nents correspond to the slowly varying intensity components
of an image, i,e., average intensity, and the higher frequency
components begin to correspond to faster and faster intensity
changes in the intensity, i.e., details of an image. As minutiae
act as details of an image, we use wavelet transform as our
ﬁrst ﬁltering step to remove low frequency components of
an image. The wavelet-based procedure for extracting image
details, with the original image placed in the level J of the
approximation pyramid, is as follows:
1. Choose wavelet (e.g. Haar, Daubechies, Symlet. . .) and
the number of levels (scale), P, for the decomposition.
Then compute the FWT of the image.
2. Setting the approximation coefﬁcients at scale J −P to
zero.
3. Perform a wavelet reconstruction using the original detail
coefﬁcients and the all-zero approximation coefﬁcients at
level J −P.
We employ the Haar wavelet in our work due to its fast
speed and orthogonal property. The decomposition level P
is set to 7 based on our experimental results. At this level the
reconstructed images contain enough detail information for
crease extraction. Further decomposition does not result in
any better performance.

3.4 Gabor ﬁltering
Gabor ﬁlter [20] is a bandpass ﬁlter with frequency-selective
and orientation-selective properties which we tune to val-
ues where drainage patterns are likely to occur and exhibit
collinearity. The orientation ﬁeld of a drainage pattern image
deﬁnes the local orientation of drainage patterns contained in
a mountain. The least mean square estimation method is used
to compute the orientation image. The frequency image rep-
resents the local frequency of creases in the drainage pattern
image. The three steps for computing the frequency image
are as follows. (i) We divide the crease image into blocks of
size W × W, and compute the mean orientation within the
block. (ii) We project the gray-level values of all pixels within

the local minimum points corresponding to the creases in the
crease image. And (iii) we compute crease spacing S(i, j)
by counting the median number of pixels between consecu-
tive local minima points in the projected waveform. Then the

4.1 Map data selection
We considered and experimented with a variety of maps,
before deciding to evaluate and integrate Geo-registered
radar returns (GRRR) from an open source provider, USGS.
Accelerated by the proliferation of small, affordable, and
lightweight electronically scanning radar systems and UAS,
GRRR imaging is becoming an incredible resource for
logistics. GRRR can be characterized as structured, heteroge-
neous, and scientiﬁcally collected with consistent amounts of
completeness and standardization of resolution. GRRR has
the following advantages:
– GRRR is open-source. USGS provides it in GEOTIFF
numerical format whereas other providers will rather pro-
vide a raster image.
– GRRR data is based on ASCII, which is both a very
convenient debugging feature, and resistant to data cor-
ruption.
– While GRRR can be used in quadrilateral tiles that is not
an obligation. It can be downloaded at any size, shape, or
proportions, and does not have to be complete.
– GRRR does not carry any watermarks, legends, brand
logos, and other artifacts to be forcibly rendered on map.
– GRRR is customizable, and that is the primary reason
it can be made to suit this application. With other map
providers, the map always looks like how the provider
wants it to look. Rendering, aliasing, coloring, compres-
sion, line-styles, and many more parameters are their
proprietary style of map and cannot be modiﬁed.
– GRRR allows ﬁner control over emphasizing particu-
lar features of interest of terrain. For example, it can be
height thresholded. Water bodies can be individually col-
ored or removed together. Vegetation can be dynamically
modeled. There are no limits.
Many sources provide abstract map service at one level
or another. For the purpose of choosing which one is most
suitable, several providers shown in Fig. 7 have been test-
driven. For the following problems other sources have, we
have to disregard all but USGS-SRTM database to gather
GRRR:
– Non-contiguous maps where breaks can become artifacts
with not real counterpart.
– Address interpolation algorithms which attempt to assign
coordinates to an address based on the ZIP code rather
than actual coordinates.
– Algorithms (that Google use for the most part) that tend
to learn user behaviors and change the map accordingly.
– Inaccuracies and missing data.
– Coloring of the terrain to reﬂect ofﬁcial colors of a com-
pany logo.
– Highways shields, park boundaries, national landmark
symbols rendered directly on the terrain.
– Restrictive IP bound API’s that do not operate on another
computer, or on the same computer moved to a different
network.
– Inaccurate geographical location names for lakes. For
example, Google Maps Laona, Wisconsin identiﬁes one
of the town’s two major lakes as “Dawson Lake” whereas
the USGS, State of Wisconsin, and local government
maps all identify that map feature as “Scattered Rice

4.2 Landmark selection strategy
Landmarks refer to speciﬁc areas which we render from map
data GRRR and store in the training set. Per mission we
assume there exists a set of Markovian ﬂight paths. Only
areas around such paths are considered for landmarks. When
a match is identiﬁed, the number of correspondences is inﬂu-
enced by minutiae density in the area, i.e., more dense, more
correspondence for a positive match, therefore when choos-
ing landmarks we consider terrain areas displaying complex
drainage patterns with high priority, trying to reduce miss
rate for a given matching threshold. More details about the
minutiae-based matching will be given in Sect. 5.
4.3 Rendering image from USGS data
Computers represent 3D objects with polygons. The more is
the number of polygons, the more detail can be represented.
This works very well for geometric volumes such as a cube
where few polygons can represent very large objects. How-
ever when it comes to a whole mountain it takes billions of
polygons to represent it in 3D as in much detail as it is seen
from an aircraft, which is a huge computational burden.
Using a general purpose graphics processor unit (GPGPU)
however we can use fewer polygons (hundreds) to represent
a 3D mountain where simulation of lighting of bumps and
dents is interpolated according to natural models. Natural
landscapes behave in generally multispectral-fractal manner.
Therefore, a real approach to modeling landscapes requires
the ability to modulate fractal behavior spatially. We achieve
this via Displacement Mapping [26]. The main steps in the
basic idea of real-time template creation utilizing GRRR data
are given as follows.
1. During normal (GPS enabled) ﬂight, periodically, a set of
predicted aircraft paths are generated based on the mis-
sion and dynamic model of the aircraft. They are intended
to indicate what locations the aircraft might end up in the
coming few minutes if it was lost.
2. Based on the initial search window on the previous step,
a region of interest is picked from the GRRR stored on
the aircraft pre-mission. This data is passed on to GPGPU
videomemoryandit remains thereuntil either GPSsignal
is lost, or the aircraft moves on such that new region of
interest is needed.
3. Loss of GPS triggers an enable signal. The current PVA
knowledge of aircraft, along with time of the day, day of
the year, known weather and atmospheric conditions, are
also passed on to GPGPU.
4. GPGPU models a virtual camera at the current altitude
of the aircraft. A virtual sun is also modeled using direc-
tional light model and positioned with respect to the
aircraft, based on the time of day. GPGPU then mea-
sures elevation steps in the radar return and generate a
vegetation texture dynamically using the elevation.
5. GPGPU renders terrain image from GRRR data via Per-
Pixel displacement mapping with distance map. More
details about Per-Pixel displacement mapping approach
along with its implementation on GPU are both provided
in [26].
6. At this point in the algorithm GPGPU has generated a
life-like appearance of terrain as it would appear at that
time of day, season, and orientation. This image then
will go through a series of steps (i.e., wavelet transform,
diffusion ﬁltering, MLSEC operator, Gabor ﬁltering and
then CN operator) to come up with a set of minutiae.

5.1 MLSEC-based crease extraction
As map aided UAS navigation methods involve matching
real-time images against template images in the training sets,
rendered from open source map data, we apply the MLSEC-
based crease extraction method to both real-time mountain
images and rendered mountain images with open source map
data, to show its effectiveness. For a mountain, ridges and
valleys are dual in the sense that the valleys of a mountain
image are the ridges of the inverted image. In this paper, we
focus on the mountain valleys and set creaseness threshold
T = 0.5 for valley extraction. That means that the pixel Pi j is
a valley point as long as MLSEC operator ˜κ2[i, j] at the pixel
is smaller than −T , i.e., ˜κ2[i, j] < −T = −0.5. Note that,
as mentioned before, we need to apply wavelet transform
to remove the low frequency component of the image, and
ﬁlter the wavelet transform processed image with diffusion
process based on structure tensor, before extracting creases.
Parameter values for wavelet transform, diffusion ﬁltering
and valley extraction are given in Table 1.

5.4 Minutiae-based matching algorithm

the shape context combined with local orientation descrip-

correspondences (i.e., matching minutiae pairs) between the
two input images. We employ the total number of correspon-
dences to describe the similarity between two input images.

Wehavepresentedanovelapproachtousemountaindrainage
patterns for GPS-denied navigation of small unmanned aer-
ial systems operating in mountain areas, with no assumption
of human-made geographic objects. In this work, drainage
patterns formed by water and their critical minutiae points
are used as a novel representation of mountain areas. The
use of minutiae points allows us to employ conventional
minutiae-based ﬁngerprint matching algorithm to match aer-
ial images to pre-stored landmarks, taking advantage of the
analogy between mountain drainage patterns and human ﬁn-
