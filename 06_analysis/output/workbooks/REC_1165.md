UAV Localization in GPS-Denied
Environments: A Review
Yusen Wu , Dawei Liu , Jieming Ma , Senior Member, IEEE, and Xu He
Abstract—With the increasingly wide application of unmanned
aerial vehicles (UAVs) in various scenarios, accurate UAV localiza-
tion in indoor and urban environments has been a critical issue to
address. However, in these signal-degraded environments, global
positioning system (GPS) localization becomes unreliable; thus,
alternative localization methods are essential. This article surveys
localization methods for UAV systems in such environments.
We classify these methods into two groups: 1) onboard-only
methods, which rely exclusively on onboard sensors and 2) hybrid
methods, which incorporate external anchors. To evaluate their
performance in UAV localization systems, we ﬁrst analyze the
hardware requirements, the data preprocessing strategies, and
the localization algorithms of the two groups, and then compare
their experimental settings and localization accuracy. Finally, we
summarize the strengths and limitations of these two groups
and outline several research directions on GPS-denied UAV
localization.
Index Terms—Autonomous navigation, global positioning sys-
tem (GPS)-denied localization, indoor navigation, sensor fusion,
unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs).

III. INITIALIZATION AND PREPROCESSING METHODS
In this section, we discuss hardware initialization and front-
end preprocessing for UAV localization. These operations
are performed prior to localization and have a signiﬁcant
impact on localization performance. Following the classiﬁ-
cation framework adopted throughout this survey, relevant
studies are grouped by onboard-only and hybrid methods.
A. Hardware Initialization
1) Onboard-Only: One common initialization approach in
onboard-only methods is sensor calibration, which aims to
estimate the geometric relationships among onboard sensors.
This oﬄine calibration measures relative rotation and transla-
tion among sensors mounted on the UAV body to align their
respective coordinate frames. For instance, Xiao et al. [38] and
Vidal et al. [39] both developed an oﬄine sensor alignment
scheme between the IMU and camera. Without precise calibra-
tion, inconsistencies in spatial and temporal alignment would
introduce signiﬁcant errors into state estimation, ultimately
degrading localization performance.
To reduce the eﬀort required for oﬄine calibration, some
methods employ online sensor calibration during ﬂight. For
example, VINS-Mono [37] performed online calibration using
the ﬁrst 30 stable frames of visual feature tracking. By trian-
gulating stable visual features, an up-to-scale translation was
reconstructed while aligning corresponding IMU data [80].
This step included gyroscope-related calibration and camera-
IMU alignment.
2) Hybrid: Distance-based hybrid localization algorithms
require prior knowledge of the exact coordinates of all
deployed anchors in the environment to formulate the local-
ization equations. In addition to measuring the positions of
anchors in advance, some solutions estimated external anchor
coordinates during online ﬂight without manual calibration.
The WiSion method [58] applied two calibration processes:
one involved aligning the coordinate frames of the UAV-
mounted Wi-ﬁantenna with the IMU, and the other involved
calibrating anchor locations. The coordinate calibration esti-
mated the UAV’s initial position, velocity, and orientation,
as well as the positions of access points (APs) deployed in
the environment. The method performed coordinate calibration
through an automatic initialization procedure. During this pro-
cess, the user manually moved the UAV for several seconds,
while the camera and Wi-ﬁmodules kept collecting data.
Assuming that the system behaved linearly over this short
period, a least-squares problem was formulated. The result-
ing optimization problem was then solved using Newton’s

B. Front-End Preprocessing Methods
1) Onboard-Only:
Data preprocessing in onboard-only
localization methods commonly faces challenges related to
visual feature degradation, such as variations in environmental
lighting and occlusions, which may lead to unstable feature
tracking.
To improve the stability of visual tracking, Sani and Karim-
ian [36] employed ArUco markers to help identify visual
features in the environment. An ArUco marker, which is com-
posed of black and white grids, contains data bits and check
bits. When the onboard camera detected square boundaries by
using the OpenCV library [82], the parsing algorithm deter-
mined the orientation that yielded the highest data validation
accuracy. This process enabled robust recognition in complex
environments.
Building on these vision-based techniques, Vidal et al. [39]
enhanced feature extraction robustness in unstable light condi-
tions by incorporating data from event cameras. Event cameras
can generate pixel-wise asynchronous events; however, these
events cannot be directly aligned with the corresponding RGB
camera frames. Therefore, all pixel events collected by the
event camera over a period were synthesized and grouped into
a virtual two-dimensional (2-D) event camera frame, which
facilitated subsequent feature extraction.
Since VIO techniques heavily rely on visual feature points,
stable extraction of features is important. Luo et al. [45]
proposed using line segment merging techniques to combine
several small, closely spaced parallel line segments into a sin-
gle stable, longer segment. This approach eﬀectively reduced
unstable fragmentation of line features during feature extrac-
tion. The Omni method [46] employed LiDAR to identify and
track feature points. After the LiDAR scan, feature points were
ﬁltered according to their height. This strategy reduced track-
ing failures caused by unstable low-altitude features. Similarly,
Chang et al. [51] used onboard LiDAR for measurement. They
applied voxelization to the point cloud data accumulated over
three frames to partition the data into regular volumetric units.
Edge-located voxels were then extracted and tracked as stable
environmental feature points. To mitigate feature loss from
viewpoint rotation and tilt, Chen and Jiang [49] employed a
rotation-equivariant network based on steerable convolutions.
This inherent equivariance improved feature extraction by
eliminating the need to compensate for viewpoint rotation
or tilt.

2) Hybrid: UAV localization methods based on hybrid
system architectures rely heavily on signal transmission and
typically employ measurements such as TOF, RSS, and CSI.
Various issues arise in signal transmission, propagation, and
reception. For instance, when multiple anchor points emit sig-
nals simultaneously, it is necessary to distinguish the sources
of each signal. Signal propagation also suﬀers from environ-
mental noise and occlusions. Furthermore, in indoor scenarios,
multipath signals may reﬂect oﬀsurrounding surfaces before
reaching the receiver and interfere with useful line-of-sight
(LOS) data. The following studies present representative solu-
tions to these issues.
The RAIL method [55] deployed multiple ultrasonic trans-
mitters at given reference points within a room, enabling
the UAV to receive several ultrasonic signals from these
reference points. The method introduced frequency hopping to
mitigate multipath eﬀects. The key idea is to switch frequency
bands after the arrival of the original LOS signal but before
the reﬂected signals are received. This mechanism ensured
that the signal used for data decoding is primarily based
on the clean LOS component, thereby reducing multipath-
induced ranging errors. Similarly, Vasisht et al. [60] applied
nonuniform discrete Fourier transform (NUDFT) to model
each signal path from the mixture of multiple paths to mitigate
multipath eﬀects. After obtaining the TOF for all candidate
paths, the shortest TOF path was selected as the primary
direct path, based on the fact that the direct path requires
the shortest propagation time. Li et al. [35] modeled both
LOS and NLOS signals to exclude samples contaminated by
NLOS propagation. The method assumed that the residuals of
pure LOS measurements follow a Gaussian distribution, and
iteratively identiﬁed signal samples contaminated by NLOS.
This method used a dynamic threshold to remove NLOS
signals in a few iterations, thereby avoiding large-scale global
recomputation.
Several studies further improved the reliability of data trans-

dimensional (4-D) RSS interpolation over the 3-D physical
coordinate space and RSS sample space, enabling an 80%
reduction in the required number of sampling points while
maintaining localization accuracy.
To further improve localization robustness under noisy
wireless measurements, some studies adopted outlier ﬁltering
strategies. For example, Liu et al. [57] conducted batch-
wise outlier detection on Wi-Fi-derived RTT measurements.
A batch of RTT values was collected within a short time, and
the mean of these RTT values was computed as the estimated
RTT since position changes during this short interval were
negligible. Additionally, this method incorporated an outlier
penalty term within the KF to suppress outliers that are not
removed during the preprocessing stage.
AIM [70] utilized spectral features generated under diﬀer-
ent propeller ﬂight speeds to estimate the UAV pose. After
collecting audio signals from multiple external microphone
anchors, the method evaluated audio beam stability to iden-
tify NLOS conditions. If the conﬁdence level of an audio
anchor fell below a predeﬁned threshold, measurements from
this anchor would be regarded as unreliable and temporarily
removed.

B. Hybrid
The following discussion categorizes the hybrid localization
algorithms based on probabilistic ﬁelds of RSS signal strength,
linear equation formulations, iterative optimization algorithms,
acoustic frequency analysis, and DL.
1) RSS Space: RSS ﬁngerprint-based localization exploits
signal attenuation characteristics during signal propagation.
Since the UAV’s distance to diﬀerent anchor points varies
in space, the resulting RSS attenuation diﬀers. The HiQuad-
Loc [53] method employed multiple Wi-ﬁAPs deployed in
the environment to measure RSS intensities from various
sampling points to these anchor APs to construct a joint
RSS probability map. During UAV ﬂights, real-time signal
strength measurements were compared with the constructed
probability map to identify the most likely location. This
approach employed KF to optimize the localization esti-
mates by incorporating historical trajectory data. The proposed
method attenuated the weighting of historical path points near
corners. This adjustment is necessary because the kinematic
state and environmental context may change signiﬁcantly
after turning. Consequently, trajectory information collected
before a maneuver becomes less informative for subsequent
localization.
2) Trilateration Rules: Numerous methods relied on geo-
metric relationships between the UAV and anchor points to
perform trilateration. Each anchor deﬁnes a sphere centered at
its location, with a radius equal to the measured distance to
the UAV. For 2-D localization, the intersection of three such
circles provides an estimate of the UAV position, whereas
four anchors are generally required to resolve 3-D localization
problems.
Eckert et al. [54] reported a trilateration method in which the
UAV transmitted ultrasonic and Wi-ﬁsignals simultaneously.
Environmental microphones received the ultrasonic signals and
estimated their propagation time using the Wi-ﬁsignal for
synchronization. Given the known speed of sound, the distance
between the UAV and each microphone was calculated. A trio
of coplanar microphones was utilized to perform an initial
2-D coordinate estimation via trilateration. After estimating
the UAV’s 2-D position, the vertical height was subsequently
determined by incorporating the estimated horizontal coordi-

superimposed signals, allowing frequency shift calculations

distances to individual anchors. With measurements from at
least four anchors, the UAV’s 3-D coordinates were computed
using the linear system equation.

these limitations, alternative approaches combined multiple
measurement modalities for hybrid localization. For instance,
Liu et al. [57] employed Wi-ﬁRTT data for anchor-UAV
distance measurements, combining trilateration-based global
localization with IMU-based short-term navigation. An error
state KF (ESKF) was then used to fuse both measurements
and reduce the residual.

B. Trade-Oﬀ
In this section, we discuss the advantages and disadvantages
of onboard-only and hybrid methods. We have summarized the
trade-oﬀbetween these two system architectures in terms of
accuracy, robustness, and deployment cost in Table V.
1) Trade-Oﬀof Onboard-Only Methods:
Onboard-only
localization methods rely on IMU, VIO, or radar sensors to
analyze the UAV’s inertial motion and environmental features.
These methods estimate the UAV’s pose between successive
key frames. Since estimation error between closely spaced
frames is typically limited in the short term, inertial-based
onboard-only systems can achieve relatively high short-term
localization accuracy. Furthermore, sensors mounted on the
UAV body maintain alignment and do not require recalibra-
tion across diﬀerent operating environments, reducing labor
during deployment. However, this approach faces two critical
challenges. First, the long-term drift caused by accumulated
error is a fundamental limitation of these methods. Since
sensor measurements only provide displacement information
relative to the previous frame or within a short sliding window,
they cannot reconstruct the UAV’s global trajectory reliably.
Although displacement estimation errors may be negligible
over short durations, small estimation biases accumulate over
time, leading to signiﬁcant deviations for the localization
system [103]. The second issue is the instability of envi-
ronmental feature tracking. This instability can stem from
various factors, including abrupt lighting variations, object
occlusion, and motion blur induced by high-speed UAV move-
ment. When a signiﬁcant portion of feature tracks becomes
inconsistent or lost, the visual odometry system may experi-
ence frequent cycles of feature loss and recovery, which can
introduce cumulative errors and disrupt the continuity of pose
estimation.
2) Trade-Oﬀof Hybrid Methods:
Hybrid localization
methods primarily rely on communication between anchors
deployed in the environment and signal transceivers mounted
on the UAV. These methods exploit measurements such as
the TOF to estimate the distance between the UAV and
individual anchors. A key advantage of these methods is
their ability to directly compute the UAV’s global coordinates
by leveraging the positions of the anchors. However, these
methods also face signiﬁcant challenges related to anchor
deployment and signal interference. The ﬁrst challenge is to
accurately determine the positions of the external anchors,
which is a requirement for distance-based localization. In
real-world deployment scenarios, anchor placement may be
restricted due to environmental constraints. The second major
limitation of hybrid methods is signal interference. During
propagation, signals may be blocked by environmental obsta-
cles, such as walls or structural columns, leading to NLOS
conditions. Furthermore, these RF signals are susceptible to
multipath interference, making it diﬃcult to identify the LOS
path and degrading distance measurement accuracy. Advanced
signal processing techniques have been proposed to mitigate
multipath eﬀects as discussed in Section III-B, but they often
require increased computational overhead, creating a trade-oﬀ
between accuracy and system eﬃciency.

VI. CONCLUSION
This article analyzes and compares existing UAV localiza-
tion systems for GPS-denied environments. Based on the types
of devices used, the surveyed methods are categorized into two
groups: those relying solely on onboard equipment and those
utilizing a hybrid architecture that combines onboard sensors
with external anchors for position estimation. For each group,
we ﬁrst summarize the hardware components involved and
then examine how their data preprocessing pipelines and local-
ization algorithms address challenges, including inter-device
discrepancies, measurement inaccuracies, and environmental
interference. Finally, this article compares the advantages
and limitations of each category based on experimental set-
tings and ﬂight performance, followed by a discussion of
existing issues and future research directions related to sys-
tem consistency, environmental constraints, and sensor fusion
