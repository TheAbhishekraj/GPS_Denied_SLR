Abstract—The use of autonomous Unmanned Aerial Vehicles
(UAVs) continues to increase for a wide range of applications,
such as wildlife monitoring, planetary exploration, and emer-
gency Search and Rescue (SAR). Autonomous UAVs typically
rely on Global Positioning System (GPS) usage and most oper-
ations require correct flight conditions with good visibility (no
rain, fog nor smoke). However, some difficult environments are
characterized by partial to no GPS signal, limiting the drone’s
ability to localise itself. Moreover, visual obscurants affect the
UAV’s performance and behaviour by restricting its visibility,
adding complexity to some subsystems such as target detection.
This paper presents the development of a framework enabling
a drone to autonomously navigate and explore in GPS denied
environments impacted by visual obscurants. The study illus-
trates this by formulating the navigation and target detection
problem as an autonomous Sequential Decision Problem (SDP)
with uncertainty caused by degraded visibility and lack of GPS.
The SDP is modelled as a Partially Observable Markov Decision
Process (POMDP) and tested using the Adaptive Belief Tree
(ABT) algorithm.
The navigation task was created following
the model of a classic SAR operation in a cluttered indoor
environment with visual obscurants. The framework includes
target detection under uncertainty from a vision-based camera.
The formulated SDP is tested in a simulated environment with
partial observability and in presence of smoke. The framework
integrates Gazebo, Robot Operating System (ROS), Software In
The Loop (SITL) and PX4 firmware to recreate a SAR scenario
in simulation.
Experiments conducted in the simulated SAR
scenario test the target finding task under different levels of
visibility, with the target position being unknown, and a pose
uncertainty modelled from real-life flight experiments. The ex-
periments in normal visibility were a success, but the developed
framework was limited in presence of visual obscurants, reduc-
ing the target detection rate. Allowing an UAV to autonomously
navigate in challenging environments with low visibility con-
ditions aims at increasing the range of UAV’s utilization in
critical applications such as SAR and mining, where human
interventions are often dangerous and unfeasible.

1. INTRODUCTION
The popularity of UAVs continues to increase, from recre-
ational to commercial and industrial purposes. In particular,
autonomous UAVs have known a gain in interest in critical
applications such as surveillance [1], mining [2], and SAR
[3]. These missions gain in complexity when taking place
in difficult and challenging environments, compromising the
UAV’s ability to localise itself and to perceive its surround-
ings. GPS may also not be present, and visual obscurants in
the form of dust, smoke, or low luminosity are characteristics
of such environments. These particular conditions are of great
challenge for autonomous navigation, decision making, and
target detection.
With the objective of increasing the autonomy of an UAV in
GPS denied and visually degraded environments, this work
proposes the combination of localisation algorithms in the
form of odometry using on-board sensors, with a POMDP
framework to model the navigation and target detection tasks
as a SDP under uncertainty.
In this case, the POMDP
computes a motion plan while taking in consideration the
uncertainties caused by the lack of GPS and the presence of
visual obscurants. This paper presents a starting point for
a framework allowing an UAV to explore and find targets
using vision based sensors in low visibility environments.
The pose-estimation uncertainty is modeled from real-life
experiments that tested an odometry algorithm using a 2D
LIDAR and IMU in low luminosity conditions. The exper-
iments conducted in simulation highlight the challenges of
visually degraded environments, as well as the limitations of
the proposed framework. Possible solutions are proposed,
and future work is suggested.

2. RELATED WORK
Robot missions are often subject to uncertainties. Decision
making under uncertainty is the process of an agent receiving
an incomplete or noisy observation at a precise time, and then
choosing an action with respect to this observation [4]. In
particular, uncertainty and partial observability are modelled
in two mathematical frameworks, Markov Decision Process,
and POMDPs. POMDPs can be used in several UAV appli-
cations and were proven to be functional for navigation under
partial observability and uncertainty [5–8]. More importantly,
POMDPs were proven useful for UAVs navigation in difficult
and uncertain environments. As an example, Vanegas et al.
[9] formulated a navigation problem for a small UAV with
sensor and environmental uncertainty in cluttered and GPS-
denied environments using POMDP. In their paper, the map
and obstacles’ position were known to the agent, and only
the target was known but with uncertainty.
The POMDP
solver used was the ABT solver. In later work, Vanegas et
al. [11] proposed a framework enabling drones to navigate
in unknown and GPS-denied environments.
Simultaneous
Localisation and Mapping (SLAM), visual odometry and
POMDP were fused to model a SDP under uncertainty us-
ing the ABT solver.
The framework was only tested in
simulation using Gazebo Classic and ROS. Similarly, [12]
proposed a framework for an UAV’s autonomous navigation
under uncertainty and partial observability from imperfect
sensor readings in cluttered indoor scenarios. The navigation
problem was modelled as a POMDP and was solved in real
time with the ABT solver.
Autonomous perception in challenging visibility conditions
requires the use of multiple sensors allowing the agent to
perceive its surrounding environment. Related research in
terms of UAV navigation under degraded visibility was ex-
plored by [13–16], where the objective was to allow a small
UAV to autonomously explore and navigate in subterranean
environments. The main framework proposed was the com-
bination of a 3D LIDAR, thermal camera, RGB camera and
an IMU to perform odometry and SLAM. The LIDAR was
the main sensor used for odometry and mapping, using the
LIDAR Odometry and Mapping (LOAM) method [17], while
the thermal and RGB camera were used solely for odometry.

3. BACKGROUND
POMDP
In order to model the navigation problem as an autonomous
SDP, POMDP was selected.
POMDP is a mathematical
framework that models decision making under uncertainty
in motion and action in a non-fully observable environ-
ment.
A POMDP is modelled using these parameters:
(S, A, O, T, Z, R, γ).
S is the state space, a finite set of
states, representing the possible conditions of the UAV. A
is a finite set of actions that the agent can execute to go
from one state to another. O is a finite set of observations
that the UAV can perceive.
T is the transition function,
modelling the transition of the agent from one state to the next
after performing an action a. Z is the distribution function
modelling the probability of observing o from state s after
executing an action a. R is a finite set of rewards, and γ is the
discount factor with γ ∈[0, 1]. In a POMDP, the robot state
is not represented by a single estimation, but by a probability
distribution called a belief state b, with B being the set of all
possible belief states.
The objective of a POMDP is to determine a sequence of
actions given a current belief state b that maximizes the
discounted accumulated reward. This sequence of actions is
called a policy, and represented by the symbol π. The dis-
counted accumulated reward is the sum of all the discounted
rewards from each action executed during the mission. The
solution of a POMDP is to find an optimal policy π∗: B →A
that maps belief states to actions, and that maximises the
total expected discounted return. Equation 1 represents the
mathematical expression of the optimal policy.

POMDP Solver Adaptive Belief Tree
The Adaptive Belief Tree is an online POMDP solver. On-
line POMDP solvers model only the known part of the
environment and its dynamics, and update the model dur-
ing the execution when new information is available. The
main limitation of online POMDP solvers is that most of
them recompute policies at every time step, discarding any
solutions that were previously computed, wasting valuable
computational resources. ABT was selected for its ability
to reuse and improve the previous policy when there are
changes in the POMDP model. Moreover, ABT can be used
in problems formulated in continuous state space using a
generative model.

UAV Navigation Task
A simulated environment was created to test the framework.
The environment consists of a restricted flying area, several
obstacles, and visual obscurants in the form of smoke. A
target, with a position unknown to the UAV, is placed in the
flying area. The principal objective of the UAV is to explore
the environment in search of the target. LIDAR and inertial
odometry for pose and motion estimation are assumed to
be incorporated in the UAV. The autopilot is also assumed
to include the take-off, landing and return home functions.
The target detection subsystem and observations are operated
using a vision-based camera facing downward. The mission
comprises two phases: the take-off to the required altitude,
and the autonomous navigation phase which ends when the
target is found. A successful mission is defined by several
parameters, including staying in the restricted flying area,
avoiding obstacles, and detecting the target. Different levels
of visibility are tested, with a pose uncertainty modeled from

The state space S = (Sa, St) is composed of Sa, the state

UAV state space is characterized by: Sa = (pa, oa, fc, fr),
in which pa = (xa, ya, za) is the position of the agent and
oa = ψa its rotation in the world Cartesian frame.
The

was simplified and restricted to the yaw angle.
fc, fr are

The target state is defined by: St = (pt, ft), where pt =
(xt, yt, zt) is the position of the target and ft is a discrete
state representing either the target has been found or not.

Observation Model
The ABT solver is a generative model that produces an
observation o, a reward r, and a new state s from an action
a taken at a precise state. It is therefore required to model
an observation o that is the result of an action a and a new
state s′. The observation model includes the UAV estimated
position in the world coordinate frame opa, as well as the
location of the target opt when it is detected by the camera.
The detection of a victim using the camera’s Field of View
(FOV) was based on Sandino et al. [12,19,20]. It consists of
calculating the footprint area of the vision-based sensor using
its sensor width, height, and focal length. The sum of the
angles between the pairs of points comprising the footprint
area and the target belief position is then calculated. The
target location point is predicted to be within the camera’s
FOV if the calculated sum is equal to 2π. In this paper, it is
assumed that there is no detection uncertainty, meaning that a
detection is always assumed to be correct.
5. IMPLEMENTATION
Sensor Selection
Using on-board sensors, the agent is assumed to compute its
position without GPS in a known map, as well as to detect
targets in visually degraded environments. Thus, the sensors
should be selected with respect of the following requirements:
• They can perceive its surroundings in presence of visual
obscurants in the form of smoke, dust, or low luminosity.
• They can estimate the motion of the agent.
• They respect small UAV constraints of size, weight, and
computational power.

System Architecture
The system architecture is highlighted in Figure 3, and is
comprised of a SITL module, containing the sensors and
UAV, a localisation and detection modules, and a decision
making module containing the POMDP solver.
Software in the Loop
To control the UAV, the open source flight control software
PX4 [26] is used. The PX4 architecture can be separated
in two layers: the flight stack, which is an estimation and
flight control system, and the middleware, a general robotic
layer. The former contains altitude and position estimators,
as well as pipelines of flight controllers.
The latter can
support a large variety of autonomous robots, and contains the
communication interfaces and the simulation layer. The PX4
Software in the Loop allows to use this PX4 architecture in a
simulated environment running on a local machine. Gazebo,
a derivation and upgrade from Gazebo Classic, was used
to develop the simulation environment. Gazebo is an open
source simulator for robotics, in which sensor models and
high fidelity physics are made available for the users. Gazebo
was chosen over Gazebo Classic for its ability to model visual
obscurants in the form of particles, creating simulated smoke
or fog. The UAV used in this application is the X3, which
was already modelled in the PX4 framework.

Environment Setup
The simulated environment was created following the model
of a cluttered and GPS-denied environment. Two maps of
different sizes were created, with a flying area of 5x5 and
10x10 meters respectively, and a maximum flight altitude of
2.5 meters. Each map is also composed of several obstacles
in the form of columns. Using Gazebo libraries, particles
were also generated to simulate the presence of smoke. In
this research, the smaller testing environment comprised three
obstacles, while the larger comprised nine obstacles.
In
both environments, the target position is unknown to the
agent. The selected target for these experiments is an Aruco
marker, which is located at a fixed position of (2.0, 1.0, 0.2)
for the 5x5m map, and (3.5, 3.5, 0.2) for the 10x10m map.
The environment is represented by a 2D grid-map, with the
obstacles, target, as well as starting position of the UAV
located in the center of the selected cells. The UAV uses the
take-off mode from the PX4 autopilot to reach the selected
altitude before the decision making module sends actions to
the autopilot.

6. RESULTS
The framework is tested with two different sizes of maps
comprised of different obstacle configurations, two levels
of visibility, and different ranges of altitude in the reward
function. The first level of visibility is referred as normal
visibility, with no presence of visual obscurants. The second
level of visibility is referred as low visibility, characterized by
the presence of smoke.
5x5m Map
Three series of tests were run in this environment:
1. Normal visibility with range of altitude of: (2.1, 2.5)(m),
defined as high altitude.
2. Low visibility with high range of altitude.
3. Low visibility with range of altitude of: (1.3, 1.7)(m),

highlight its limitations in the presence of visual obscurants.
Lowering the altitude of the agent was one solution proposed

30 times, with a mean time step’s duration of 2.0 seconds.
In these experiments, the number of actions for the agent
was limited to a 100, restricting the maximum flight time at
around 3.3 minutes.

Results Discussion
As shown by the results in both series of tests, the framework
developed is efficient in a normal visibility environment, with
the target being consistently detected. In both series of tests,
the flight time was limited because of an issue that caused
the UAV to get stuck in an area, with the same series of
actions repeating itself. This issue can be caused by either an
internal problem with the ABT solver, or a lack of complexity
from the reward function, causing the action output to be
repetitive as no better actions were found. The scalability of
the framework was demonstrated, but limitations were shown
in presence of visual obscurants, with a target detection rate
of 56.6% and 50% for the small and large map respectively.
The detection rate was already improved by lowering the
range of altitude of the UAV, but is still far from the efficiency
shown in normal visibility. Figure 9 highlights the current
limitations of the system, with a missed detection caused by
the presence of visual obscurants. As the smoke is modelled
using moving and temporary particles, its thickness changes
with time, causing randomness in the detection. If the altitude
of the UAV is too high, the target will be completely occluded
by the smoke, making the detection even more challenging.
Moreover, the range of altitude included in the reward func-
tion was manually modified between test cases. The agent
was able to modify its altitude within the selected range, but
the UAV was not able to change this range by itself, limiting
the autonomous abilities of the drone.

7. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
This paper presented a problem formulation and a framework
for UAV navigation, exploration, and target detection in GPS-
denied environments with different levels of visibility. The
problem formulation was developed as a Partially Observable
Markov Decision Process, and was able to perform a mission
based on Search and Rescue and emergency operations incor-
porating uncertainty and partial observability. The framework
was tested in simulation using Gazebo, ROS, PX4 firmware
and TAPIR. Two maps were tested to verify the scalability
of the framework, and the target position was unknown to the
UAV. The framework performed perfectly in normal visibility
environments, but the presence of visual obscurants reduced
its performance, cutting almost in half the target detection
and success rate. The results demonstrated the ability of the
framework to manage uncertainty in localisation and action,
but the target detection module, limited by the sole use of a
vision-based sensor, was too impacted by degraded visibility.
Future work includes modelling the agent’s ability to detect
changes in visibility and categorizing ”low visibility” and
normal visibility conditions. The presence of visual obscu-
rants or low luminosity would be detected by the UAV, and the
reward values and function would be modified accordingly.
Future work will also include automating the change of range
of altitude, and the implementation of the LIDAR/Inertial
odometry.
Finally, a thermal camera would be added on-
board the UAV, with sole purpose to detect targets in visually-
degraded environments. The objective will be to develop an
UAV able to detect low visibility, to change its behaviour
accordingly, and to select the appropriate sensors depending
on the environment’s visibility conditions.
