Long-Term Aerial Robot Localization based on Visual Odometry and
Radio-based Ranging
F.J. P´erez-Grau1, F.R. Fabresse2, F. Caballero2, A. Viguria1 and A. Ollero2
Abstract— Visual odometry approaches have demonstrated to
work very well for localization in GPS-denied areas, however
they could quickly diverge depending on the environment.
In order to achieve reliable long-term localization, it is a
good practice to complement vision-based estimations with an
additional source of information able to correct odometry drifts.
This paper presents an approach that complements stereo
visual odometry with distance measurements from several radio
beacons located in an indoor scenario. The computational
efﬁciency of the whole processing pipeline allows for real-time
and bounded drift localization. Field experiments have been
conducted using an indoor testbed with a very accurate tracking
system in order to validate the algorithms.
I. INTRODUCTION
Robot localization and navigation are the basic pillars of
mobile robotics. They have been extensively researched in
the last three decades for ground robots with great results in
both GPS and GPS-denied areas. Although not generally,
2D navigation and localization can be assumed in most
ground robots, simplifying the approaches and also the
computational resources needed. Extending these algorithms
to 3D poses serious challenges that go further than space
dimensionality. Sooner or later they will be overcome thanks
to the improvement in embedded computing.
Working with aerial robotic vehicles imposes limitations
in terms of payload, computational resources and sensors that
should be taken into account in real systems. Additionally,
in most cases, aerial robots cannot assume the existence of
reliable odometry systems as it is usually assumed in ground
robots.

At the cost of higher computational requirements, stereo-
vision systems provide direct depth measurement in a general
3D environment for a given camera baseline. SLAM [7] and
pure odometry approaches [8] demonstrate good results at
short-term localization and even in long trajectories without
loop closing. Many of the algorithms developed for stereo
vision odometry and SLAM can be applied to 3D cameras
based on pattern projection [9] [10].
Point-cloud based odometry and localization is another
signiﬁcant research area. This approach produces a metric
point-cloud of the environment of the sensor, usually based
on LIDAR or Time-of-Flight camera technologies. Although
computationally expensive, recent advances in kd-trees [11]
and Approximate Nearest Neighbours (ANN) [12] enable
fast computation in the presence of medium and dense point-
clouds.
Autonomous systems intended to operate over long pe-
riods of time usually perform some sort of loop closing in
order to recognize revisited places. This allows to reduce the
localization uncertainty at the cost of adding computational
complexity. Reliable place recognition can be difﬁcult in
large-scale scenarios or sites with repetitive structures. This
is a delicate issue, since a single wrong loop closure can
result in a devastating failure of the localization system.
On the other hand, range sensors have been an active
research topic in the last decade in the domain of local-
ization and SLAM systems. These devices make possible
the localization of robots or any kind of objects in GPS-
denied environments, while offer a low-cost solution which
does not require direct line of sight (LOS) between each
pair of sensors. In addition, the data association problem is
often solved by attaching the sensor identiﬁcation with the
range measurement information. Range sensors have been
applied to different areas, such as localization of autonomous
robots in manufacturing environments [13], outdoor mapping
of RFID-based range sensors using an Unmanned Aerial
Vehicle (UAV) [14] or in underwater robotics scenarios [15].
The advantage of not requiring LOS between each pair of
sensors makes them very useful for the localization problem
in unexplored environments.
However, the information provided by these range sensors
is a simple measurement of the distance between two range
sensor, which makes their integration in localization and/or
mapping applications specially difﬁcult due to the lack
of bearing information, which leads to multiple location
hypotheses.

tion of a visual odometry solution and distance measurements

II. APPROACH OVERVIEW
Figure 1 shows an overview of the method. The localiza-
tion approach is based on the integration of visual odometry
and distances to known radio beacons in order to reset
cumulative errors.
The odometry system combines inertial sensors, stereo
computation, pose estimation and key-framing in a loose-
coupling ﬁlter to build a reliable and accurate (at short-
term) localization system. However, odometry will eventually
diverge after a period of time. Range sensing to ﬁxed beacons
is used to readjust localization errors.
Thus, the approach makes use of short-term localization
estimation based on stereo visual odometry and range to ﬁxed
positions in order to detect and correct the cumulative errors
of the odometry. This paper shows how both estimations
(visual and range) beneﬁt from each other and are integrated
into a single ﬁlter to get fast and error-bounded localization.
Experimental results will show how the 3D localization error
is bounded to 0.4 meters RMS approximately.

III. VISUAL ODOMETRY
To ﬁnd a reliable solution to the localization problem, the
robot motion must be estimated. This section focuses on
the vision-based motion estimation algorithm implemented
through the use of a stereo camera. The main sensor is the
Visual-Inertial (VI-) Sensor, which provides fully-calibrated
and time-synchronized IMU and stereo camera data streams.
The main processing modules are presented in Figure 1.
A. Feature Detection
At each frame, after removing the image distortion from
the stereo pair, a set of interest points is found from both the
left and right images. These interest points are visual features
extracted using the Features from Accelerated Segment Test
(FAST) algorithm [17], which is a method for corner detec-
tion with a strong computational efﬁciency.
Sometimes the scene is very homogeneous, or there are
many features grouped in a speciﬁc strong-detailed region
in the image. This could lead to inaccurate measurements
in subsequent stages of the algorithm. In order to over-
come such situations, a minimum number of features is
required to be extracted from equally divided regions of
the images, commonly known as buckets. On the other
hand, a maximum number of features is deﬁned in order
to select from each bucket only the most powerful ones,
according to the FAST detector deﬁnition. The combination
of both parameters allows an homogeneous distribution of
the strongest selected features in both images. Our approach
is based on subdividing the image into six buckets (two rows
and three columns). The difference between the originally
detected features and the ones detected using the bucketing
technique can be seen in Figure 2.
B. Feature Description
The next step involves ﬁnding correspondences between
the sets of features from the left and right images. Some kind
of feature point description is needed in order to compare and

ﬁnd similarities between each pair, and for that the general-

(BRIEF) descriptor [18] is used. This descriptor is very
efﬁcient since it is based on simple intensity difference tests,
and the similarity evaluation is done using the Hamming
distance. Together with the computational efﬁciency, BRIEF
descriptors are especially interesting for feature tracking
under small visual perturbations, a very usual situation in
visual odometry.
C. Stereo Matching
The two sets of feature descriptors are then matched
between the left and the right images from the stereo pair.
A robust matcher has been implemented, which rejects
correspondences that are not symmetrical (i.e. are not found
from the left to the right image and viceversa). It makes use
of epipolar geometry to signiﬁcantly reduce the matching
candidates, speeding up the process.
D. Pairwise Feature Matching and Key-Framing
Once a set of robust features has been found from the
stereo pair of images, a second matching stage is established
across consecutive frames, but this time only focusing on the
images from the left camera. This allows to ﬁnd a subset
of even more robust features which have survived to both
matching processes. If enough matches are found between
the features from the previous and the current left images,
this new set of matches is used to solve a Perspective-n-Point
problem (PnP), i.e. the pose estimation from n 3D-to-2D
point correspondences. In particular, the EPnP algorithm [19]
has been used, which provides an efﬁcient implementation
of the pose change estimation between both images.
In order to partially mitigate the effect cumulative errors in
odometry, a key-framing approach has been adopted. Thus,
new key-frames are produced when the feature tracking
ﬂow exceeds a given threshold. At that moment, the current
left image descriptors, their 3D estimated position and the
robot position/orientation are stored. Subsequent frames will
compute the odometry with respect to the last key-frame
(and its pose) instead of the immediately preceding image,
so that errors are only accumulated with the introduction of
new key-frames.
E. Attitude Correction
The VI-Sensor used in this approach counts with an
integrated IMU which provides calibrated and synchronized
data that can be fused with the computed pose updates. In
particular, the odometry system estimates the camera roll
and pitch angles based on accelerometers and gyroscopes
and integrates them with the purely visual estimation in a
loose-coupling manner.
The ﬁnal sensor pose update is transformed to the aerial
robot body frame and used to calculate the new relative
position and orientation estimations with respect to the initial
pose.
IV. INTEGRATION OF VISUAL ODOMETRY AND
RANGE-BASED LOCALIZATION
As previously introduced, range measurements to radio
beacons installed in the environment will be used in order to
improve the visual odometry localization. Radio-based range
sensing provides distance to ﬁxed locations that can be used
to absolutely localize the aerial robot, in a similar fashion
as Global Positioning System (GPS) works. However, the
measurement noise typically present in radio-based distance
computation and, mostly, the outliers produced by radio
multi-path effect normally prevent its use as stand-alone
localization system when high accuracy is required.
Although localization based on visual odometry will even-
tually diverge due to cumulative errors, it can be used as a
short-time predictor of the UAV motion and hence, constraint
the robot localization based on range. Thus, using the odom-
etry as motion prior might help ﬁltering range measurement
noise and detecting/removing measurement outliers. At the
same time, the range-based localization will help to remove
the cumulative errors in the odometry, building an accurate
and stable localization system both together.
A small infrastructure of low-cost and low-power radio
sensors is used in this setup. This kind of system is usually
employed in applications where some object or person is
intended to be tracked. The philosophy is inverted in our
approach by installing sensors in ﬁxed positions, plus an
additional sensor on-board the aerial robot so that it can
measure the distance to all the sensors in the environment
and compute its position.
The sensors used for localization are manufactured by
Nanotron Technologies (see Fig. 3). The sensor onboard the
UAV computes the distance to all the sensors at 5Hz ap-
proximately, and integrates the information into an Extended
Kalman Filter (EKF) described below. The sensor makes
use of Time-of-Flight technology to compute the distances
between nodes, so it is relatively immune to attenuation with
the distance.
The measured distance has a standard deviation of one
meter approximately, in addition to outliers due to reﬂections
and multi-path effects. Figure 3 shows a characterization of
the errors in the measurement in indoor experiments. It can
be seen how the measurement follows a Gaussian distribution
with some outliers.
As the robot moves, the radio sensor periodically sends
out a query, and any other sensor within range responds
by sending a reply. Since each sensor transmits a unique
ID number, distance readings are automatically associated
with the appropriate tags, so the data association problem is
trivially solved. The robot can estimate the distance to each
responding sensor by means of Time-of-Flight, even when
they are out of LOS. This is very useful in situations where
visual-based methods usually fail, such as poorly illuminated
scenes or highly dynamic environments.

a ﬁlter update; the distance to a single sensor is enough.

prediction stage, which is expected to be accurate and stable
at short- and mid-term. This is a great advantage with respect
to state-of-the-art range-based localization systems, which
usually wait until the distance to three or more sensors are
measured.
B. Outlier rejection
Range sensing based on radio is subject to frequent
outliers when performed indoors. These outliers are caused
by multi-path and signal reﬂection in walls and structures,
and introduce signiﬁcant errors in the localization.
The odometry prior (EKF prediction) provides a short-
term accurate and reliable estimation of the robot’s position,
so the EKF measurement residual y(k) = z(k)−h(x(k), v)
can be used to decide whether a range measurement is an
outlier or not. Thus, if the residual of a range measurement is
above a threshold rth, the measurement is determined as an
outlier with high probability and the ﬁlter will reject the data.
This threshold has been set to rth = 2m in our experiments.
V. EXPERIMENTS
In order to validate our approach using visual odometry
and radio-based measurements described in sections III and
IV, an aerial robot has performed a relatively long ﬂight
(a bit less than 7 minutes). The ﬂight has taken place in an
indoor controlled scenario where a small set of range sensors
(six sensors) have been installed. The main objective of the
experiment is to demonstrate the suitability of the approach
in real-time during regular operations.
The experiments have been performed at the indoor
testbed of the Center for Advanced Aerospace Technologies
(CATEC). This testbed can be used to develop and test
technologies related to autonomous systems. The useful
volume where tests can be conducted is a cube of 15x15x5
meters. Part of it can be seen in Figure 4. The testbed
houses an indoor localization system based on 20 VICON
cameras. It only needs the installation of passive markers
on the objects to locate and/or track. This system is able
to provide the position and attitude of each object in real-
time with millimetric precision, and with very low latency
in communications.

mated localization compared with ground truth data obtained
from the testbed tracking system. Prior to the discussion
of the experimental results, the setup describing all the
elements involved in the experiment is presented in the next

VI. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
A method suitable for long-term localization for an aerial
robot has been presented. Visual odometry based on a
stereo camera and IMU measurements is used along with
radio-based measurements provided by a number of range
sensors installed in the environment. Experimental results
demonstrate the feasibility of the approach, both in accuracy
and computational efﬁciency, using an aerial robot during a
long ﬂight.
Although the presented approach is able to estimate the
robot localization with small errors, it can be improved with
other localization inputs as visual place recognition. Visual
appearance can be added on top of the current processing
pipeline in order to recognize revisited locations. This will
allow the aerial robot to reduce the uncertainty on its location
when a loop closure is detected. Special care should be taken
with this improvement to ensure that the complete processing
approach is still computationally efﬁcient.
