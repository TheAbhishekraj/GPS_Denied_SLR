Abstract—In order to develop a nano UAV in a GPS-denied
environment, a visual-inertial system (VINS) consisting of a
monocular camera and a low-cost inertial measurement unit
(IMU) should be integrated, as they make a minimum, ultra
lightweight sensor suite that enables both autonomous ﬂight and
sufﬁcient environment awareness. Although there are numerous
potential applications, getting to understand the structure of the
ﬂight platform can be cumbersome and confusing. Packages and
resources provided on the Qualcomm developer network (QCN)
and their ofﬁcial GitHub group ATLFlight contain un-uniﬁed
and disorderly chunks of information. In this paper, we present
an organized documentation that presents in detail, the platform
structure as a whole, from hardware to software architecture,
including installation and API related applications.
Index Terms—Snapdragon Flight, visual inertial odometry,
UAV, tutorial
I. INTRODUCTION
Localization and navigation in an indoor environment using
nano Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAV) always pose great
challenges. Since Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS)
reference is unavailable, the UAV has to rely on its own
onboard or external sensors to navigate. There are different
types of sensor modules such as sonar, laser range ﬁnders,
Ultra Wide Band (UWB) and vision. Sonar is fast and cheap
but usually very crude, whereas a laser scanning system is
active, accurate, however, a typical 3D Lidar sensor is not
suitable to be mounted on a nano UAV due to its large size
[1][2]. UWB radios can provide a GNSS-like localization
method in an indoor environment [3], but it requires some
external setup to be done prior to the real ﬂight, thus making it
unsuitable for exploration of an unknown indoor environment.
A vision-based localization is feasible for low-cost and energy-
constrained UAV, especially when it is combined with a low-
cost IMU, which makes it a powerful VINS, and a minimum
ultra lightweight sensor suite that allows robust autonomous
ﬂight. Moreover, Visual-Inertial Odometry (VIO) technique
has been extensively studied and proven by many researchers
as in [4][6].
Therefore, in order to develop a nano UAV to work in
a GPS-denied environment, we focus on the platform with
VINS integration, as well as high computation power and
low power consumption. With the great demand in both
academic research and commercial applications along with
rapid development of industrial manufacturing techniques ,
tons of off-the-shelf ﬂight controller board are available in
the market, and as the core part of a UAV, it is the key to
realize fully autonomous and intelligent.
Typical ﬂight controller board, such as Pixhawk, Octago-
nal Pilot on Chip system (OcPoC), AscTec Trinity, NAVI02
and Qualcomm Snapdragon Flight are popular for their high
performance [7]. Features of each platform are summarized
as follows: Pixhawk is one of the most powerful and popular
autopilots in the market due to its open software and hardware
design sources and combined func tionality of the PX4FMU,
PX4IO and GCS, making it suitable for secondary develop-
ment. However, it has limited computa tion capability and high
level vision algorithms are impossible to be executed on it.
OcPoC has a dual ARMa9 core processor and al FPGA, which
enables ﬂexibility in hardware and parallel computing abilities.
Trinity provides triple redundant sub-autopilot systems, which
maximizes the redundancy of the autopilot. Navio2 integrates
a Raspberry Pi board, which brings it more processing power
and makes it possible to implement some basic high level
algorithm on-board. Snapdragon Flight is the only one in all of
the platforms that integrates an optical ﬂow camera and one 4K
resolution video camera along with high level Machine Vision
(MV) SDK and Snapdragon Navigator (SNAV) SDK, making
it so much easier for people who wants to develop their own
utilization with a UAV under GPS-denied environment. Based
on the analysis above, we have decided to use the highly-
integrated Snapdragon Flight controller board as our autopilot.
The related algorithms implemented on the ﬂight board are
introduced in [8] [9]. Some applications implemented on a
nano UAV with Snapdragon Flight such as an autonomous
swarm of UAV test is shown in [10].
The paper will be organized as follows: we will ﬁrst intro-
duce the hardware structure of the Snapdragon Flight, followed
by the development environment setup. Then the applications
on both development SDK and ATLFlight repository are intro-
duced, after which we will test the reliability and robustness of
the ﬂight platform by implementing trajectory following and
aggressive ﬂying missions and present the experiment results.
A conclusion is given at last.

II. HARDWARE OVERVIEW
A. Snapdragon Flight board
The Snapdragon Flight board is based on the Qualcomm
Snapdragon 801 processor, which features three units: Quad-
core CPU, 4x Qualcomm Krait 400 CPU (Up to 2.5 GHz);
Qualcomm Adreno 330 GPU; Qualcomm Hexagon DSP. Ap-
plications can be built using different processing units based on
their intended purpose. It also integrates most of the necessary
sensors, which includes a barometric pressure sensor, GNSS,
9-Axis IMU Sensor, downward optical ﬂow camera with
resolution of 640480 and a 4K high resolution front camera.
The board can either be wired accessed via serial port and
USB, or remotely accessed via Bluetooth 4.0 and 2.4G/5.8G
WIFI connection.
B. Assembly instruction
To build a UAV with Snapdragon Flight, the Qualcomm
Flight Kit, Qualcomm Electronic Speed Control (ESC) board,
Dragon Drone Development Kit (DragonDDK) are needed.
The DragonDDK is an almost ready to ﬂy UAV kit that
includes a pre-assembled frame with motors, propellers, an
indicator LED, a Wi-Fi antenna. The assembly instructions
are given in [11] [12]. A Spektrum DX-8 is chosen as
the remote controller. Moreover, the Stereovision Kit can be
assembled as an add-on option for the Snapdragon Flight Kit.
It provides two additional forward-facing cameras for stereo
vision applications. The hardware structure is shown in Fig.
1.

IV. APPLICATIONS
The vision and control API as well as applications that
run in both Snapdragon Flight and workstation obtained from
the MV SDK, SNAV SDK and the ofﬁcial GitHub group
ATLFlight, are abundant but disordered and confusing. In this
section, the architecture and features are given for those APIs
and applications.
A brief structure is shown in Fig. 2. The ﬂight controller is
running on the DSP of Qualcomm Snapdragon 801 processor,
which can make sure the real-time property. The CPU and
GPU are used to run Linaro system, robot operating system
(ROS) and high level algorithms. Both the ROS-based and non
ROS-based applications makes use of the Machine Vision and
Snapdragon Navigator API. The qrlSDK, Hexagon SDK and
tools are needed if one wants to develop applications to run
on the Snapdragon Flight platform with their workstation. An
android tablet controller application is provided as well.

A. Non ROS-based applications
1) Machine Vision SDK: The MV SDK supports the fol-
lowing functions: VISLAM for 6-DOF localization and pose
estimation, dense depth mapping with stereo, downward facing
tracker for relative localization, voxel map for 3D depth fusion
and mapping, online monocular camera calibration for both
stereo and monocular camera, camera auto gain and exposure
control. After installation, one can have access to many online
and ofﬂine (i.e., playback) applications provided in this SDK.
• Online applications
The online applications, running real-time, demonstrate
various functionalities. The mvCameraView is used for live
camera stream given by the camera ID. The IDs of the four
onboard cameras are shown in Table III.
The mvCapture is used for capturing sensor data sequences.
The mvCPA is for getting good exposure/gain for tracking
features. The mvVISLAM can display the 6-DOF poses. The
mvDFS is for dense depth mapping.

• Playback applications
There are playback applications on how to exercise the
algorithms performance by using ofﬂine data which has
been captured by mvCapture, SNAV or a developers own
application. See more details on the machine vision quick
start guide [15].
2) Snapdragon Navigator SDK: SNAV SDK is comprised
of multiple libraries, applications, and conﬁguration ﬁles.
Applications in SDK include snav for running Snapdragon
Navigator, snav calibration manager for sensor calibration, as
well as automatic ﬂight real-time viewing tool snav inspector.
• Snapdragon Navigator
SNAV is a vision-supported ﬂight controller application
which can be started with the command: $ start snav. It will
also autostart some other process, such as the DFT and Qual-
comm UAV Controller companion processes by default. And
multiple vision-related applications including snav dft app,
snav dft vio app, and snav voa app can be conﬁgured as
autostart functions by changing the corresponding parameter
ﬁles associated with each application located in /etc/snav
folder. See Navigator user guide [16] for further information.
When running the SNAV, the core ﬂight controller runs
on the DSP and supports various ﬂight modes from manual
to assisted modes. Other components, such as vision-based
algorithms run on the applications processor, i.e. CPU and
GPU. Furthermore, data logging is started automatically when
SNAV is started and all the onboard data, including UAV
status, sensors data and estimated results are logged to a binary
dump in the /var/log/snav/ﬂight/ folder.
• SNAV Inspector
SNAV Inspector is a console application that enables live
data viewing from the UAV, such as the status of the UAV
and sensors as well as the estimation result of VIO. It works
by continuously reading log ﬁles. The post-processing log
parsing capability is also a feature of this application, which
can be useful for system tuning and discovering the cause of
an unexpected behavior.
• SNAV calibration manager
The
snav calibration manager
contains
several
sensor
calibration procedures triggered via a RC or through the
SNAV. Although there are many calibration procedures,
the following two are the most important and have to
be done before ﬂight, which are the static accelerometer
offset estimation and the dynamic accelerometer offset and
trim estimation. See Navigator user guide [16] for further
information.

A. Simulation setup
A simulation setup is required to ensure that a new appli-
cation works as intended during simulation before the doing a
real ﬂight test. The procedure to setup a network for multiple
UAVs is provided below as well. Simulation mode is a feature
of SNAV, which uses a simulation engine that models physics
and sensors as sensor inputs. To start a simulation, the SNAV
needs to be shut down ﬁrst, and then launched in simulation
mode by ‘$ snav -w sim time’. The sim time is the minimum
simulation seconds.
With SNAV running in simulation mode, the live simulated
results can be observed using snav inspector. The UAV status
can be checked on rviz in ROS. Perform a real ﬂight test after
the new application works ﬁne in simulation mode. For real
tests, before running any of the programs, the UAV should be
placed on a ﬂat ground with plenty of visual texture.

C. Trajectory following
We evaluated two aggressive trajectory paths, ﬁgure 8 and
spiral, in simulation mode. The trajectories were generated by
our own safe corridor navigation with jerk limited trajectory
generation algorithm [19].
• Figure “8”: The traversing path has a length of 8 m along
the y Cartesian axis. The results are shown in Fig 9.
• Spiral path: The traversing path has a length of 2 m along
both the x and z Cartesian axes. The results are shown
in Fig 10.
Despite the trajectories having different velocities and ac-
celerations, the error results are in the same range of values.
The maximum error in x and y direction are at around ±0.1 m.
Error in z axis could be larger. It is caused by the initialization
of VIO or the estimation drift due to the lack of feature
numbers and bad light conditions in the test room.
VI. CONCLUSION
This paper has uniﬁed several ofﬁcial documents into one
clear and organized tutorial on developing a nano UAV us-
ing the Snapdragon Flight platform. Simulation results of
trajectory-following and aggressive-ﬂying missions have been
given to show the reliability and robustness of this ﬂight
controller platform. Future works will include experimental
veriﬁcation of our own high level algorithms such as trajectory
generation, human tracking and an autonomous swarm of
UAVs with the presented Snapdragon Flight platform.
