Abstract
This paper presents a smart hybrid vision‐aided inertial navigation system
(VAINS) for enhancing the navigation accuracy of UAVs in GNSS‐denied envi-
ronments. The proposed system consists of GNSS, INS, monocular visual
odometry (VO) based on optical flow and regression trees, a Gaussian process
regression (GPR), and extended Kalman filter (EKF) for data fusion. Although
a variety of monocular VO‐based on photogrammetric, structure from motion,
and machine learning approaches have been proposed to assist the navigation
process, the accuracy of 3‐D positioning using these techniques is still affected
by some factors such as the lack of the observed features, incorrect matches,
and the accumulated positioning drift errors. Therefore, the development of a
reliable navigation system that can handle such challenges is necessary. In this
system, the GPR algorithm is deployed to model these errors when the GNSS
signal is available and to correct them when the GNSS signal is lost.

INTRODUCTION
Over the past few years, autonomous Unmanned Aerial
Vehicles (UAVs) have been extensively deployed in a
variety of civil and military applications. UAVs play a
key role in different civil applications such as monitoring
of territory, surveying infrastructures (roads, power lines,
pipelines), mapping, search and rescue, disaster assess-
ment and management, and firefighting. In addition, they
have been utilized in many military missions such as bor-
der monitoring, reconnaissance, and communication
nodes behind enemy lines.
In order to meet the needs of these applications, an
accurate and reliable navigation solution is required. In
the majority of current autonomous navigation systems,
the typical onboard sensor installation for most of the
commercial
UAVs
relies
on
fusing
both
Global
Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) and Inertial Naviga-
tion System (INS) measurements using a Kalman Filter
(KF) for positioning and localization purposes. With
UAVs performing missions in challenging and cluttered
environments, the GNSS signals might be lost or suffer
from
attenuation,
multipath
effects,
jamming,
and
spoofing. During the GNSS signal outage period, the vehi-
cle
navigation
states
are
obtained
by
the
INS
in
standalone mode until GNSS signals are retrieved. Hence,
the navigation solution will deteriorate due to the drift
exhibited by the INS solution even during short periods
of GNSS signal outages. Consequently, using other aiding
sensors or techniques is essential to limit the accumu-
lated errors associated with INS measurements during
the GNSS signal outage. In this study, a novel smart
hybrid vision‐aided inertial navigation system is proposed
based on the main idea of optical flow‐based for vision‐
aided inertial navigation using regression trees.1
Visual Odometry (VO) is the process of estimating the
motion of a camera in real time using sequential images.

The proposed system in this paper extends our previ-

• The proposed system attempts to correct the VO and
INS systems used by the previous work1 by predicting
both monocular VO drift and INS drift based on
trained Gaussian Process Regression (GPR)2 against
GNSS reference data.
• The proposed system has a real‐time training perfor-
mance since the monocular VO, GPR for INS, and
GPR for monocular VO training times are 0.027,
0.018, and 0.012 seconds, respectively, for 1 minute
of collected data. Such computational performance
allows a real‐time and incremental training of the
employed GPRs whenever the GNSS is available as a
reference to enhance the learned GPR and capture
the recent and new drift behaviors over time.
• Contrary to many other machine learning drift predic-
tion approaches, the employed GPR can provide both
predictions and their associated uncertainties to be
utilized to assess the quality of the GPRs predicted

applied to the monocular VO and INS outputs versus
when they are discarded.
• Unlike many other approaches that depend on a
priori collected data for training purposes, the pro-
posed training process can take place during the flight
upon the availability of reference GNSS data. Such
ability enables the system to capture the effect of sen-
sor parameter changes from one flight to another as it
allows for using the most recent data for training.
The proposed system handles both the INS and VO
drifts in a smart fashion as the proposed system does
not employ a single off‐line training session before the
operation. Instead, it adopts a real‐time and incremental
learning session whenever the GNSS is available as a ref-
erence to enhance the learned GPR and capture the
recent and new drift behaviors over time. Counter to
many other visual/INS navigation approaches that adopt
a single training session such as Clark et al,3 the proposed
incremental learning allows the system to adapt its model
for new VO/INS drift behavior that emerges during the
flight and also allows in situ learning if no prior data is
available for training.
This system employs monocular VO, INS, Extended
Kalman Filter (EKF), and GPR. In addition, this system
consists of three main blocks: (1) monocular VO,1 (2)
GPR for INS drift prediction, and (3) GPR for monocular
VO drift prediction. All these blocks are integrated
together to obtain a more accurate navigation solution.
The employed machine‐learning approaches consist of
two sessions, namely, training and prediction. During the
training phase, the GNSS/INS derived navigation states
are utilized as ground truth inputs for these different
blocks to build the proper underlying function that maps
the inputs to the desired outputs. The training takes place
during the flight (even if no previous data are available for
training purposes). The best performance from the pro-
posed system is expectedly achieved when all three blocks
have been trained while the GNSS signals are available.
For example, if the GNSS signal is lost after the training
of the first and second blocks and before the training of
the third block, the navigation solution will be based on
the first and second blocks only. On the other hand, if
the GNSS signals are available for a long time, the third
block can be trained, and the navigation solution will be
based on the three blocks. The proposed system employs
the different types of predictions (Monocular VO, VO drift,
INS drift) in a hybrid fashion and it switches between the
usage of these predictions based on the availability of their
accomplished learning at each time.
In the proposed system, the GPR has two functions.
First, the GPR is used to predict the velocity and orienta-
tion error states and correct the velocity and orientation
drifts exhibited by the inertial navigation mechanization
output. Second, the GPR is used to obtain the velocity
errors in the flight path direction between the ground
truth (GNSS/INS) and the predicted velocity component
from the monocular VO during the GNSS signal outage.
The main benefit of using such a scheme is its ability to
model the different drift errors (monocular VO drift and
INS drift) when the GNSS measurements are available
and to predict those errors during the GNSS signal out-
age. This helps to improve the system reliability.
The proposed algorithm has been evaluated using a
public airborne dataset from outdoor environments to
assess its feasibility, performance, and accuracy.

2.1 | Visual sensors for GNSS denied
environments
In the last few decades, vision has been used in many
applications to achieve different tasks such as camera
pose estimation, 3D reconstruction of the surrounding
environment, and obstacle avoidance. Onboard cameras
are small in size and lightweight, have low power con-
sumption, and can provide useful measurements in terms
of color and texture, which make them more appealing
for small UAVs.
VO can assist in achieving these different missions
through three main steps that form the basis of most
VO approaches: feature detection, matching, and camera
pose update. This architecture is used for example in pre-
vious studies.4-6
In many cases, VO is integrated with an Inertial Mea-
surement Unit (IMU),7-9 and it is then termed a Vision‐
Aided Inertial Navigation System (VAINS). Alternately,
many other sensor configurations rely on using vision alone
as a pose estimator10-12 and these are simply called Vision‐
Based Navigation (VBN). Both VAINS and VBN can be
classified into two main approaches (1) monocular VO
and (2) stereo VO. Monocular VO has received considerable
attention due to its small size and low cost13-19 which
makes it more convenient for many different applications,
such as robotic devices.16 Monocular VO can be imple-
mented by using a variety of photogrammetric and com-
puter vision approaches. The two primary implemented
types of monocular VO are Structure from Motion (SfM),
an approach that generates and refines structure at every
time step,20,21 and a fundamental matrix approach, which
computes the relative translation and orientation between
successive frames without constructing the 3D point
clouds.22,23 Although monocular VO techniques have some
benefits, they still suffer from the scale ambiguity if no
external aiding measurements or any a priori knowledge
about the surrounding environment is available. To dis-
band this problem, a stereo or multiple pairs of rigidly fixed
cameras with overlapping views are used.24-26 The stereo
VO solution accuracy typically depends on its lever arm
and bore sight parameters. Therefore, any small change in
these parameters during the flight can affect the triangula-
tion process accuracy as well as the estimated navigation
solution. Moreover, the short baseline between the fixed
stereo pair limits the triangulation accuracy. So, the stereo
VO is only used with UAVs flying at lower altitudes.
Alternatively, a limited amount of work based on
machine learning techniques has been applied to monoc-
ular VO algorithms. Such approaches attempt to learn the
relationship between image observations and cameras
ego‐motion, without requiring geometric camera models.
A monocular VO based on GPR and Support Vector
Machines (SVM) regression algorithms have been pro-
posed in Vitor and Ramos27 and Ciarfuglia et al,28 respec-
tively. These approaches utilize the optical flow as input
for the training session. Although these approaches are
successfully capable of estimating the vehicle motion
and resolving the scale ambiguity, their machine learning
based approaches are not capable of handling missing
optical flow vectors in some image parts due to the lack
of the observed features or inconsistent matches caused
by repeated patterns. This forms a serious issue for esti-
mating the navigation solution.
Deep learning approaches have been explored for
localization purposes. The research work in Konda and
Memisevic29 proposes VO based on a Convolutional Neu-
ral Network (CNN) through synchronization detection
between image sequences and features.
The main limitation of this work is its treatment for the
pose estimation problem as a classification rather than
regression. Another VO based on an appearance “map”
of the scene is introduced in Kendall et al.30 A trained
CNN model is used to achieve this task. This network
needs to be re‐trained or at least fine‐tuned while navigat-
ing in a new environment. This issue limits its widespread
usage as a generic solution in unknown environments. To
tackle this problem, a dense optical flow‐based approach is
utilized to train CNN models rather than RGB images for
motion estimation in Costante et al.31 A pre‐processed
dense optical flow is used as the input for this network,
which limits its incorporation for real‐time applications.
This happens because such a CNN network is not capable
of modeling image sequences or videos for sequential
learning. To overcome this issue, Recurrent Neural Net-
works (RNN) have been merged with the CNN in Clark
et al.3 The RGB images for the monocular camera, IMU
acceleration, and angular velocity measurements are used
as inputs. The three‐dimensional translation vector and
four‐dimensional orientation quaternion are the outputs
for this network. Although the proposed system has the
ability to eliminate the need for manual calibration
between the IMU and camera and is capable of performing
end‐to‐end sequential learning, the network requires a
long time to be trained (6 hours). In addition, large
amounts of data are required for training purposes. Such
requirements limit its usage if the vehicle platform param-
eters have been changed from the training time and also
eliminate the ability to train this network online during
vehicle navigation.

SYSTEM OVERVIEW
The Smart Hybrid VAINS framework proposed in this
paper overcomes the VAINS limitation factors and
enhances the positioning accuracy and reliability during
GNSS signal outages.1 This system is considered smart
as it attempts to exploit the reference solution (GNSS
when it is available) to enhance solution accuracy
through modeling error behavior. This system is imple-
mented in six major steps as follows:
1) Extraction of optical flow parameters,
2) GNSS/INS integration,
3) GPR‐based INS drift correction,
4) Monocular VO based on optical flow and regression
trees,
5) VAINS (monocular VO/INS integration), and
6) GPR‐based monocular VO drift correction.
In addition, the system consists of three main opera-
tional phases. The first and second phases act upon the
GNSS signal availability while the third phase occurs
when the GNSS signal is lost. The first phase is a training
session for the monocular VO and INS drift predictor
(GPR). The second phase is the training stage for the mon-
ocular VO drift predictor (GPR) to model the errors associ-
ated with the predicted velocity from the monocular VO.
The third phase is a prediction stage for the monocular
VO drift predictor (GPR) when the GNSS signal is lost.

VAINS algorithm1 during GNSS outage periods. The
VAINS algorithm is affected by the IMU biases and lack
of observed features or inconsistent matches. The pro-
posed system can reduce such effects on the perfor-
mance by modeling the main trend for different drift
errors (monocular VO drift and INS drift) when the
GNSS measurements are available and predicting those
errors during the GNSS signal outage. The proposed sys-
tem is divided into three main modes of operation as
follows:
1) The first phase is a training phase for the monocular
VO and the INS drift predictor when the GNSS signal
is available.
2) The second phase is a prediction phase for the mon-
ocular VO and the INS drift predictor also when the
GNSS signal is available. In order to properly model
the monocular VO drift error behavior as the same
as the situation when the GNSS signal is lost, two
separate EKFs are used to fuse the GNSS/INS and
monocular VO/INS, respectively. This phase is also
a training phase for the monocular VO drift predic-
tor. The predicted velocity in the flight direction
obtained from the VO is used as training input and
the difference between the ground truth forward
velocity obtained from GNSS/INS integration and
the predicted velocity from VO is used as output for
this monocular VO drift predictor.
3) The third phase is a prediction phase for the monoc-
ular VO, INS drift predictor, and monocular VO drift
predictor when the GNSS signal is lost.
The proposed system is evaluated through two outage
periods. To assess the proposed system performance, four
experiments were performed in each outage period as
follows:
1) The INS was tested in standalone mode for 1 minute
of GNSS signal outage.
2) The monocular VO/INS integration (VAINS) was
tested for 1 minute of GNSS signal outage. The VO
was trained for 1 minute when the GNSS signal is
available. The VO starts to predict after 1 minute
from the training session and the VO/INS integration
is assessed in the third minute (GNSS signal outage).
This experiment is shown in the first row of Figure 8.
3) The monocular VO/INS integration augmented with
the INS drift predictor was tested for a 1‐minute
GNSS signal outage. The VO and INS drift predictors
were trained by the collected data during the first
minutes when the GNSS signal is available. The VO
and INS GPR started to predict in the second minute
and were assessed in the third minute (GNSS signal
outage). This experiment is shown in the second
row of Figure 8.
4) The smart hybrid VAINS was tested for a 1‐minute
GNSS signal outage. The VO and INS GPR were
trained during the first minute when the GNSS signal
is available. The VO and INS GPR started to predict
in the second minute and the VO GPR was trained
during this minute when the GNSS signal is still
available. The monocular GPR started to predict dur-
ing the third minute. This experiment is shown in the
third row of Figure 8.
An airborne dataset is used to assess the proposed sys-
tem performance. The experimental results demonstrate
the ability of smart hybrid VAINS to enhance the average
3D RMS positioning errors to 61.5% and 32.4% of monoc-
ular VO/INS integration and monocular VO/INS integra-
tion augmented with the INS drift predictor, respectively,
during the GNSS signal outage even with a short learning
period. In addition, the experiments illustrate the ability
of Smart Hybrid VAINS to reduce the effect of the prob-
lems associated with VAINS in Mostafa et al.1
