Thermal–Inertial Localization for Autonomous Navigation of Aerial
Robots through Obscurants
Christos Papachristos, Frank Mascarich, and Kostas Alexis
Abstract— In this paper the problem of autonomous nav-
igation of aerial robots through obscurants is considered. As
visible spectrum cameras and most LiDAR technologies provide
degraded data in such conditions, the problem of localization
is approached through the fusion of thermal camera data with
inertial sensor cues. In particular, a long–wave infrared camera
is employed and combined with an inertial measurement unit.
The sensor intrinsic and extrinsic parameters are appropriately
calibrated, and an Extended Kalman Filter framework that uses
direct photometric feedback is employed in order to achieve
robust odometry estimation. This framework is capable of
accomplishing real–time localization while navigating though
obscurants in GPS–denied conditions. Subsequently, an exper-
imental study of autonomous aerial robotic navigation within
a smoke–ﬁlled machine-shop environment was conducted. The
presented results demonstrate the ability of the proposed
solution to ensure reliable navigation in such extreme visually–
degraded conditions.
I. INTRODUCTION
Aerial robots have been widely used in a variety of
applications, including those of infrastructure inspection [1–
6], precision agriculture [7–9], search and rescue [10–13]
and more. Relying on the recent advancements in research
and technology, autonomous aerial systems are now capa-
ble of operating in GPS–denied environments and provide
useful information, as well as real–time map reconstruction.
However, despite this rapid progress, GPS–denied Degraded
Visual Environments (DVEs) represent a hard limit for
autonomous systems as the sensor information inside them
can be severely degraded. Although in some types of DVEs,
e.g., darkness, the problem can be resolved using onboard
illumination or [14] active sensors such as LiDARs, more
complex cases including fog– and smoke–ﬁlled environments
remain particularly hard. In such environments, traditional
cameras or LiDARs used for Simultaneous Localization
And Mapping (SLAM) provide ill–conditioned information
therefore not allowing to reliably estimate the pose of the
robot and build the map of its surroundings.
Towards enabling aerial robotic autonomy in a broader set
of DVEs, including fog/smoke–ﬁlled environments, this work
examines the problem of utilizing Infrared (IR) vision that
can penetrate, to a certain extent, such degraded conditions
and facilitate the means for GPS–denied localization based
on the fusion of thermal camera data and cues from an Iner-

application potential of such a solution.

II. RELATED WORK
The problem of navigation through obscurants such as
smoke and fog has been widely discussed in the ﬁeld of
manned aerospace. Solutions to assist helicopter pilots have
focused among others on the fusion of LiDAR, RADAR and
camera. However, in the ﬁeld of small robotic systems, where
integration of RADAR is also particularly difﬁcult, little
work has been conducted with regard to the methods that
can enable navigation autonomy in GPS–denied DVEs and
especially through obscurants. The works in [15, 16] describe
results for hand–held monocular SLAM and visual odometry
using a thermal camera. The contribution in [17] discusses
the issue of selective combination of visual and thermal
imaging for localization in conditions that include those of
smoke, but on one hand it focuses on ground vehicles and on
the other hand inertial data are not fused in a tight manner
within the localization process, but rather a posteriori in order
to recover the scale. Similarly, the work in [18] overviews a
multispectral approach that includes thermal vision to assist
the localization task of a team of ground robots. The authors
in [19] present a method for combining appearance and
thermal information (RGB, Infrared) for SLAM. On a non–
robotic application, the paper in [20] discusses the use of
stereo thermal vision to recover the trajectory for smoke
diver applications. Furthermore, work has been presented
in regards to the problem of mutual thermal camera cali-
bration [21]. As indicated, despite these signiﬁcant efforts,
the robust approach of camera–inertial localization has not
been widely studied or developed, especially for the case of
autonomous aerial robots.
III. PROPOSED APPROACH
The proposed approach emphasizes on the ability of
aerial robots to autonomously navigate GPS–denied degraded
visual environments with strong presence of obscurants such
as smoke and fog. To enable this capability it proposes the
fusion of LWIR thermal camera data with time–synchronized
IMU cues. This selection was based on validation of the fact
that solutions such as LiDAR– or visible spectrum camera–
based solutions can provide ill–conditioned information in
such environments. Normal camera images present a white–
out effect due to the scattering of light through obscurants,
while LiDAR data become unreliable and the derived point
clouds particularly sparsiﬁed and noisy. On the contrary, in
this work we demonstrate how an appropriately calibrated
and fused thermal–inertial localization architecture performs
robustly even in environments with very dense levels of
obscurants (fog/smoke).
A. Perception through Obscurants
A 3D model view of the developed robotic solution is
depicted in Figure 2. The aerial robot corresponds to a widely
adopted quadrotor platform (DJI Matrice 100) which has
been extended with a multi–modal perception solution and
extensive onboard computational processing capabilities.
Fig. 2.
Visualization of the developed robot indicating the integration of a)
Long–Wave IR (LWIR) thermal camera, b) visible spectrum (ROYGBIV)
cameras, as well as c) an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU). A high–level
processor, namely an Intel NUC7i7BNH is utilized for the purposes of
GPS–denied localization through obscurants based on the fusion of thermal
camera data and IMU, alongside the handling of position control tasks.
More speciﬁcally, the multi–modal perception solution
consists of a FLIR Tau2 LWIR camera, a stereo visible light
camera pair based on Point Grey Chameleon3 cameras, and
a VectorNav VN–100 IMU. The cameras of the visible–
light stereo rig are hardware synchronized with the use
of custom–made electronics, however the data from these
cameras are not employed for the purposes of localization
in the experiments presented in this work, but are only
used for sparse stereo depth estimation, as it was shown
that their data are inadequate to perform reliable pose
estimation. The data streams from the FLIR Tau2 LWIR
thermal camera and the VN–100 IMU are synchronized via
the SYNC policy functionalities of the Robot Operating
System (ROS). This perception solution is interfaced by a
powerful Intel NUC7i7BNH, which is responsible to run
the proposed thermal–inertial odometry pipeline alongside
position control. For the purposes of position control, a
Linear Model Predictive Control strategy has been deployed
following on the path of previous work of the authors [22].
This control law derives reference roll, pitch, yaw and thrust
values which are then fed through the ROS middleware to
the low–level controller of the DJI Matrice 100 quadrotor
platform. The overall system weighs approximately 2.9kg.
B. IR Camera and Camera-to-IMU Calibration
Reliable operation of the proposed thermal–inertial local-
ization system relies on appropriate calibration of camera
intrinsics, as well as an accurate estimate of the extrin-
sic transformation between camera and IMU. Commonly,
camera calibration is performed with the use of a checker-
board [23] pattern and relevant optimization methods. A
variety of approaches has been proposed for the problem of
thermal camera intrinsic model calibration [21, 24]. In this
work we developed a specialized checkerboard that a) is built
on white-colored aluminum plate due to the high emissivity
of this material, b) at the place of black–colored rectangles
we have added a thin layer of black foam which also provides
thermal insulation from the aluminum surface, and c) at
the place of the white rectangles we leave the aluminum

C. Thermal–Inertial Localization
Given the calibrated thermal camera and camera-to-IMU
extrinscis, the proposed thermal–inertial localization scheme
is deployed. More speciﬁcally, the open–source Robust Vi-
sual Inertial Odometry (ROVIO) is utilized [26], a brief
summary of which is provided below. ROVIO closely cou-
ples the tracking of multilevel image patches with the
Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) through the direct use of
image intensity errors to derive the ﬁlter innovation term
and uses a QR–decomposition to reduce the dimensionality
of the error terms, therefore keeping the Kalman ﬁlter update
state computationally tractable. As ROVIO, and other similar
methods, depends on landmarks tracked on te basis of
the image intensity information within them, IR camera–
based operation is facilitated in environments with thermal
gradient (not perfect thermal equilibrium). The ROVIO ﬁlter
formulation is robocentric, which implies that the landmarks
are estimated with respect to the camera pose. Then the
landmarks estimated and tracked among consecutive frames
are decomposed into a bearing vector alongside a depth
parametrization. The IMU ﬁxed coordinate frame (B), the
camera ﬁxed frame (V) and the inertial frame W are
considered, and the employed state vector with dimension

information.
IV. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION
In order to evaluate and verify the ability of the proposed
thermal–inertial navigation solution to enable autonomous
ﬂight in degraded visual environments with signiﬁcant pres-
ence of obscurants, a test in a smoke–ﬁlled machine shop
was conducted. The experimental area was ﬁlled with very
dense smoke using a Hurricane 1800 Flex glycerin-based
fog–machine. The smoke density level was such that it
could be veriﬁed from the intense white–out effect in the
normal camera images that light scattering was very strong.
In such a challenging environment, the robot was tasked
to perform an automated trajectory, demonstrate its ability
for GPS–denied autonomous ﬂight through obscurants such

V. CONCLUSIONS
The problem of thermal–inertial localization and au-
tonomous navigation of aerial robots through obscurants
was considered in this paper. A technological and algo-
rithmic solution was developed which only relied on a
lightweight system consisting of a single thermal camera and
an IMU. The sensor intrinsics and extrinsics were calibrated
