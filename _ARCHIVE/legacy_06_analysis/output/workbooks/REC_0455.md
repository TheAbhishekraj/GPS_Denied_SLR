Abstract—We propose a location algorithm to promptly 
search for crashed unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), using an 
airborne communication relay UAV (ACRU) in global 
positioning system (GPS)-denied environments. Conventional 
UAV based location methods require at least four UAVs to 
search for the crashed one. However, such methods are subject 
to significant errors according to the distance between the 
crashed UAV and the others. In the proposed algorithm, only 
one ACRU is required to locate the crashed UAV. The ACRU 
relays its own position and the signals from the crashed UAV in 
real-time to a ground control station (GCS), which then 
estimates the position of the crashed UAV based on time 
difference of arrival (TDOA) processing using the signals 
relayed from the ACRU. According to the estimated position of 
the crashed UAV, the ACRU then flies in the direction of the 
crashed UAV by applying a weighting algorithm to effectively 
estimate the distance to it. The performance of the proposed 
algorithm is verified by computer simulations by considering 
the conditions of an army battalion battlefield. We improve the 
accuracy of the proposed location algorithm by applying a 
weighting algorithm to the TDOA data, and we demonstrate 
that the location algorithm can be used to efficiently locate 
crashed UAVs in GPS denied environments. 
Keywords— Airborne communication relay UAV, Multi-hop, 
Crashed UAV, Ground Control Station, Time difference of arrival, 
Location algorithm, GPS Denied Environments 
 
I. INTRODUCTION 
The unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) industry is growing 
rapidly, and UAVs are being used for various purposes in a 
wide range of fields. In battlefield environments, military 
UAVs play particularly important roles in surveillance, 
reconnaissance, supplying goods, deception operations, 
information sharing, and delivering precise attacks. because 
UAVs are low cost, they can be easily deployed to restricted 
areas, and communication coverage can be extended through 
UAV relay. Baek and Lim previously developed a prototype 
of a UAV-relay tactical datalink (TDL) for effective military 
operations [1]. 
Most unmanned weapon systems are equipped with a 
global positioning system (GPS) receiver, which is 
vulnerable to cyberattacks such as GPS jamming and 
spoofing. Owing to the long distance between GPS satellites 
and Earth’s surface, the received signal is very weak. Thus, 
GPS signals can be easily attacked by spoofing and jamming. 
If a UAV is subjected to cyberattacks, it can crash because of 
the loss of communication with the controller. Moreover, 
UAVs are likely to crash owing to such GPS attacks, physical 
crashes, adverse weather conditions, malfunctions, etc. In 
emergency situations, it is difficult for UAVs to 
autonomically 
make 
problem-solving 
decisions 
by 
themselves. If a UAV crashes during military operations, the 
reconnaissance information and specific data of the UAV 
may be retrieved by enemy forces, thereby critically 
jeopardizing future military operations by revealing the 
intentions of such operations. For example, an RQ-170 
sentinel was captured by Iranian forces in 2011. The Iranian 
state replicated the RQ-170 using reverse engineering and 
publicly displayed it in 2014 [2]. Furthermore, GPS signals 
should be guaranteed for Line of Sight (LoS). Otherwise, 
GPS information can be error-prone and unreliable during 
tactical operations in mountainous terrains owing to various 
factors such as signal refraction, scattering, diffraction, 
reflection, and path loss. Consequently, the GPS information 
from crashed UAVs is unreliable in tactical environments. 
Therefore, UAVs must overcome beyond-line-of-sight 
(BLoS) and none-line-of-sight (NLoS) situations in 
mountains to accurately transmit position information to the 
ground control station (GCS). 
Conventional way to locate the crashed UAV is using 
cameras [3]. However, cameras are extremely limited due to 
performance of lens and radiation angle. Particularly in the 
battlefield and mountain environments it is meaningless to 
use. Excluding visible way, there are currently two methods 
of detecting the position of a crashed UAV. The first is 
automatic dependent surveillance-broadcast (ADS-B), which 
is commonly used in commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) 
UAVs [4]. The other is beacon signals such as those used in 
the military [5]. Because both methods involve the use of 
GPS information, they are GPS-information dependent and 
therefore vulnerable to signal spoofing and interference on 
the battlefield. Furthermore, GPS-dependent UAVs must be 
equipped with an additional transmitter to transmit GPS 
information, and the additional mounting options may 
interrupt the continuous and efficient operation of the UAVs 
owing to size, weight, and power (SWaP) issues. 
To replace global navigation satellite systems (GNSSs) 
when local jamming attacks occur on the battlefield, a method 
of locating UAVs was previously proposed wherein a 
receiver detects the position of a downed or damaged UAV 
using a small number of reference UAVs, which know the 
position of the former [6]. In 3D environments, at least four 
UAVs are required to locate target UAVs. To estimate the 
position of a target UAV, the time-difference-of-arrival 
(TDOA) algorithm must be performed. However, because of

II. RELATED WORK 
A. ADS-B System 
The ADS-B system is a technology originally proposed 
by the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) to 
prevent collisions due to ever-increasing aircraft and UAV 
traffic. The system was devised to take control of the entire 
airspace. ADS-B transmitters operate at 1,090 MHz in the 
radio frequency spectrum to continuously broadcast the flight 
information such as the altitude, speed, and position of 
aircrafts in real-time every 0.5 to 1 sec. The broadcast signals 
are transmitted to the GCS and to other aircrafts possessing 
an ADS-B receiver. The GCS identifies the incoming signals 
and shares the corresponding flight information with other 
aircrafts in the area to prevent aircraft collisions. 
Although there are no crash codes in ADS-B messages, 
we can assume that a UAV has crashed based on ADS-B 
emergency code messages when the position of the UAV 
remains the same for a long time and a fuel shortage message 
is transmitted. At this point, the position of the UAV is 
determined based on the GPS information. Therefore, on the 
battlefield, ADS-B systems are GPS-information dependent, 
so they are vulnerable to signal spoofing and radio 
interference by GPS attacks. In addition, ADS-B transmitters 
must be attached to the UAV platform. 
B. Emergency Radio Beacon System 
The beacon signal is an emergency signal that operates at 
406 MHz when a UAV is in distress or crashes. UAVs must 
be able to detect obstacles on the ground without any people 
onboard to control them. Beacon signals identify whether a 
UAV has made a safe or emergency landing. Therefore, a 
signal processor is installed before the UAV is operated. 
However, mounting the processor burdens the UAV with 
SWaP issues. 
Because the beacon signal is also GPS-information 
dependent, it is difficult to detect the position of the UAV in 
battlefield environments. Because GPS signals are unstable 
and are often unavailable owing to BLoS and NLoS issues, 
the GPS signal must propagate reliably when the UAV 
crashes into mountainous terrain. 
C. UAV-based Localization 
When local jamming attacks occur on the battlefield, the 
ADS-B receiver detects the position of the target UAV using 
several reference nodes (i.e., other UAVs). When the GNSS 
signal is unstable or weak in the battlefield environment, the 
receiver rapidly locates the target UAV by deploying other 
UAVs in the area. 
UAV-based localization algorithms require at least 4 
reference signals to accurately locate the target UAV. To 
estimate the position of the target UAV, the received signals 
must be processed using the TDOA algorithm. Frequently 
estimating the location of the target UAV reduces the 
operational capability of the other UAVs. The estimated 
location of the target UAV is affected by the positions of the 
other UAVs and by the time synchronization between them 
because the accuracy of the estimated position of the target 
UAV mainly relies on the geometric deployment of the other 
UAVs, precise time synchronization, and the capabilities of 
the TDOA processors. As a result, accurately estimating the 
positions of the target UAV and the other UAVs is very 
inefficient in a battlefield environment. 
 
III. PROPOSED ALGORITHM 
Operating a UAV involves a GCS directly controlling all 
the functions of the UAV while the UAV flies around and 
communicates with the GCS in real-time. The UAV 
essentially receives from the GCS the control and non-
payload communication (CNPC) link to control its flight path 
and datalinks to share situational awareness. 
A. System Model 
When a UAV crashes, it transmits signals from the same 
position at fixed intervals. If one ACRU flies to different 
positions and receives the signals from the crashed UAV, it 
obtains that multiple ACRUs will be deployed to the area. For 
example, if the crashed UAV transmits 1 signal/s, it takes 
about 125 s for the ACRU to circulate at 180 km/h for a 
turning radius of 1 km. During this time, the ACRU will have 
received 125 signals from the crashed UAV. Therefore, we 
operate one ACRU. To improve the accuracy of the 
measurements, the distance between the ACRU and the 
crashed UAV significantly affects the accuracy of the 
position measurements. Thus, we applied a weighting 
algorithm to the location data obtained from the ACRU near 
the crashed UAV.

To demonstrate the validity and analyze the performance 
of the proposed algorithm, we simulated the battlefield 
environment of an infantry battalion unit by computer. The 
battlefield was simulated in MATLAB® under the following 
conditions. First, the ACRU was equipped with a transceiver 
and navigation systems to transmit its own position and the 
signals received from the crashed UAV to the GCS in real-
time. Second, the crashed UAV periodically transmitted a 
datalink after the crash, assuming that the datalink transmitter 
was not destroyed. Lastly, the ACRU was based on the RQ-1 
platform currently used by the U.S. army. In the simulation, 
we considered a multipath error for the signal transmissions. 
We used the satellite navigation (SatNav) toolbox. The 
SatNav toolbox generates multipath errors at zero-elevation 
angle by a linear autoregressive model. The measurements 
were repeated 10,000 times to estimate the mean distances 
between the crashed UAV and the flying RQ-1. 
As depicted in Fig. 4, the simulation area was a 10 × 10

Figure 6 illustrates the RMSEs obtained by applying the 
proposed algorithm when the distance between the RQ-1 and 
the crashed UAV was 1 or 2 km. According to Fig. 6, we 
applied a flight altitude of 1 km for the RQ-1. The turning 
radius of the RQ-1 was also 1 km. The x-axis represents flight 
time. The RQ-1 takes about 120 s to fly in a complete 1-km-
radius circle and return to its original coordinates. Starting at 
the point the farthest from the crashed UAV, the two RQ-1s 
flew over the GCS, which then estimated the location of the 
crashed UAV. Then, both RQ-1s flew to waypoints within 1 
and 2 km of the distance between the RQ-1 and the estimated 
location of the crashed UAV after which the RQ-1s flew in 
circular paths and the GCS modified the estimated position of 
the crashed UAV. 
The experimental results demonstrated that the RMSE of 
the RQ-1 whose TDOA was weighted was lower than that of 
the RQ-1 whose TDOA was non-weighted. Because the 
position estimation was significantly affected by the distance 
between the RQ-1 and the crashed UAV, the estimated 
position of the RQ-1 whose TDOA was weighted was much 
more accurate near the crashed UAV than was that of the RQ-
1 whose TDOA was non-weighted. The positions of the 
crashed UAV and the RQ-1 whose TDOA was weighted 
converged 70 s later. The RMSEs converged to 3 and 5 m 
when the distances between the estimated position of the 
crashed UAV and the RQ-1 whose TDOA was weighted were 
1 and 2 km, respectively. The RMSEs of the RQ-1 whose 
TDOA was non-weighted, on the other hand, converged to 3 
and 5 m 90 s later. Consequently, the RMSE of the RQ-1 
whose TDOA was non-weighted converged about 20 s later. 
Thus, the RQ-1 whose TDOA was weighted could rapidly 
detect the position of the crashed UAV and could estimate the 
position of the crashed UAV much more accurately than could 
the RQ-1 whose TDOA was non-weighted. 
V. CONCLUSION 
We propose an algorithm to detect crashed UAVs in 
tactical environments where GPS signals are unreliable. When 
a UAV crashes and periodically transmits signals, the GCS 
uses an ACRU to search for the crashed UAV. In the proposed 
algorithm, the ACRU relays signals using an internal 
communication system involving 2-hop relay communication 
wherein the ACRU relays the signals received from the 
crashed UAV to the GCS. By estimating the position of the 
crashed UAV from the GCS, the SWaP issues of the UAV can 
be overcome. The ACRU eliminates the need for additional 
mounted equipment. 
Conventional positioning requires several references and 
time synchronization. In the proposed algorithm, when the 
crashed UAV periodically transmits signals from a fixed 
position, only one ACRU flies and receives signals from 
different positions. Therefore, we do not require a few UAVs 
to locate the crashed UAV. In applying the proposed 
algorithm, the GCS estimates the position of the crashed UAV 
by calculating the transmission signals without using any GPS 
information. The simulations showed that the RMSEs of the 
RQ-1 whose TDOAs were weighted were lower than those of 
the RQ-1 whose TDOAs were non-weighted. The RMSEs of 
the RQ-1 whose TDOAs were weighted converged to 3 m 
when the estimated distance between the crashed UAV and 
the RQ-1 was 1 km. In addition, the RQ-1 whose TDOAs were 
weighted saved 20 s on the amount of time required to locate 
the crashed UAV. Future work will focus on locating the 
crashed UAV much rapid and more accurate within the 
designated time.
