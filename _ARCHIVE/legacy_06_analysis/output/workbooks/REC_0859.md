A Multimode Visual-Inertial Navigation Method for Fixed-wing Aircraft Approach 
and Landing in GPS-denied and Low Visibility Environments  
Xiaogang Song, School of Computer Science & Engineering, Xi’an University of Technology, Xian, China
Lei Zhang+, School of Computer Science and Technology, Northwestern Polytechnical University, Xian, China 
Zhengjun Zhai, School of Computer Science and Technology, Northwestern Polytechnical University, Xian, China 
Guanfeng Yu, AVIC Xi’an Aeronautics Computing Technique Research Institute, Xi'an, China 
 
Abstract—The landing safety is still one of the greatest 
challenges for fixed-wing aircraft, meanwhile not all airport 
runways are equipped with instrument landing system (ILS), 
and global positioning system (GPS) is not always available. It 
is necessary for aircraft to autonomously acquire high 
accuracy and stable navigation data during approach and 
landing in case of GPS loss and ILS unavailability. In this 
paper, we propose a multimode visual-inertial navigation 
method that combines the inertial measurement unit (IMU), 
the forward-looking infrared (FLIR) camera and the 
barometer into unscented kalman filter (UKF) adaptively 
according to flight height. In order to obtain accurate motion 
estimation, different sensing measurements are used at 
different flight phase. This proposed method run on three 
significant stages: 1) initial approach (flight height: 1000-200 
feet), runway azimuth information and inertia data are fused 
to estimate aircraft motion; 2) precision approach (flight 
height: 200-100 feet), sparse runway features extracted from 
the FLIR images, airport geo-information and inertia data are 
integrated to obtain precise motion estimation; 3) precision 
landing (flight height: 100-0 feet), visual simultaneous 
localization and mapping (VSLAM)and inertial measurements 
are used to estimate motion states. A general aircraft is 
elaborately equipped with a short-wave infrared (SWIR) 
camera, an IMU, a barometer and a flight data recorder to 
collect real flight data for algorithm verification. Finally, the 
experimental results demonstrate that the proposed approach 
can be used for fixed-wing aircraft accurate approach and 
landing in low visibility and GPS-denied environments 
robustly. Furthermore, its accuracy has been reached to the 
level of Inertial/Differential GPS integration.  
Keywords - Visual-inertial navigation; Approach and landing; 
Fixed-wing aircraft; GPS-denied; Low visibility  
I. 
 INTRODUCTION  
The landing is the most accident-prone phase for both 
military and civil aircraft since it is a high maneuver process 
to exhaust the large amount of kinetic energy of the airplane 
in a narrow and long airspace. Fixed-wing aircraft usually 
descend smoothly at a constant angle, pointing in the 
direction of the runway centerline, and touch down at the 
beginning of the runway. The flight accidents of aircraft 
landing stage account for more than 70% of the total flight 
period accidents worldwide, of which 30% of all fatal 
accidents are categorized as Controlled Flight Into Terrain 
(CFIT) [1] due to the incorrect guidance information. If low 
visibility conditions (e.g. fog or haze) are encountered, the 
pilots have no choice but to manipulate the aircraft to land 
using navigation instruments. If the conventional radio 
navigation systems are disturbed or disabled, they can 
mislead the pilots and cause a CFIT accident. In addition, 
most airports are equipped with simple and coarse radio 
beacons rather than expensive and precise ground-based 
guidance systems. Nowadays neither avionics systems, nor 
airport infrastructures are perfectly designed to support 
precision landing. Faced with these challenges, an 
autonomous, accurate and affordable landing navigation 
mechanism is extremely necessary for most fixed-wing 
aircrafts. In this paper, an adaptive multimode visual-inertial 
navigation method is proposed to estimate motion for fixed-
wing aircraft approach and landing in GPS-denied and low 
visibility conditions.  
II. 
RELATED WORK  
As a traditional navigation mechanism, the instrument 
landing system (ILS) can meet the landing of most aircraft I 
and II classes, however ILS with high cost and constant 
maintenance is not suitable for the large number of general 
airports. One of the most commonly used methods is the 
INS/GPS integrated navigation, but GPS signals are 
vulnerable to jamming or disable. In recent years, many 
visual pose estimation methods for fixed wing aircraft landing 
have been proposed, which can be divided into two 
categories, the ground-based methods [2,3] and the airborne 
visual aided methods [4-9]. The former often utilizes cameras 
located on the ground to detect, track and positioning the 
aircraft, which has high accuracy but high cost. The latter 
fully utilizes the airborne sensors and visual navigation 
algorithms to achieve autonomous navigation. Gui et al. [4] 
propose an airborne vision-based navigation approach for 
UAV accuracy landing based on artificial markers. This 
method needs to install a visible light camera integrated with 
a DSP processor on the UAV and place four infrared lamps 
on the runway. Cai et al. [5] adopt SR-UKF to fuse 
homography and inertial measurements to implement the 
UAV auto landing navigation. Fan et al. [6] employ spectral 
residual saliency map to detect region of interest, then select

aircraft approach and landing. To our knowledge, it is the 
only full-stage autonomous vision-aided landing navigation 
method that runs in three working modes in turn according to 
landing height above threshold elevation (THRE). This is in 
contrast to single vision observation such as [7], which revert 
to multiple measurements for state estimation. In the initial 
approach mode, runway azimuth, barometric pressure and 
inertial data are fused to position aircraft. In the precision 
approach mode, runway features, airport geo-information and 
inertia data are integrated into the filter. In the precision 
landing mode, visual SLAM and inertial measurements are 
used to estimate pose. To accommodate low visibility 
scenarios, a SWIR camera is installed to capture the FLIR 
image. Finally, the real flight data verify that the method is 
accurate and robust. 
III. 
SYSTEM SETUP  
A. Approach and Landing  
In general, a landing procedure of fixed-wing manned 
aircraft are divided into two parts: instrument flight segment 
(IFS) and vision flight segment (VFS). The Decision Altitude 
(DA = 200 feet (ft)) above the Threshold Elevation (THRE) is 
a watershed. Before descending to DA, the crew rely on 
cockpit instrument indications, such as ILS, to maneuver the 
aircraft. When approaching DA, the pilots look for the 
approach lighting system that can help them align the aircraft 
with the runway and provide position and distance remaining 
information. At 100 feet above THRE, the visual transition 
point, the pilot decides to continue the approach or pull up the 
aircraft at once by visual references.  
Compared with fixed-wing manned aircrafts, fixed-wing 
unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) rely on autonomous landing 
navigation without manual work. In order to further refine the 
navigation requirements of fixed-wing UAV landing, this 
procedure can be divided into three substages according to 
flight altitude, namely initial approach, precision approach 
and precision landing, as shown in Figure 1. Initial approach 
is defined as the sub-stage in which an aircraft descends from 
1000 ft to 200 ft above THRE, needs accurate position data to 
approach airport runway along the glide slide. Precision 
approach refers to the sub-stage in which an aircraft glides 
from 200 ft to 100 ft above THRE at a deceleration speed and 
requires precision pose to align with the runway and judge 
whether the runway meets the landing conditions. Landing 
from 100 ft to THRE is the final sub-stage, precision landing, 
during which the aircraft needs more accurate pose 
parameters to adjust its attitude and be flattened before 
touching the ground.

B. Method Framework   
Under the conditions of GPS-denied and low visibility, the 
available sensors in each sub-stage are different. Inertial 
measurement unit (IMU) is not constrained by environmental 
conditions and has the advantages of autonomous work and 
high instantaneous accuracy, but its errors will accumulate 
over time. As primary navigation instrument, it needs 
complement with other sensors, such as barometer, radio 
meter and camera. Firstly, the accuracy of barometric data is 
not high intrinsically, especially at low altitude. However, it 
can be used as a powerful supplement to the altitude 
measurement. Secondly, radio altimeter is often limited by 
measuring range, and is only used at low altitude. Thirdly, the 
vision cues extracted from the image is greatly influenced by 
the scale of the scene. As the aircraft descends, the more 
texture belongs to the image, then the more vision 
information can be extracted. To meet the navigation 
requirements of each landing sub-stage, it is necessary to 
combine different sensors and design a reasonable filtering 
framework. The proposed integrated navigation algorithm 
consists of three work modes according to the required 
navigation performance of each sub-stage. In the initial 
approach, the aircraft is far from the airport runway, and the 
runway contour in the image is small. The center coordinates 
of the runway ROI can be used as the runway azimuth. In the 
precise approach, the runway details in the image is clear and 
complete, and the detected runway edge lines and vanishing 
point can be used as vision cues. In the precision landing, the 
aircraft is close to the ground, then the scale is significantly 
smaller. The accurate visual positioning information can be 
obtained by VSLAM. The foundation of the proposed 
approach is the SR-UKF that fuse inertial data with high 
accurate vision observations and runway geographic data. All 
input images are captured and synchronized with the IMU 
according to unified time stamp. In the SR-UKF, the 
processing model is derived by inertial data. As shown in 
Figure 2, the part in blue box is the core of this algorithm 
framework.

VISION OBSERVATIONS

of the image is gradually reduced and the runway area is 
gradually visible. As the texture in the FLIR image becomes 
dense from sparse, the visual information in the FLIR image 
becomes more abundant. In order to maximize the accuracy 
of visual features, the methods that can be used should also 
be changed, rather than a fixed way. Therefore, for a 
specific sub-stage, the corresponding processing method is 
used to extract visual information as accurately as possible. 
In this paper, vision observations of the three sub-stages are 
runway azimuth, runway edges and camera pose extracted 
by three different ways respectively.

B. Runway Detection 
Visible images have high spatial resolution and rich texture 
details, but these images can be easily influenced by severe 
conditions, such as poor illumination, fog, and other effects of 
bad weather. Visible images capture reflected light, whereas 
infrared images capture thermal radiation. In general, infrared 
images are resistant to these disturbances. In the present 
paper, we adopt the SWIR camera to capture FLIR images 
with important airfield features in low visibility. However, 
infrared images typically have defects of low resolution and 
poor texture [16]. Existing runway detection algorithms [17–
19] cannot satisfy the requirements of robustness and 
accuracy in airborne extract runway features from FLIR 
images accurately and robustly. Improvements have been 
made on the basis of our recently proposed method [20]. 
This paper improves a hierarchical approach from coarse to 
fine to accurate detect and extract runway features from the 
FLIR image. In the coarse layer, YOLO (You Only Look 
Once) algorithm [21,22] is trained to detect range of interest 
(ROI) of the runway. Due to its special geometry, line 
segments give a high-level description of airport runway. In 
the fine layer, the line segments are extracted from ROI by 
EDlines 
detector 
[23] 
and 
are 
classified 
into 
the

Some random points are selected from the line segment by its 
weight. Then these random points in each line set are fitted 
into a complete runway line by RANSAC. Finally, three 
vertexes of the runway triangle contour can be calculated by 
the right edge, the left edge and the front edge of the runway. 
1) ROI Detection 
In this paper, the YOLO is used to detect ROI of the airport 
runway, which is a target detection system based on a single 
neural network. The detection process is: (1) an input image 
is divided into 7×7 networks; (2) give each network a 
prediction of two borders; (3) remove the less possible 
window according to the 7×7× 2 target windows predicted in 
the last step and use the Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) 
to remove the redundant windows. 
2) Line Segments Extraction 
An ideal line segment detection algorithm could process 
any images regardless of its origin, orientation or size, and 
produce robust and accurate line segments in real-time 
without parameters tuning. Among existing algorithms, Line 
Segments Detector (LSD) [24] and EDLines Detector can 
satisfy 
the 
above-mentioned 
requirements. 
However, 
EDLines runs up to 11 times faster than LSD, which makes it 
more suitable for real-time runway detection.

C. Nonlinear Filtering 
As a typical nonlinear filtering algorithm, the unscented 
Kalman filter (UKF) uses a finite number of sigma points to 
capture high-order system characteristics, leading to the high 
filtering precision and good convergence. However, it need 
calculate square root of state covariance matrix during sigma 
points at each time update, it may cause negative definite 
state covariance matrix. Nevertheless, the SR-UKF [25] 
adopts a Cholesky factorization of state covariance matrix in 
propagation directly to guarantee positive semi-definiteness 
of the state covariances. Considering the flight safety of 
fixed-wing aircraft, the SR-UKF is suitable to integrate 
inertial data and vision measurements.

C. Discussion 
The reasonable vision observations, accuracy features 
extraction and multimode visual-inertial fusion method are 
essential for precision motion estimation. Experimental 
results have supported such notion. Based on the 
experimental results, we have obtained the following key 
observations:  1) Because of employing a SWIR camera, the 
proposed method can capture the FLIR image clearly under 
low visibility conditions and extract visual features from it. 
Meanwhile, IMU can measure accelerations and rotational 
velocities at high update. The two type of sensors can be 
integrated well in a filtering framework. 2) The pose accuracy 
of the proposed method is not only superior to INS/GPS 
integration, but also comparable to INS/DGPS integration. 
Ultimately, the results are derived from accurate runway 
detection, reasonable features selection and efficient visual-
inertial fusion. 3) Compared with the conventional integrated 
navigation methods, our method has higher pose accuracy. 
Due to poor measurement accuracy of GPS, the traditional 
navigation methods are inferior to our proposed method.  
VII. CONCLUSION 
In this paper, a novel visual-inertial navigation for the fixed-
wing aircraft approach and landing in GPS-denied and low-
visibility environments is proposed. Three reasonable vision 
observations are designed to meet required navigation 
performances. Through SR-UKF fusion of visual information 
and inertial data, the random error of inertial sensors can be 
eliminated and the accuracy of pose estimation can be 
improved. Finally, the validity of the algorithm is verified by 
real flight data.
