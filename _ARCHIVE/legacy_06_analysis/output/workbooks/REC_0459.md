Safe Tightly-Constrained UAV Swarming in
GNSS-denied Environments
Andriy Dmytruk, Tiago Nascimento, Afzal Ahmad, Tom´aˇs B´aˇca, and Martin Saska,
Abstract—A decentralized algorithm for ﬂocking of Un-
manned Aerial Vehicles (UAV) in environments with high
obstacle density is proposed in this work. The method combines
a local planning loop with bio-inspired swarming rules for
navigating a compact UAV ﬂock in a real workspace without
relying on external infrastructures, such as motion capture
system and GNSS. The group stability and coherence are
achieved by employing a purposely designed onboard UVDAR
system for mutual localization of teammates in local proximity
of each UAV. The required robustness and scalability of the
multi-UAV system are therefore achieved without any need
for communication among the swarm particle. Such minimal
sensory and communication requirements have allowed the
system to become a backup technique for centralized multi-
robot systems in case of communication and GNSS dropout. The
proposed approach has been veriﬁed in numerous simulations
and real experiments inside a forest that represents one of
the most challenging environments for deployment of compact
groups of aerial vehicles.
Index Terms—Swarm Robotics, Aerial Systems: Applications,
Distributed Robot Systems, tightly-constrained boids algorithm,
UAV ﬂocking algorithm.
I. INTRODUCTION
The application of UAV teams in numerous tasks involving
exploration, search and rescue, monitoring, transportation,
etc., have recently been discussed in the literature. Multi-
UAV systems usually require efﬁcient algorithms for multi-
UAV path ﬁnding and following in order to solve complex
tasks cooperatively [1], [2]. In some cases, due to limited
communication capabilities or processing capability, central-
ized models are not applicable, and a distributed algorithm
is preferable for controlling the UAV group [3], [4]. One
way to solve this problem is inspired by biological groups of
animals, e.g. ﬂocks of birds or schools of ﬁsh. In 1987, Craig
Reynolds [5] proposed a set of rules to avoid collisions in
a multi-agent system, while keeping a single structure. The
advantage of this approach is its simplicity and scalability.
One good example of applying this approach is the work of
Chen et al. [6] in which the authors extended the boids model
for simulating the aggregate movement of ﬁsh schools in a
complex environment. However, many issues are raised when
applying this decentralized approach in real-world situations.
Let us take UAVs as a case study for this work, since
multi-rotor UAV motion capabilities are similar to birds and
ﬁsh. In comparison with ideal numerical simulations, multi-
rotor UAVs have their movement limited by physical rotor
constraints, the measurements are noisy, and the system
is under-actuated. Additionally, safety features need to be

Some works focusing on decentralized nature-inspired
UAV swarming can be found in the literature. In Braga et
al. [8], the authors developed a boids algorithm for UAVs
while adding a force called migration, which incrementally
moves the swarm towards a destination point. Park et al.
[9] incorporate a path into the boids algorithm and also
emphasize the collision force as crucial for survival in
comparison with mission forces, such as path following
and group convergence. Their idea was to ignore all other
forces when an obstacle is near. However, the original boids
algorithm and its slight modiﬁcations face the problem of
not being able to work efﬁciently in narrow spaces between
obstacles, which is the workspace where small multi-rotor
UAVs are particularly well-suited in comparison with ﬁxed-
wing UAVs, for example. The weight parameterization is also
often over-sized (e.g. when an obstacle group is added up).
These problems are increased in GNSS-denied environments
such as forests (see Fig. 1), ofﬁces, and mines where a
precise GNSS cannot be used and where light can inﬂuence
observation. Additional conditions on forces can provide
better safety, but will also constrain the motion of the UAV
swarm in the environment.
Decentralized boids-based algorithms are also used in UAV
ﬂocking to optimize network connectivity. Leu and Tang [10]
proposed a nature-inspired approach to survivable networks.
They use an online real-time Genetic Algorithm to opti-
mize the movements of a UAV swarm towards maintaining
communication between the ground agents by modeling the

group even more, which can be a problem in a forest-like

movement of the UAV is constrained only by the physical

In this work, we propose a safe, tightly-constrained boids-
inspired approach for UAV swarms ﬂying in a GNSS-denied
environment with high density of obstacles such as forests.
Our main contribution is the design of a decentralized swarm-
ing algorithm to guarantee a safe and rapid response for each
UAV within the group without any communication channel
and global localization available. Furthermore, the proposed
approach is designed to satisfy constraints such as robot
deployment in real demanding environments (e.g. forests),
and the limitations of onboard sensors. It is known that most
works on swarm intelligence still do not perform real robot
experiments in GNSS-denied environments [14]. In the work
presented here, we use only UVDAR [15], a distance and
bearing estimator based on ultra-violet light developed within
our team’s previous work. The UVDAR sensor is inspired
by the perception organs of animals moving in ﬂocks, and
therefore ﬁts together with requirements on decentralized,
scalable swarming using only local sensory information. The
proposed approach is robust to partial occlusion, since the
UAV needs to have visual contact with at least one UAV
to have a cohesive swarm. Our approach combines local
path planning with reactive forces in a strictly decentralized
manner and provides a way to put an upper bound on each of
the forces, while also gradually increasing the forces based
on required actions. These contributions enable the swarm
to be more stable and more predictable, allowing the UAVs
to move towards the target even in harsh environments with
numerous obstacles.

II. UAV LOCALIZATION AND MAPPING
The platforms used in this work consist of several ROS-
based interconnected subsystems, as depicted in Fig. 2. The
Mission & Navigation block embeds the proposed approach
described in sections II and III, and supplies the time-
parametrized sequence of desired velocities. Let us ﬁrst
describe the localization of each UAV in the group, which is
extremely important, and then describe the proposed swarm-
ing rules.
A. UVDAR

error of position estimation by GNSS in the order of meters,
depending on the amount of obstacles on the GNSS signal
in the workspace. Communication of the robot’s position
between UAVs within the ﬂock also suffers drawbacks due to
communication delays and intermittent communication inter-
ruptions. Furthermore, GNSS-denied environments present a
major problem for currently-developed UAV swarms. In an

B. Path-Planning in Obstacle-Filled Environments
Environments such as forests present several problems
besides the impossibility of using a GNSS sensor. One of
the problems is the existence of many obstacles that a UAV
must avoid. For a compact group (or swarm) of UAVs, the
existence of a large number of obstacles presents an even
greater problem to be solved, specially due to the well-
known problems of local minima and movement oscillations
blocking swarm movement [16].
In our approach, we detect all the obstacles within a
desired distance zone odmax (to calculate the obstacle force),
and we use a sub-optimal planned path for each UAV
(to calculate the navigation force) in order to avoid these
problems. To obtain the obstacle information, the UAVs use
a 2D RPLIDAR, which is then processed by a hector slam
algorithm [17]. The UAVs end up creating their own obstacle
map, represented by a square occupancy grid, containing 3
possible values at each position corresponding to: obstructed
space, free space, or unknown area (in cases where the area
was not explored). The resulting local map is then used in a
path-planning module for each UAV independently. Both the
map and the path are recalculated, at each control loop.

III. SAFE TIGHTLY-CONSTRAINED UAV SWARMING
In this work, we solve the task of moving a group of UAVs
in a compact distributed manner towards a common target
in a GNSS-denied obstacle-ﬁlled environment. The core of
this approach is local reactive swarming, which combines
local path planning within the Mission & Navigation block.
The UAV swarm should maintain a compact formation while
avoiding obstacles, with no communication among team-
mates available.
Let us ﬁrst name the UAV that we are analyzing as the
focal UAV. In order to accomplish the goal, we propose
a distributed algorithm that is based on four boid-like in-
teraction forces that act in the focal UAV. The forces are
obstacle collision avoidance, UAV collision avoidance, UAV
cohesion force, and the navigation force being triggered by
the local planning. The magnitudes of the forces are modeled
using linear functions of the distance to the targeted object.
However, in our approach each force is constrained with
starting and ending zones of inﬂuence.

Aerial swarming imposes special requirements on control
reference generation. Swarming approaches often require
classical tracking of the desired altitude and heading, but the
horizontal motion may be dictated by the desired velocity
or by the desired acceleration. Thus, our system uses a
tracking approach called Velocity tracker, which enables the
UAV to track a desirable reference velocity. This tracker is
based on our previous work [18], where the desired velocities
are processed and are converted into a feasible, smooth,
and evenly-sampled full-state control reference. Then, this
information is passed to the Model Predictive Controller
(MPC) for position control, which uses only the speciﬁed
portion of the control reference to calculate the feedback.
