Abstract
To address the problem of lacking effective evaluation benchmarks for UAV navigation
algorithms in complex-structured, GNSS-denied industrial environments (e.g., fully en-
closed stockyards), this paper proposes and open-sources a multi-modal UAV navigation
dataset. The dataset is collected in a real steel plant enclosed stockyard, integrating LiDAR
point clouds, IMU, RGB images, and high-precision total station ground truth trajectories,
and specially designs ArUco markers to aid visual localization. Different from existing
datasets targeting urban or campus scenes, this dataset realistically reflects the challenges
of GNSS-denied signal, weak texture, high dust, and complex spatial grid structures in

1. Introduction
In recent years, the application of unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) systems has expe-
rienced rapid development [1,2]. UAV systems are becoming increasingly miniaturized
and civilian-oriented. With the trend toward intelligent UAV systems, their application
domains continue to expand, including indoor scenarios such as underground parking
lots, railway stations, and factory workshops [3,4]. Particularly in industrial settings, as
concepts like safe production, smart manufacturing, and energy efficiency gain promi-
nence, there is growing demand for UAV systems in applications such as mine inspection
systems, inventory in thermal power plant dry coal sheds, routine inspection systems for
power plant turbine halls, and anti-corrosion coating inspection systems for high-speed
railway platform truss structures. These industrial applications require UAVs to operate in
increasingly complex environments.
The navigation system is the most critical component for UAV operations in industrial
environments. Unlike ground vehicles or ships that primarily move in two-dimensional
planes, UAVs perform three-dimensional spatial movements, making them more reliant on
navigation system accuracy. Navigation systems directly impact UAV operational safety,
precision, and stability. Sensors in navigation systems are particularly vulnerable to in-
dustrial environmental factors. These environments are typically characterized by GNSS
denial and complex features including disordered high-intensity lighting, carrier black-
body effects, large open spaces, and dust/smoke interference. Such conditions can cause
significant sensor challenges such as acquisition noise, signal loss, and carrier scattering,
creating bottlenecks for UAV integration into industrial intelligence.
Numerous scholars have researched navigation systems in GNSS-denied environ-
ments. Common approaches include vision-based methods such as MSCKF [5], loosely cou-
pled RDVO [6], and tightly coupled systems like ROVIO [7], OKVIS [8], ORB-SLAM2 [9],
VINS-Mono [10], VINS-Fusion [11], ORB3-SLAM [12], and VI-ORB [13]. LiDAR-based
solutions include the Cartographer series [14–16], LOAM [17], and its variants like LeGO-
LOAM [18], LIO-SAM [19], LIV-SAM [20], FAST-LIO [21], F-LOAM [22], LIOM [23], and
T-LOAM [24]. However, these algorithms were not designed for industrial scenarios.
Evaluations typically rely on open datasets like KITTI [25] and NCLT [26], which record
campus, park, or urban environments [27–31]. These datasets feature regular structures
that facilitate easy extraction of planar/corner features, clear visual conditions without
auxiliary markers, and ground-based data collection with differential GNSS ground truth.
Industrial environments present distinct challenges: heterogeneous spatial grid struc-
tures that are difficult to replicate in existing datasets; complex low-light conditions requir-
ing auxiliary visual markers that are not commonly available in current datasets; lack of
flat surfaces for ground vehicles, necessitating true UAV operational states; and complete
GNSS denial, which complicates reliable ground truth acquisition. Although algorithms
like LOAM [17] and Traj-LO [32] perform well on traditional datasets, they often encounter

significant difficulties when applied to industrial heterogeneous scenarios. Current datasets
may not be sufficient to fully validate navigation algorithms in authentic industrial settings.
Recent efforts address specific degraded scenarios like underground mines [33],
tunnels [34,35], caves [36,37], and bridges [38]. Emerging datasets like WHU [39] and
ENWIDE [40], along with competitions like DARPA [41], aim to enhance navigation ro-

configuration and environmental heterogeneity.
Existing datasets cannot adequately evaluate navigation algorithms in common indus-
trial environments. Therefore, creating specialized datasets for heterogeneous scenarios is
crucial. Such datasets would overcome current limitations in environmental representation,
accelerate navigation technology development for practical industrial applications, address
critical challenges in GNSS-denied structural environments, and promote UAV adoption in
industrial intelligence. This foundational work will support the development and evalua-
tion of navigation algorithms while accelerating UAV deployment in industrial sectors.
2. System Overview
This dataset aims to extend LiDAR-based navigation systems to complex-structured,
GNSS-denied industrial environments. Therefore, widely adopted hardware models were
selected to ensure more researchers can utilize this dataset for developing navigation
algorithms tailored to industrial environments, thereby facilitating greater integration of
novel technologies into industrial applications.
2.1. Sensor Setup
The dataset employs multiple sensors to capture different types of data: a mechanically
rotating LiDAR collects environmental point clouds, inertial measurement units capture
platform kinematic data, a gimbal camera acquires images, and a total station scanner
measures ground truth for the navigation system.
The point cloud data is collected using the Puck VLP-16 mechanically rotating LiDAR
developed by Velodyne (San Jose, CA, USA). The Puck VLP-16 is a widely adopted LiDAR
model extensively used by researchers for navigation algorithm development, which is
shown in Figure 1a.
Platform kinematic data is collected using the MTi-300 AHRS inertial measurement
unit developed by Xsens (Enschede, The Netherlands). The MTi-300 AHRS is the company’s
highest-performance inertial unit, capable of providing 9-axis inertial data measurements,
which is shown in Figure 1b.
Image data in the dataset is collected using the Zenmuse H20 gimbal camera produced
by DJI (Shenzhen, China). Due to the dim and complex lighting conditions in GNSS-denied
space grid scenarios, traditional forward-facing visual systems cannot be directly applied
for navigation. Therefore, a gimbal camera was selected for the dataset to observe auxiliary
facilities in the environment. In practice, the gimbal camera maintains a nadir view and
provides real-time feedback on gimbal angles, which is shown in Figure 1c.
Ground truth positional data for the navigation system is collected using the Nova
MS60 total station scanner produced by Leica (Wetzlar, Germany). This total station
scanner represents Leica’s fifth-generation measurement robot, featuring automated target
search and measurement capabilities. Once deployed within the GNSS-denied space grid
environment, it can autonomously track the navigation system and measure its global
position by using a reflector, which is shown in Figure 1d,e. During operation, over
a range of 0.9 m to 3500 m, the total station can continuously measure points with a
positional accuracy of 3 m + 1.5 ppm, or achieve a single-measurement positional accuracy

selected inner markers with patterns that contain more black pixels internally, so that at

3. Experimental Design
3.1. Test Site and Layout
The dataset was created at an enclosed iron ore powder stockyard of a large state-
owned steel plant in southern Hebei Province. This plant belongs to the largest steel
production group in southern Hebei and is involved in the strategically important steel
industry. Its production heavily relies on strategic resources such as coal, iron ore, and
rare metals. The enclosed stockyard at this plant site has the following characteristics: Its
foundation consists of cement retaining walls, with a shell-shaped roof supported by a
spatial grid structure, resulting in a complex physical layout; the interior has high dust
concentration at low altitude, dim internal lighting contrasting with bright surrounding
light, creating a complex lighting environment; large machinery such as bucket-wheel
stacker-reclaimers operate inside, leading to a complex electromagnetic environment; the
internal environment is not fully enclosed, resulting in complex airflow conditions. The
performance of the intelligent UAV system during autonomous operation in this enclosed
stockyard can accurately reflect the common practical conditions in industrial sectors like
strategic resource inventory.
This enclosed stockyard consists of two parallel-arched, ultra-large indoor stockpiles.
The dataset was created in the smaller one, which measures 500 m in length and 100 m
in width. The roof of this section is constructed with an arched spatial grid structure. A
conveyor belt platform runs through the center, and maintenance walkway platforms run
along the walls near both ends. The images of the dataset creation building are shown in
Figure 5. In Figure 5a, the lower half was the stockyard used for dataset collection.

3.3. Experimental Steps
Due to the illumination intensity within the dataset collection site being only 6∼9 lux,
below the threshold required for the UAV platform to utilize its own visual system for
navigation, the UAV platform could only maintain stable attitude control via the inner loop
and was unable to perform position control via the outer loop. Furthermore, since fully
autonomous flight systems require strict safety certification within the plant area, and in
compliance with site safety regulations, the dataset collection experiment was conducted
solely in manual control mode, with the UAV platform flying along a predefined path. The
main operational steps carried out on-site during the dataset collection experiment are
as follows:
Stp. 1 Lay artificial turf on the central platform of the enclosed stockyard and place the
takeoff/landing ArUco markers on it. Position the remaining ArUco markers at
their respective locations. Set up the total station scanner. Connect all hardware
components to the UAV platform, and establish the ground station and data link.
Stp. 2 Use a reflector to sequentially measure and record the coordinates of the four
corners of each ArUco marker using the single measurement mode of the total
station scanner.
Stp. 3 Power on the ground station and the UAV platform, and establish the data link
connection. Install the reflector onto the UAV platform.
Stp. 4 Place the UAV on the takeoff/landing platform. Point the camera gimbal downward
so that the image center aligns with the smallest ArUco located at the center of the
takeoff/landing platform.
Stp. 5 Sequentially activate the total station scanner to record the reflector’s position in
continuous measurement mode. From the ground station, control the onboard
computer to start the LiDAR and inertial measurement unit. Then, initiate the
recording program on the onboard computer to log LiDAR point clouds, inertial
measurement unit outputs, camera images, gimbal angles, and UAV attitude data.
Stp. 6 Manually control the UAV platform. Upon reaching the predetermined altitude, fly
back and forth along a “Z”-shaped flight path. During flight, adjust the spacing
between adjacent flight lines to maintain overlap in the down-looking camera images
while keeping the flight speed stable.
Stp. 7 After completing the flight path, land the UAV on the takeoff/landing platform.
Sequentially stop the recording program on the onboard computer and shut down
the total station scanner. Complete the current set of data collection tasks.
Stp. 8 Repeat Stp4 through Stp7 until all data collection tasks are completed.
Stp. 9 After completing all collection tasks, export the recorded data for subsequent processing.

5. Evaluation
Before the creation of this dataset, existing datasets were unable to provide quantita-
tive evaluation of navigation algorithms in common industrial scenarios such as complex-
structured, GNSS-denied industrial environments. For instance, in studies such as A
Global ArUco-Based Lidar Navigation System for UAV Navigation in GNSS-Denied
Environments [46], A Lidar-Inertial Navigation System for UAVs in GNSS-Denied En-
vironment with Spatial Grid Structures [47], and A Closed Stockyard UAV Intelligent
Inventory System [48], the navigation accuracy of UAV navigation systems could not be ad-
equately assessed. This was due to the lack of direct evaluation conditions in real industrial
settings and the absence of effective datasets, which resulted in the reliance on qualitative
methods and indirect metrics to demonstrate the advancement of navigation algorithms.
Therefore, the algorithms from these studies were applied to the dataset constructed in this
paper for evaluation, aiming to showcase the dataset’s comprehensiveness in characterizing
real industrial environments and its flexibility in adapting to various algorithms.
In the dataset evaluation, the method from Reference [47] was independently adopted
as one evaluation modality. Additionally, the LiDAR odometry factor from the same
paper [47] was combined with the sensor fusion frameworks from Reference [46] and
Reference [48], respectively, forming two other evaluation modalities. These three modal-
ities correspond to three algorithmic modes for dataset evaluation: without using any
ArUco markers, using all of the ArUco marker poses, and using a subset of the ArUco
marker poses.

6. Conclusions
To address the issue that existing datasets cannot complete the evaluation of navigation
algorithms operating in common industrial scenarios, this paper produces a LiDAR-based
multi-modal UAV navigation dataset for heterogeneous scenes. The evaluation of the
dataset in this paper shows that the dataset can complete accurate quantitative evalu-
ation of various navigation algorithms in complex-structured, GNSS-denied industrial
environments, breaking through the scene limitations of existing datasets, reducing the
evaluation cost of navigation algorithms in industrial scenarios. The dataset establishes
a new benchmark for navigation algorithms for heterogeneous structured GNSS-denied
scenarios, fostering further research and innovation in intelligent navigation algorithm for
industrial applications, and advancing the field.
