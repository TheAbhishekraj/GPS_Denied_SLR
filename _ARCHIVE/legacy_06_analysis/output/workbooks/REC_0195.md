Low-Altitude UAVs Based on Image Recognition in 
GNSS-Denied Environments

Abstract—The article proposes a five-stage autonomous 
continuous positioning method that integrates inertial 
navigation and image recognition to address the prob-lem of 
accumulated positioning errors in unmanned aerial vehicles 
(UAV) in satellite-denied environments. This method first 
used inertial navigation (INS) for initial positioning to capture 
satellite image basemaps, and achieved aerial image 
preprocessing through heading angle correction, scale 
registration, and maximum inscribed rectangle cropping. 
Then, this study combined a U-Net semantic segmentation 
model to extract building areas, and innovatively adopted a 
two-stage matching strategy of template matching and 
SuperPoint & SuperGlue feature point matching to effectively 
over-come the interference of seasonal and lighting changes 
on matching. Finally, the height data was integrated into 
output three-dimensional coordinates. The experiment was 
based on the UAV-VisLOC dataset, and showed that two-
stage matching can reduce the average plane positioning error 
from 26.32m in single-stage matching to 12.95m, a decrease of 
49.2%. The maximum error decreased from 68.74m to 
18.67m. The proposed framework significantly improves the 
robustness and accuracy of positioning in complex 
environments, providing reliable technical support for urban 
low-altitude operations, emergency rescue, and other 
scenarios. 
Keywords- GNSS-denied environment, UAV visual location, 
Dual-stage matching, Template-feature fusion 
I.
INTRODUCTION
With the rapid advancement of unmanned aerial vehicle 
(UAV) technology, UAVs are being increasingly deployed 
across various fields such as logistics, agriculture, surveying 
and mapping, and emergency rescue. Driven by the growing 
“low-altitude economy,” market demand for UAVs has 
surged. 
Furthermore, 
accelerated 
urbanization 
has 
highlighted the important role of UAVs in the development 
of smart cities—including urban traffic monitoring, 
emergency management, and environmental monitoring. 
Autonomous positioning and navigation technologies are 
essential for ensuring the safe and efficient operation of 
UAVs. Among these, the Global Navigation Satellite 
System (GNSS) plays a critical role. Under ideal conditions, 
GNSS can provide high-precision position, velocity, and 
timing information anywhere at any time. Currently, the 
widely used GNSS Standard Positioning Service (SPS) 
achieves meter-level accuracy [1], while high-precision 
techniques such as PPP, DGNSS, and RTK can provide 
decimeter- or even centimeter-level positioning [2–4]. 
However, GNSS signals are susceptible to weather 
conditions and environmental interference. In rainy 
weather, densely forested areas, or high-rise urban canyons, 
these signals experience multipath effects or are completely 
blocked, severely com-promising the continuity and 
reliability of onboard navigation systems. Under such 
conditions, 
UAV 
positioning 
accuracy 
degrades 
significantly, 
jeopardizing 
operational 
safety 
and 
effectiveness. Hence, the development of autonomous 
positioning technologies capable of operating in GNSS-
denied environments is imperative to ensure stable and 
dependable UAV performance in complex scenarios. 
The Inertial Navigation System (INS), which is based 
on Inertial Measurement Units (IMU), enables fully 
autonomous navigation and positioning without relying on 
external information. When integrated with GNSS, it 
enhances positioning continuity and reliability, making 
GNSS/INS integration one of the most widely adopted 
solutions. MEMS-based Strapdown Inertial Navigation 
Systems (SINS) eliminate the need for complex mechanical 
structures, offering advantages such as compact size, light 
weight, low cost, maintainability, and high reliability [5]. 
Although GNSS/INS integration delivers satisfactory 
performance over the short term, the positioning errors of 
INS accumulate rapidly in GNSS-denied environments, 
substantially reducing navigation accuracy and mission 
effectiveness. 
Integrated navigation systems that supplement INS with 
external observations can be broadly categorized into two 
types: wireless positioning and vision-assisted navi-gation. 
With the advancement of smart devices and communication 
technologies, 
wireless 
positioning—such 
as 
Ultra-
Wideband (UWB) [6], Wi-Fi [7], Bluetooth [8], LTE [9], 
and Dedicated Short-Range Communications (DSRC) 
[10]—has been widely applied in pedestrian and vehicle 
navigation. Commonly used techniques include Time of 
Arrival (TOA) [11], Angle of Arrival (AOA) [12], and 
RSSI-based ranging [13]. However, wireless positioning is 
susceptible to environmental noise, multipath effects, non-
line-of-sight (NLOS) conditions, and imperfect signal 
propagation models, all of which can lead to degraded 
accuracy. 
In contrast, vision–inertial integrated navigation offers 
several significant ad-vantages: 
1. Enhanced autonomy: Vision systems utilize cameras
to capture environmental features (e.g., landmarks and 
structures), enabling reliable operation in GNSS-denied or 
degraded environments such as indoor spaces, urban 
canyons, and tunnels. They operate using only visible light 
without relying on external signals. 
2. Real-time environmental perception: Vision systems
detect and interpret dynamic surroundings (e.g., vehicles 
and pedestrians), ensuring continuous and stable positioning 
while also supporting complex mission-oriented tasks.

II. 
RELATED WORK 
A. Vision-based Navigation System 
In GNSS-denied environments, UAVs increasingly rely 
on visual sensors, such as cameras, for accurate pose 
estimation 
to 
enable 
autonomous 
navigation 
and 
positioning. Vision-based navigation methods can be 
broadly divided into two categories: 
1. Visual Odometry (VO): This technique estimates 
incremental motion between consecutive image frames 
without relying on prior maps. 
2. Image-matching-based Localization: This approach 
uses pre-existing maps (e.g., satellite imagery) and matches 
them against real-time captured images to determine 
position. 
Visual Odometry(VO) employs compact, low-cost 
visual sensors to estimate the UAV’s local pose in real time. 
Based on the methodology for establishing correspondences 
between images, VO algorithms can be classified into three 
types: 
1. Feature-based methods (e.g., ORB-SLAM [14]): 
These methods extract and match distinctive features (e.g., 
ORB descriptors) and optimize camera motion by 
minimizing reprojection errors. ORB-SLAM [15] improves 
computational efficiency through multi-threaded tracking, 
relocalization, and loop closure. 
2. Direct methods (e.g., DSO [16], VINS-Mono [17]): 
These techniques use pixel intensities directly and minimize 
photometric error to estimate motion. 
3. Hybrid methods (e.g., SVO [18]): These combine 
feature extraction (e.g., FAST corner detection) with direct 
inter-frame tracking. 
Most VO systems adopt a keyframe-based strategy and 
utilize multi-threading to track motion trajectories, thereby 
balancing real-time performance with positioning accuracy. 
However, VO is often subject to accumulated drift over 
extended operation and lacks a global geographic reference, 
limiting its use in large-scale aerial applications. Therefore, 
integrating VO with complementary sensing technologies is 
essential to overcome these limitations. 
B. Visual-inertial Integrated Navigation 
Due to the inherent limitations of vision-only systems 
during extended operations in GNSS-denied environments, 
Visual-Inertial Navigation Systems (VINS) have be-come a 
core technology in autonomous navigation for drones, self-
driving vehicles, and robots. VINS integrates data from 
visual sensors (e.g., cameras) and IMUs to compensate for 
the mutual limitations of each sensor, thereby achieving 
more accurate and robust positioning and navigation. Visual 
sensors provide rich environmental information are 
susceptible to illumination changes, dynamic objects, and 
rapid motion. In contrast, IMUs deliver high-frequency 
attitude and 
acceleration 
measurements, effectively 
compensating for the shortcomings of visual sensors during 
short-term fast movements. Therefore, the fusion of both 
modalities enables higher-precision positioning. 
The sampling frequency of an IMU is typically higher 
than that of a camera. To synchronize IMU measurements 
with image frame timestamps, Foster et al. [19] developed 
a mathematical formulation for IMU pre-integration on the 
manifold space. This technique has been widely adopted in 
subsequent VINS research, such as the monocular visual-
inertial fusion algorithm VINS-Mono [20]. The frontend of 
VINS-Mono tracks features using optical flow and employs 
a tightly coupled approach that combines IMU pre-
integration with visual observations through nonlinear 
optimization for pose estimation and mapping. Extending 
VINS-Mono, the team at the Hong Kong University of 
Science and Technology later released VINS-Fusion [21], 
which supports multi-sensor integration, including stereo 
cameras and GPS. 
Despite the effectiveness of Visual-Inertial Odometry 
(VIO) in enhancing navigation accuracy through multi-
sensor fusion, it still faces several inherent limitations: 
1. Cumulative Drift: Since both INS and VO inherently 
estimate motion in a relative manner, VIO remains 
susceptible to the accumulation of errors over extended 
periods of operation in GNSS-denied environments. This 
issue is particularly pronounced under conditions involving 
significant illumination variations or in feature-deficient 
scenarios. 
2. Lack of Absolute Positioning: VIO primarily depends 
on relative motion estimation and does not incorporate 
global references. As a result, it is unable to pro-vide 
accurate absolute geographic positioning, which limits its 
applicability in long-duration flights or large-area 
operations where global consistency is essential. 
C. Image-matching-based Positioning 
In recent years, advancements in UAV flight technology 
and the increasing commercial availability of drones have 
led to the extensive application of UAV aerial imagery in 
various domains, including target detection and tracking. 
Such imagery can also be accurately matched with satellite 
reference images to support continuous drone positioning. 
Moreover, with ongoing progress in image matching 
techniques, sophisticated matching networks can now 
effectively handle the significant appear-ance variations 
between satellite and aerial images caused by seasonal and 
lighting changes. As a result, image matching-based 
geolocation has become increasingly feasible, allowing 
effective correspondence establishment between aerial and 
satellite images to determine a UAV’s geographic location 
accurately. 
Several methods have been proposed for image 
matching and positioning in GNSS-denied environments.

A 
major 
drawback 
of 
image 
matching-based 
geolocation is its high computational cost, which often leads 
to significant latency. This makes it difficult to meet the 
real-time positioning requirements of UAV applications. To 
improve positioning performance, some studies have 
combined visual navigation with image matching. Kinnari 
et al. [24] corrected UAV images using Visual-Inertial 
Odometry (VIO) and planarity assumptions to adapt them 
for downward-facing cameras. They used orthorectified 
images for satellite matching and integrated the results with 
VIO pose estimates within a particle filter. Zhang et al. [25] 
proposed an iterative trajectory fusion method based on a 
scaled pose graph, combining SLAM estimates with image 
matching results. 
These studies generally treat VO and image matching as 
relatively independent modules and fuse their outputs at the 
trajectory level. As a result, image matching corrections are 
not directly incorporated into the VO estimation process, 
which may limit the overall accuracy and robustness of the 
system. 
Furthermore, when Visual Odometry (VO) is applied in 
high-altitude UAV scenarios, the small scale of captured 
objects makes it difficult to accurately estimate camera pose 
based on subtle feature motion. In such cases, integrating 
image matching-based geolocation with an Inertial 
Navigation System (INS) offers notable ad-vantages: image 
matching provides absolute position updates, while INS 
supplies high-frequency relative motion information, 
making the two highly complementary. 
Therefore, this paper adopts a tightly coupled 
methodology combining INS and image matching to reduce 
the impact of environmental variations on matching 
performance. This leads to a novel five-stage positioning 
architecture with INS-based initial positioning, aerial image 
preprocessing, semantic segmentation, template and 
feature-point two-stage matching, and 3D fusion. The 
proposed pipeline aims to enhance both positioning 
accuracy and robustness in challenging environments. 
III. 
APPROACH 
A. Overview 
The proposed five-stage architecture including INS 
Initial Positioning, Aerial Image Preprocessing, Semantic 
Segmentation, Template and Feature Point Two-Stage 
Matching, and 3D Fusion is as following: 
1. Dynamic Image Capture Guided by Inertial 
Navigation: The INS mechanization output provides initial 
latitude and longitude coordinates, which are used to 
retrieve a 1 km × 1 km satellite reference image centered at 
this location from a large-scale satellite imagery database. 
2. Aerial Image Preprocessing: Based on camera 
parameters (focal length and sensor size), flight heading, 
and altitude, the captured aerial image is rotated, cropped, 
and scaled. This ensures that its scale (i.e., ground distance 
per pixel) and perspective align with the satellite reference 
imagery, with horizontal and vertical directions corrected to 
true east–west and north–south orientations, respectively. 
Flight altitude is determined by integrating barometric 
altimeter readings with ground elevation data from digital 
terrain models. 
3. Training of the Image Recognition Model: A building 
extraction model is trained using the Inria Aerial Image 
Labeling Dataset under the U-Net semantic segmentation 
framework. 
4. Model Application and Template Matching: The 
trained U-Net model (which outputs a binary mask: white 
for buildings, black for background) is applied to both the 
preprocessed UAV image and the satellite reference image. 
Template matching is then performed between the two 
building masks. Given that the preprocessed aeri-al image 
has lower resolution than the satellite image, the latter 
serves as the tem-plate. 
5. Fine Matching: The region corresponding to the 
template matching result is extracted from the satellite 
image. Fine-grained matching is then conducted between 
this region and the aerial image using the deep learning-
based SuperPoint and Su-perGlue framework for feature 
extraction and correspondence estimation. Using the 
matched feature points and a planar geometric assumption, 
a refined two-dimensional latitude–longitude coordinate is 
computed. 
6. 3D Coordinate Acquisition: The two-stage matching 
process yields a precise 2D coordinate. This result is 
combined with the UAV’s altitude information to pro-duce 
accurate 3D world coordinates.

C. Template Matching and Fine Matchong 
After preprocessing the aerial images and extracting 
building regions through semantic segmentation, the next 
step involves performing template matching between the 
recognized building maps from both the aerial and satellite 
images. This approach offers the advantage of significantly 
reducing the impact of seasonal variations, illumination 
changes, environmental conditions, and dynamic objects 
on the matching results. 
Template matching is a technique used to locate the 
region in a target image that most closely resembles a given 
template (a smaller image). It works by sliding the template 
over the target image and computing a similarity measure 
at each position. The best match is identified as the location 
with the highest similarity score.

is often limited, leading to potential misidentification and 
significant deviations from the true location. To enhance 
localization accuracy, this study supplements the coarse 
template matching results with pixel-level correspondence 
estimation and feature point matching. 
SuperPoint is a self-supervised framework for keypoint 
detection and descriptor generation [26]. It extracts 
distinctive feature points and their corresponding 
descriptors from images using a pre-trained model without 
requiring manual annotation. SuperGlue is a deep learning-
based feature matcher that employs graph neural networks 
(GNNs) to significantly improve matching accuracy by 
capturing both local appearance and geometric consistency 
[27]. 
The SuperPoint & SuperGlue framework is employed

A. Image Preprocessing 
U-Net features a distinctive U-shaped encoder-decoder 
architecture [28]. The encoder (left side, downsampling 
path) progressively extracts high-level semantic features via 
convolutional and pooling layers, while the decoder (right 
side, upsampling path) restores spatial resolution using 
transposed convolutions. This symmetric structure has been 
widely adopted in domains such as medical imaging and 
remote sensing. 
This study utilizes the Inria Aerial Image Labeling 
Dataset [29], which comprises aerial imagery from multiple 
cities worldwide at a resolution of 0.3 meters. The dataset 
includes 
360 
large-scale 
images, 
each 
measuring 
5000×5000 pixels, with pixel-level binary segmentation 
labels indicating building contours. 
 
 
Figure 7.  The dataset samples 
The dataset was partitioned into training, validation, 
and test subsets with a ratio of 7:2:1. Each 5000 × 5000 
large image is divided into 256 × 256 small images with 
repeated regions, and the repeated pixels of adjacent 
clipping blocks are 64. Dynamic data enhancement 
strategies are used in image translation, flipping, contrast 
change and other aspects to improve the amount of data. 
Then, the U-Net model was trained using the PyTorch 
framework, employing Dice Loss as the objective function. 
The batch size was set to 16, the initial learning rate was 
set to 0.0001, the learning rate optimizer was set to Adam, 
and the learning rate scheduling strategy was set to 
ReduceLROnPlateau: if the verification accuracy of three 
consecutive epochs is not improved, the learning rate will 
be reduced to half of the original, so as to train 50 epochs, 
and the data was randomly disordered in each epoch. 
Moreover, in the verification phase, the binarization 
threshold of the output result determination was set to 0.5.

The positioning experiments were conducted using the

of each UAV image, such as center-point latitude and 
longitude, capture altitude, acquisition date, and heading

longitude ranges) of the 11 satellite images. 
In this experiment, one specific scene (Taizhou-1) was 
selected for evaluation, comprising one satellite base map 
and over 700 UAV aerial images, used to validate the 
proposed positioning framework.

V. 
CONCLUSION 
This study proposes a five-stage positioning architecture 
by integrating INS initial positioning and aerial image 
matching. The main contributions to UAV navigation in 
GNSS-denied environments are as follows: 
1. A Dynamic Image Capture Mechanism Based on 
Inertial Navigation: By leveraging real-time inertial 
navigation outputs, the system dynamically retrieves 
satellite reference imagery, enabling continuous and 
adaptive positioning. 
2. An Innovative Image Matching Pipeline: The 
integration of semantic segmentation, template matching, 
and feature-based matching significantly mitigates the 
impact of environmental variations such as weather and 
seasonal changes, leading to more reliable and accurate 
positioning. 
3. A Lightweight and High-precision Continuous 
Positioning Framework: The proposed approach reduces the 
minimum planar positioning error to the meter level. 
Through the use of pre-trained models, matching time per 
image is constrained to under one second, supporting 
efficient and real-time continuous operation. 
However, the current implementation only applies 
inertial navigation to initial positioning. In future work, we 
plan to further integrate inertial navigation data into the 
entire calculation process to achieve more accurate and 
higher-frequency continuous positioning.
