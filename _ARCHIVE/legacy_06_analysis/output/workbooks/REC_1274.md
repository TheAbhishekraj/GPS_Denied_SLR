Abstract
Post-blast operations in tunnel construction represent a critical bottleneck due to manda-
tory downtime and hazardous environmental conditions. This study addresses these
challenges by developing and validating an integrated cyber–physical architecture that
coordinates an autonomous Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) and an Autonomous Wheel
Loader (AWL) under the supervision of a Digital Twin acting as central operational dig-
ital interface. Specifically, this technology was designed to access the tunnel, evaluate
post-blasting conditions, and initiate operations during mandatory exclusion periods for
personnel. The system was validated in a realistic, Global Navigation Satellite System
(GNSS)-denied tunnel environment emulating post-detonation visibility constraints. The
results demonstrate that the aerial agent successfully navigated and mapped the excavation
front in less than 8 min, establishing a shared coordinate system for the ground machinery.
Through this collaborative workflow, the autonomous deployment enabled operations to
commence 50% to 80% earlier than conventional manual procedures. Furthermore, the
system reduced daily operational time by approximately 8%, with an estimated return on
financial investment between one and seven months. Overall, the proposed framework

1. Introduction
Underground construction plays a pivotal role in the development of modern infras-
tructure, facilitating essential urban transportation networks and enabling the efficient
utilisation of subsurface space. This sector is currently undergoing a phase of rapid expan-
sion, driven by accelerating global urbanisation and the transition towards more resilient
infrastructure systems. Recent market analyses reflect this momentum, forecasting a com-
pound annual growth rate of approximately 6.8% for the global tunnel construction market
between 2023 and 2028 [1], with certain regional estimates projecting growth as high as
9% [1]. However, the execution of these subterranean works presents significant technical
and operational challenges. The environment is inherently hostile, characterised by the
absence of GNSS (Global Navigation Satellite System) signals, poor visibility, the presence
of dust, and the potential accumulation of harmful gases. These factors, combined with
severe communication constraints, critically affect navigation, sensing capabilities, and
mission autonomy [2,3].
Consequently, these conditions complicate planning and operations while significantly
elevating safety risks for the workforce. Comparative analyses between underground and
surface construction activities indicate that accident rates in subterranean works can be
considerably higher, in some instances more than double, due to restricted spatial configu-
rations, complex geological conditions, and limited evacuation options [4,5]. Within this
context, the post-blast phase, which encompasses cavity inspection, geological character-
isation, debris removal, and material transport, represents a persistent bottleneck in the
tunnelling cycle. Traditionally, this phase relies on a series of sequential manual operations
that must be executed by specialised personnel. However, these activities cannot commence
immediately, as they are contingent upon the completion of mandatory ventilation periods
to ensure atmospheric safety, often depending directly on on-site observations [6,7].
This dependency on human intervention introduces significant limitations, includ-
ing exposure to potential ground instability, oxygen deficiency, and airborne dust [4,8].
Furthermore, the requisite downtime for ventilation and the subsequent sequential exe-
cution of inspection and cleaning tasks results in substantial periods of inactivity. This
directly impacts the project’s efficiency by lengthening excavation cycles and reducing
overall productivity [9,10]. Compounding these operational inefficiencies is the difficulty of
obtaining reliable real-time data. Measurements in such environments are often qualitative
rather than quantitative, hindering accurate decision-making and delaying the planning
of subsequent project phases [11]. Moreover, the lack of integrated digital information
constrains interoperability with management platforms, impeding effective coordination
between on-site operations and control systems [12–14].
In response to these challenges, the automation of operations through advanced
robotic technologies and autonomous navigation is increasingly recognised as a decisive
step towards a safer and more resilient construction paradigm [15–17].
Over the last decade, significant advances have been made in specific domains. Un-
manned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) have been successfully deployed for tunnel inspection and

operational status together with tasks orchestration and decision support capabilities.
These systems are essential for enhancing situational awareness and traceability; however,
their integration with autonomous robotic platforms remains in an early stage, and the
orchestration of multi-agent operations in complex tunnel environments is still largely
unexplored [13,24–26].
Despite these individual advancements in autonomous mapping, robotics, and digital
supervision, a critical technological gap persists: the fragmentation of current solutions. Re-
cent literature has introduced promising concepts for UAV-UGV coordination; for instance,
exploring vision-based collaboration architectures for autonomous site preparation in sur-
face construction [27]. Additionally, significant theoretical advances have been proposed
regarding adaptive cooperative localization methods for heterogeneous robots operating in
satellite-denied environments [28–30]. However, these approaches largely remain theoreti-
cal or rely on external infrastructure, failing to address the specific complexities of heavy
machinery operating in realistic underground scenarios. Most existing developments focus
on isolated tasks, such as aerial inspection, debris removal, or monitoring, without provid-
ing a comprehensive framework that unifies these processes. To overcome these limitations,
this research, framed within the BEEYONDERS project [31], proposes a fully integrated
solution. Consequently, there is currently no existing approach that fully combines aerial
mapping, ground-based transport, and digital orchestration within a single operational
architecture suitable for GNSS-denied and low-visibility environments [15,32–34]. This lack
of integration prevents the deployment of fully autonomous systems capable of managing
the post-blast phase as a seamless and optimised process.
The present study aims to bridge this gap by demonstrating the feasibility and advan-
tages of an integrated robotic–digital approach for autonomous post-blast tunnel operations.
The proposed methodology validates the coordinated interaction between UAV and an
autonomous Wheel Loader (AWL), linking them to a Digital Twin environment to enable
data-driven decision-making. This architecture is designed to overcome the limitations of
fragmented systems by ensuring that perception, cooperative localisation, and decision
support function as a unified entity.
The main contribution of this work is the proposal and validation of a novel cyber-
physical architecture for heterogeneous robotic coordination in GNSS-denied environments.
Unlike previous studies that focus on isolated robotic tasks, this research formalizes a
reusable methodology where the Digital Twin acts not merely as a visualizer, but as an
active orchestrator closing the data loop between aerial perception and ground actuation.

2. Materials and Methods
The tunnel demonstrator implements an integrated cyber–physical architecture de-
signed to automate post-blast operations. This system coordinates a heterogeneous robotic
fleet, comprising an UAV for autonomous inspection and an AWL for material handling,
under the supervision of a cloud-edge DT.
Serving as the central intelligence layer, the DT moves beyond static geometric mod-
elling to create a dynamic ecosystem that merges sensing, actuation, and analytics [35–37].
Functionally, the architecture assigns three primary roles to the DT: (1) a supervisory layer
that coordinates the UAV, AWL, and human operators; (2) an integrative layer that fuses
heterogeneous datasets, including LiDAR point clouds and robotic telemetry, into a coher-
ent 3D environment; and (3) an analytical layer that applies data-driven models for key
performance indicator (KPI) computation and predictive performance evaluation [38].
To support these functions in a GNSS-denied environment, the system employs a
hybrid edge–cloud configuration. This structure guarantees low-latency responsiveness for
safety-critical operations while ensuring scalability for long-term data processing [39,40].
The resulting architecture facilitates a closed-loop data flow where 3D maps generated
by the UAV are processed and transmitted to the AWL to enable precise autonomous
navigation and debris removal.
2.1. UAV Subsystem
In the framework of this research, a custom multirotor platform has been specifically
developed to operate in confined tunnel environments. The design prioritizes a compact
and functional configuration, aiming to maximize performance and accessibility while
maintaining robustness. Specifically, after a thorough analysis of component distribution,
a quadrotor layout was selected to optimize the weight-to-space ratio; consequently, the
UAV can operate with high stability during exploration and surveillance missions in
hard-to-reach areas. Furthermore, the modular layout allows for precise adjustment of
the center of mass to enhance flight dynamics under variable payload configurations.
Regarding operational resilience in harsh tunnel environments, the platform features a
custom protective fairing specifically designed to shield internal electronics from dust and
small particles, alongside a cage system to mitigate risks during close-proximity flight. The
resulting aircraft configuration and its deployment in the test environment are illustrated

The software architecture, depicted in Figure 2, is modularized into two primary
subsystems executed on the onboard computer: GNSS-denied localization and reactive
navigation. In this context, a critical component for operating in post-blast conditions is
the PointCloud Dust Remover module. Since suspended particles can be misinterpreted as
obstacles, this module filters out noise before it reaches the mapping stack. To filter out
residual noisy points caused by dust clouds while preserving valid non-dust points, the
intensity threshold for clustering was determined through manual analysis of data collected
during multiple flight tests in degraded visibility. Additionally, a region of interest (ROI)
is defined around the sensor to discard near-field artifacts, as such noise was observed to
occur predominantly at specific distances from the sensor.

external infrastructure. Based on a prior analysis of state-of-the-art LiDAR-inertial odome-

control references in confined spaces. To overcome this limitation, the system employs

Finally, the navigation and guidance system coordinate three interconnected modules
to enable safe flight in unknown environments. The global planner utilizes a volumetric
exploration algorithm to generate optimal paths for autonomously mapping the unknown
space [44]. To ensure operational safety within the tunnel geometry, the maximum explo-
ration path length is strictly constrained to 8 m. Consequently, the UAV maintains a safe
distance from walls and obstacles while keeping the trajectory centered within the tunnel
cross-section. Furthermore, to reduce exploration downtime and sustain a consistent flight
speed, subsequent exploration paths are generated proactively before the UAV reaches the
end of the current segment. Real-time trajectory generation is handled by Fast-Planner [45],
which computes collision-free paths by integrating path-based optimization with topo-
logically calculated routes derived from the filtered LiDAR data. This allows the system
to adapt instantaneously to dynamic obstacles. In short, the guidance layer utilizes a
Finite-State Machine (FSM) to manage mission logic, coupled with a Cascade proportional–
integral–derivative controller (PID) that translates the local planner’s trajectory references
into precise actuator commands for the low-level autopilot [46].
2.2. AWL Subsystem
For the experimental validation, a commercial heavy-duty Wheel Loader utilizing a
drive-by-wire architecture served as the automation platform. This configuration allows
operational commands, including throttle, steering, and gear shifting, to be managed
via CAN bus, providing a robust base for the integration of autonomous capabilities
without requiring invasive mechanical modifications. Regarding perception, the platform
is equipped with a comprehensive multi-modal sensor suite specifically designed to operate
in the harsh, low-visibility conditions typical of post-blast tunnel environments. Specifically,
the system comprises four 3D LiDAR sensors and a multi-camera RGB system providing

particulate matter. To ensure precise positioning and safety, a dual-antenna Real-Time

Regarding the control framework, the software architecture depicted in Figure 4 is
built on ROS2 Humble and structured around a Behavior Tree (BT) framework that or-
chestrates high-level decision-making. In this context, the BT serves as the central control
logic dynamically triggering perception, navigation and control routines according to the
mission status. This modular approach facilitates flexible task sequencing and reliable mis-
sion execution, even under changing environmental conditions. Regarding mapping and
localization, the system employs LG-SLAM [47], a graph-based framework that seamlessly
integrates LiDAR, GNSS and IMU data. This approach is particularly advantageous as
it incorporates a robust loop-closure mechanism ensuring global consistency and long-
term map stability, even in large-scale or partially revisited environments. Moreover, to
maintain accurate localization in GNSS-denied areas such as tunnels, G-LOC [48] is used.
This framework maintains an active graph managed through a sliding-window approach;
consequently, it allows the system to coherently fuse prior map information and its uncer-
tainty while seamlessly handling transitions between outdoor (GNSS-available) and indoor
(GNSS-denied) environments.
Navigation capabilities rely on the NAV2 stack [49], which has been configured to
address the non-holonomic constraints of the articulated vehicle. Specifically, feasible
trajectories between navigation goals are computed by means of a Hybrid-A* global planner.
This planner extends the traditional grid-based approach by considering motion primitives
composed of straight and circular arc segments which comply with the robot’s kinematic
constraints, such as the minimum turning radius. Subsequently, a Time Elastic Band (TEB)
local planner [50] is employed to compute optimal control actions (linear velocity and
steering angle of the articulation joint). The optimization problem considers the trajectory
length, execution time, separation from obstacles and strict adherence to the vehicle’s
dynamic and geometric constraints. To ensure smooth obstacle avoidance, a dynamic
replanning node verifies the global path against the costmap in real-time; hence, if the
path is obstructed by a dynamic object or a new environmental feature, a new trajectory is
seamlessly computed and executed.

and propulsion systems. The longitudinal control module computes the desired acceler-

In relation to the autonomous operations, specifically the loading and unloading
maneuvers, the approach presented in this work is based on a deterministic state machine
architecture. In contrast to recent research relying on data-driven strategies such as deep

unreliable in dusty conditions. Regarding the unloading process, the machine relies on the

2.4. Experimental Setup and Validation Scenario
Herein, the experimental validation was conducted at the Santa Bárbara Foundation
(SBF) in León, Spain. This facility, specifically designed for subterranean research, features
a 500 m tunnel characterized by confined cross-sections, covering an area of approximately
50 m2, with an equivalent diameter that narrows from roughly 8 m at the entrance to
between 5 and 7 m in deeper sections, and the complete absence of GNSS signals (Figure 9).
Consequently, it provides a representative benchmark for evaluating robotic autonomy
under realistic denial-of-service conditions.
 
Figure 9. Overview of the use case location showing the exterior area and tunnel access in SBF.
To address the operational downtime associated with the mandatory post-blast ven-
tilation periods described in Section 1, the validation scenario was designed to simulate
the immediate aftermath of a detonation. Since ensuring experimental repeatability and
safety precluded the use of live explosives during the robotic trials, a controlled particulate
generation system was employed. Specifically, this equipment generated a dense aerosol
cloud capable of reducing optical visibility to critical levels, thereby effectively emulating
the suspended dust and toxic gases typical of a blast event. While the structural and light-
ing conditions of the tunnel remained identical across all trials conducted during the same
week, the density of the simulated smoke was intentionally varied to fine-tune and validate
the robustness of the perception algorithms under different levels of visibility degradation.
The setup and validation of this simulation system are depicted in Figures S4 and S5 of
the Supplementary Information, while the technical specifications of the equipment are
detailed in Section S6.
Regarding the supporting infrastructure, specific adaptations were required to coun-
teract the environmental hostility. Ideally, SLAM algorithms rely on natural features;
nevertheless, the tunnel walls at SBF are coated in soot from prior fire tests, exhibiting low
reflectivity that compromises standard LiDAR odometry. To overcome this limitation, a
sparse array of reflective markers was deployed along the initial section to provide stable
radiometric features for localization, as illustrated in Figure S6. Furthermore, to guarantee
continuous telemetry and video streaming, a temporary mesh network was distributed
along the tunnel (Figures S7 and S8). To prevent bandwidth degradation in the multi-hop
topology, the proximal nodes were interconnected via Ethernet backhaul and powered
by independent battery systems, the details of which are provided in Supplementary
Information (Section S7).

4. Discussion
4.1. Comparative Analysis and Lessons Learned
The experimental results obtained from the demonstrator represent a significant ad-
vance over current construction operations in post-blast tunnel excavation. While previous
works have largely focused on isolated subsystems, such as UAV-based inspection, ground
robotics or digital supervision [54,55], the proposed architecture successfully integrates
https://doi.org/10.3390/rs18081133

### [[page 19]]
Remote Sens. 2026, 18, 1133
19 of 25
these technological domains into a unified workflow. This integration addresses the opera-
tional bottleneck identified in the introduction by transforming the conventional sequential
approach, inspection followed by hauling, into a continuous and collaborative process. By
deploying the UAV immediately after the event to generate environmental data, the system
enables early robotic intervention without exposing personnel to hazardous conditions.
This capability extends recent advances in robotic exploration for underground environ-
ments [56] by introducing a cooperative framework where the aerial agent dynamically
informs the navigation of the heavy machinery.
From a methodological perspective, the experiment validates the role of the DT as
an active orchestrator rather than a passive monitoring tool. Unlike previous Digital
Twin applications in tunnelling primarily limited to visualization or post-operations [36],
the framework herein proposed integrates real-time telemetry and point-cloud data to
enable live supervision and task synchronization. Furthermore, the implementation of a
hybrid cloud–edge architecture with semantic ontologies ensured the scalability required
for real-time data exchange in complex construction scenarios.
Regarding the autonomous ground operations, the Finite State Machine (FSM) im-
plemented on the heavy machinery successfully reproduced expert operator behavior.
The system effectively leveraged pressure sensors and encoder feedback to manage pile
penetration and loading cycles, demonstrating robustness against the irregularity of the
terrain. Nevertheless, specific constraints were identified during the validation that limit
the scalability of the current setup. Regarding communication, the deployed linear mesh
topology implies that each additional wireless hop theoretically reduces the available
bandwidth by approximately half. Although proximal nodes were interconnected via
Ethernet backhaul to mitigate this progressive attenuation, the observed signal degradation
over long distances motivates the adoption of industrial-grade private networks such
as 5G in future deployments. Similarly, regarding UAV endurance, the aerial platform
completed the 8 min mission within the safety margins of a single battery cycle, which
has a theoretical maximum flight time of approximately 14 min. However, this figure can
vary in practice due to airflow conditions within the tunnel, such as ventilation currents,
which increase motor load. Consequently, scaling the system to longer tunnel distances
will require higher energy density batteries or intermediate recharging strategies, along
with adaptive return-to-home logic based on real-time energy consumption.
From a critical standpoint, the experimental validation strategy proved effective for
Technology Readiness Level (TRL) 6 evaluation, although specific simplifying assumptions
were required. It is acknowledged that the validation was conducted using artificial
aerosols. While this setup effectively replicates the visual occlusion, light scattering, and
attenuation effects critical for optical and LiDAR sensors, it does not simulate the high
thermal gradients or overpressure waves present in a live blast. In a real detonation,
extreme temperature variations can induce localized changes in the refractive index of the
air, potentially affecting laser propagation accuracy. Consequently, while the current fusion
algorithms successfully handle particulate occlusion, future pilot tests in active tunnels
will focus on validating the sensor housing resilience and data calibration stability against
these specific thermodynamic phenomena.

5. Conclusions
The current study reports on the validation of an integrated cyber–physical archi-
tecture for the automation of post-blast tunnel operations. The systematic experimental
characterization in a GNSS-denied environment confirmed that the coordination between
aerial and ground agents via a Digital Twin is a feasible strategy to overcome the current
bottleneck of manual inspections.
Regarding aerial performance, the UAV successfully executed autonomous explo-
ration under degraded visibility conditions. The system generated high-fidelity 3D maps in
less than 8 min, providing geometric precision comparable to total station measurements.
Consequently, the shared localization framework allowed the Autonomous Wheel Loader
(AWL) to navigate and perform mucking operations without human intervention, validat-
ing the robustness of the proposed sensor fusion strategy against environmental clutter. By
decoupling the inspection phase from physical human entry, the system ensures a higher
safety standard while maintaining data integrity.
From another point of view, the coordinated workflow demonstrated a clear impact
on efficiency. The autonomous deployment enabled the start of operations 50% to 80%
earlier than standard procedures, effectively converting mandatory ventilation downtime
into productive time. This efficiency gain translates to an estimated daily time reduction of
5% to 10%. Furthermore, the economic analysis suggests a return on investment between
one and seven months, contingent on the tunnel geometry. Moreover, the system ensured
the elimination of human exposure to instability and gas and particle risks during the
inspection phase.
Future developments should focus on enhancing communication robustness through
industrial-grade private networks, such as 5G, to guarantee connectivity throughout the
full tunnel length. Additionally, extending the Digital Twin framework to cover the entire
excavation cycle, including drilling and blasting design, would further consolidate the
utility of the system, aligning with efforts in smart tunnelling lifecycle management. Finally,
the implementation of distributed scheduling algorithms is required to enable simultaneous
multi-agent coordination, allowing for parallel mapping and loading operations in adjacent
sections to achieve full system autonomy.
In short, the proposed framework converts the fragmented excavation process into a
continuous data-driven workflow, paving the way for the digitalization of underground
construction.
