Leonardo Drone Contest 2021: Politecnico di Milano team architecture
Gabriele Roggi1, Salvatore Meraglia1, Marco Lovera1
Abstract— In this paper, the winning solution of the second
edition of the Leonardo Drone Contest (LDC), an autonomous
drone competition, is presented. The participating teams were
asked to design and build an autonomous system, capable of
accomplishing complex tasks in an indoor urban-like environ-
ment. To reach this goal, the designed system should be capable
of navigating in a Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS)-
denied environment with autonomous decision making, online
planning and collision avoidance capabilities. In this light, the
authors describe the competition, its rules and objectives, as
well as the proposed solution in terms of hardware, software
and algorithms.
I. INTRODUCTION
In recent years, the research on autonomous Unmanned
Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) has seen great interest. Autonomous
systems can play an important role in many applications,
e.g., search-and-rescue [1], inspection [2], surveillance [3]
and mapping [4]. In the robotics community, as in many
other scientific and technological domains, there has been
a significant growth in the number of challenge prizes
and competitions launched in recent years. The aim is to
stimulate innovation more effectively, to meet a defined
challenge, and to provide solutions to the problems that
matter to roboticists and society [5].
In particular, drone racing has seen autonomous systems
catching up fast with human performance. In this frame-
work, while the first drone racing competition has been
organized during the IROS conference in 2016 [6], the
most important event has been the 2019 Lockheed Martin
AlphaPilot challenge [7]. This challenge led to the first
season of Artificial Intelligence Robotic Racing (AIRR), co-
organized with the Drone Racing League (DRL) for human
pilots [8]. In the literature, some of the approaches taken
by the teams during this competition are available, e.g., [9],
[10] and [11]. Other than drone racing, autonomous UAVs
have been object of competitions in the MBZ International
Robotic Challenge (MBZIRC) [12]. In this context, a variety
of autonomous drone operations has been explored ranging
from wall building [13] to ground fires extinguishing [14].
With similar objectives, namely to encourage the de-
velopment of Artificial Intelligence (AI) applied to Un-
manned Aerial Systems (UAS), Leonardo S.p.A. designed
and launched the Leonardo Drone Contest (LDC) [15] in
collaboration with six Italian universities. The LDC is a
drone competition designed to push the capabilities and

II. LDC COMPETITION FORMAT
The aim of the Leonardo Drone Contest is to encourage
the creation of a national ecosystem that involves and brings
together the capabilities of large companies, universities,
small and medium enterprises, and national spin-offs and
start-ups. In particular, Leonardo is supporting the activities
of six PhD students (one per university) of the Politecnico di
Milano, Politecnico di Torino, Universit`a di Bologna, Scuola
Superiore Sant’Anna di Pisa, Universit`a degli Studi di Roma
Tor Vergata and Universit`a degli Studi Federico II di Napoli.
The students are engaged for three years, in the development
of an autonomous drone system, sharing university and busi-
ness resources and knowledge. The designed system should
be capable of navigating in a GNSS-denied environment with
autonomous decision making, online planning and collision
avoidance capabilities.
The second edition took place in Turin on 28-30 Septem-
ber 2021. The competition was held in the indoor urban-like
environment shown in Figure 1. The contest was composed

were moving randomly in unknown confined regions on the

the form of a string of 10 digits from 0 to 9. The i-th number

numbers were communicated to the team before starting
the round, were collaborative agents. The remaining one
was the intruder that carried the exact rewards associated
to each landing. The drone exploration was assisted by a
fixed pan–tilt–zoom (PTZ) camera placed in correspondence
of the cross icon in Figure 2, which could be controlled by
the teams. After having found the intruder robot, the drone
had to send a visible picture of the rewards to the team’s
ground control station. This action had to be executed within
30 minutes from the start of the round. At this point, the
sequence of landing spots to maximize the reward had to be
selected. This task could be either carried out autonomously
on the drone computing hardware or the sequence could be
chosen by the team based on their judgment. After having
determined the sequence, the drone had to reach and land on
top of the pads in the given order. The landing was consid-
ered valid if all the UAV contact points with the ground were
inside the 1 m×1 m square. The points corresponding to valid
landings were summed up for obtaining the round result. The
team obtaining the highest amount of points in the three (out
of four) best rounds was proclaimed the winner. It is worth
pointing out that, in each round, the UAV takeoff position
was represented by a different landing pad, communicated to
the teams before the start of the run. The reward string and
the ground robots’ region of motion varied across rounds.
III. SYSTEM OVERVIEW
Before introducing the system hardware, we define frames
and notations that are used throughout the paper. We indicate
the map frame with {M}, i.e., an East-North-Up (ENU)
fixed frame; {O} represents the odom frame, i.e., an ENU
frame which drifts over time with respect to {M}; {B}
is a Forward-Left-Up (FLU) body fixed frame; {C} is the
camera fixed frame and {T} is the ArUco marker fixed
frame. RA/B ∈SO(3) is the rotation matrix describing the
attitude of the {A} frame with respect to the {B} frame,
while pA/B ∈R3 is the position of the frame {A} with respect
to frame {B}, resolved in {B}.
The drone, specifically designed for the competition and
developed in collaboration with the Politecnico di Milano
spin-off company ANT-X1, is a coaxial octocopter (see
Figure 4). Its dimensions are 430×300×200 mm for a takeoff
weight of 2.85 kg. The drone is equipped with a Pixhawk 4
Flight Control Unit (FCU) and an NVIDIA Jetson Xavier
NX companion computer. The sensor suite is composed by
a forward-looking Stereolabs ZED stereo camera inclined
slightly downward (15 degrees with respect to the horizontal
plane), a Lidar-lite v3 laser range finder and two OpenMV
H7 Plus monocular cameras, one forward-looking and one
downward-looking. The Robot Operating System (ROS) was
employed as middleware on the UAV and the ground control
station.
It is worth noting that laser scanners were explicitly
banned by the rules and, thus, only vision-based sensors
were allowed for navigation in the indoor GNSS-denied
environment of the competition. In particular, the stereo
camera is used for visual odometry computation and its
output is fused with the Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU)
information (available on the Pixhawk 4) for obtaining an
estimate of drone pose in the odom frame. In order to
enhance the accuracy of the altitude estimation, the range
finder was employed as an altimeter. The downward-looking
monocular camera was exploited for localization purposes

IV. DETAILED SOLUTION
In this section, the proposed solution for the contest is
presented in detail. The overall architecture is composed by
three main subsystems:
• Navigation. It takes as inputs the sensor measurements
and returns as outputs the drone pose in the map frame
and a global map.
• Decision-making. It manages the high-level objectives
of the mission. It takes as inputs preliminary informa-
tion on the environment and outputs the desired goal
positions.
• Planning and control. The global map and the goal
positions are used for planning collision free paths and
trajectories to be fed to the drone controller.
A. Navigation
The visual odometry algorithm employed is provided in
the ZED stereo camera SDK2. This choice has been driven
by the fact that, considering the available hardware, i.e., with
GPU acceleration, this algorithm outperforms other open-
source algorithms with CPU implementations. The position
and yaw output of the visual odometry are fused in the
PX43 firmware estimator, i.e., an Extended Kalman Filter
called EKF2. The EKF2 returns the position and attitude of
the UAV in the odom frame, pB/O = [px, py, pz]⊤and RB/O
respectively, which will be used for feedback control.
For what concerns the localization, a Kalman Filter (KF),
which fuses information coming from drone’s odometry,
monocular cameras images and laser altimeter, has been
implemented. The state of the filter is the bias term bM/O =
[bx,by,bz]⊤that is the difference between the position of the
robot in the odom reference frame and the position in the
map reference frame. Assuming the evolution of the bias as

B. Decision-making
The decision-making module is responsible for assigning
the waypoints to the planning and control module. In particu-
lar, the waypoints are computed through different approaches
based on the output of a state machine, which manages the
different phases of the competition. The workflow of the
state machine and associated inputs are shown in Figure 6
and described in the following.
When the operator sends the starting signal, the decision-
making module provides a takeoff setpoint. After having
completed the takeoff operation, a probabilistic exploration
algorithm is used for steering the UAV towards the area of the

The probabilities are updated based on ground regions
seen by the three cameras (the forward monocular camera,
the downward-looking camera and the fixed surveillance
camera). If no ArUco corresponding to the ground robots
is found in the images, the cells are labelled as already
explored and their probability is decreased, while if an
ArUco corresponding to a collaborative robot is detected, the
probabilities associated with the adjacent cells are decreased
(based on the assumption that the robots will not be one next
to the other). Once the intruder robot has been found (either
by the drone cameras or the PTZ camera) the tracking phase
starts. In this state, the output waypoint will correspond to
the position of the ground robot at a pre-defined altitude,
adjusted to allow to read the reward string with the available
downward-looking monocular camera. At this point, two
alternatives exist:
1) the robot has been lost, i.e., it is outside the field of
view of the cameras. In this condition the state machine
will resort to a probabilistic exploration phase in the
neighbourhood of the drone position.
2) the robot is in the field of view and a readable picture
of the reward string has been taken. In this case, the
operator will decide the landing sequence to follow in
order to maximize the reward considering remaining
drone endurance and the probability of success of
each landing (this could be also computed through an
optimization process on the drone hardware). The output
is a sequence of waypoints in the initial approximate
world map of the considered landing pad.

The planning module is meant for generating collision-

First, a global planner generates a path from the current
position of the drone to the given waypoint. To this aim, it
exploits the knowledge of the world approximated map with
a coarse resolution (1 m). The planner is a 2D implementa-
tion of the Djikstra shortest path algorithm. Successively, the
generated path is pruned for removing the collinear points.

Finally, the generated position set-points are sent to the
UAV controller. Namely, the available implementation in the

VI. CONCLUSION
In this paper, the winning solution of the second edition of
the Leonardo Drone Contest, an autonomous drone competi-
tion, has been presented. In particular, the aim of the contest,
its rules and objectives have been described. Successively, the
authors presented the platform specifically designed for the
competition, with particular focus on the equipped sensors
and software architecture. Then, the modular architecture,
comprising navigation, planning and decision making mod-
ules has been discussed in details. Finally, the performance
achieved during the last round of the competition have been
reported along with the contest results.
