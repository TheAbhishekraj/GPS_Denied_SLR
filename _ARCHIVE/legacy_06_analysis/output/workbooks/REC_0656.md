ABSTRACT The deployment of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) in tasks such as urban surveillance,
disaster assessment, and logistics necessitates enhanced autonomous navigation capabilities. Traditionally,
the localization of UAVs relies on Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS). However, in urban canyons,
indoor environments, or bad weather conditions, GNSS signals are prone to obstruction and interference,
leading to degraded navigation performance. To overcome the limitations, the development of GNSS-denied
alternative localization techniques has become an important topic. In this paper we present an UAV visual
localization and heading estimation in GNSS-denied environments by matching the UAV overhead imagery
with satellite maps. The proposed NavCLIP constructs an image database using satellite data and retrieves
the best-matched by querying with aerial images. A homography matrix is then employed to estimate the
UAV location and yaw angle. Due to the lack of publicly available datasets, this work collects an aerial and
satellite image dataset (UAV-Satellite Dataset) for training and validation. The experimental results show
strong localization performance on the Google Earth dataset, while UAV flight experiments exhibit larger
localization errors under the real-world conditions. In addition, the proposed technique improves over the
GeoCLIP baseline and achieves low heading estimation error. Code and dataset are available on GitHub at
https://github.com/PHChenGit/NavCLIP

I. INTRODUCTION
Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have been deployed
across a wide range of application scenarios, including
military reconnaissance, agricultural monitoring, emergency
responses, and infrastructure inspection. The abilities to
hover and maneuver at low altitude in constrained space
makes lightweight multirotor platforms particularly attractive
for these tasks. In many outdoor missions, the UAV naviga-
tion usually relies on the Global Navigation Satellite System
(GNSS) due to its low payload burden, cost efficiency,
and widespread availability. However, GNSS signals are
susceptible to intentional interference and might become
unavailable under environmental occlusion. The scenarios
are commonly considered as GNSS-denied environments,

Due to challenges of multi-sensor integration, recent stud-

on the images captured by onboard cameras to perceive the

imagery and satellite maps using computer vision algorithms
or deep neural networks to derive UAV pose and geographic
location. This offers clear cost advantages and substantially
reduces system complexity. In addition, it can mitigate error
accumulation introduced by cross-sensor integration, making
visual navigation a promising alternative for GNSS-denied
environments [1].
Despite the benefits, the UAV visual navigation also faces
inherent difficulties. Rapidly changing outdoor illumination,
seasonal transition, and dynamic objects such as vehicles can
significantly affect the perception and processing. A common
and effective strategy is to treat the collected satellite imagery
as a large-scale database and match real-time UAV images to
infer the geographic location. Although this multi-modal and
cross-view matching paradigm has broad potential, it remains
challenging in practice due to the discrepancy with the
low-altitude UAV images. The viewpoint, scale, and level of
detail can be substantially different, and the mismatch often
leads to incorrect correspondences when using traditional
feature-based methods.
In this paper, we propose NavCLIP, a CLIP-based visual
localization framework that integrates MapGlue [2] for UAV
heading estimation in GNSS-denied environments. It consists
of two modules, for localization and orientation, respectively.
For efficient retrieval under the hardware constraints, satellite
maps are partitioned into tiles to form an image database.
The localization module, inspired by GeoCLIP, employs
a CLIP-like architecture to align geo-coordinates with
satellite maps via contrastive learning, and retrieves the
most relevant tile for given UAV queries based on the
cosine similarity [3]. The orientation module then applies
MapGlue for establishing the correspondences between the
retrieved tile and query images, and estimates homography
via RANSAC. The heading angle derived from homography
is combined with the localization output to provide a unified
estimate of location and heading.
For training and evaluation, we recorded real-world flights
with a camera mounted on the UAV in a nadir-facing config-
uration. Each image is paired with its corresponding satellite
tile and precise geographic coordinates. We also constructed
a large-scale UAV-to-satellite matching dataset, covering
urban and campus scenes and providing geo-coordinates
with ground-truth heading direction. The dataset serves as
benchmarking for cross-view localization for GNSS-denied
UAV navigation. The contributions of this paper are as
follows.
• A vision system combining retrieval-based localiza-
tion with feature-based orientation is proposed for
GNSS-denied UAV navigation.
• Using a single camera, the proposed method achieves
strong localization performance on the Google Earth
dataset and improves upon GeoCLIP in real-world UAV
flight experiments.
• A large-scale UAV-satellite image matching dataset
with precise geo-coordinates and ground-truth heading
is established for training and validation, and made
available publicly.
II. RELATED WORK
Autonomous navigation and localization for UAVs in
GNSS-denied environments remains a critical challenge [4].
In such scenarios, a navigation stack must provide globally
consistent position estimates for task planning, safety, and
long-horizon autonomy. Recently, visual localization has
attracted increasing attention, serving either as a primary
positioning source or complement to other onboard sensors.
In particular, cross-view localization estimates the UAV’s
pose by incorporating onboard imagery with geo-referenced
satellite maps. Satellite images providing wide coverage
with an explicit geographic reference are suitable for global
positioning when GNSS is unreliable or unavailable. Many
techniques adopt a two-stage pipeline: first performing
retrieval over a satellite database to obtain a set of regions,
followed by refining the estimates via local alignment by
image-level correspondences [5].
The difficulty of cross-view localization is the domain
gap between viewpoints. UAV imagery is usually captured
at low altitude with oblique or near-ground views, whereas
satellite imagery is typically nadir and orthorectified. This
mismatch induces large differences in scale and content,
which affects correspondences of local structures. In addition
to geometric discrepancy, the appearance variation caused
by illumination, weather, and long-term change further
complicates matching by altering textures, shadows, and
scene layouts. The existing methods follow two directions,
one integrating complementary sensors to provide motion
priors and reduce ambiguity, and the other focusing on
learning visual representations to improve invariance to
viewpoint and appearance changes [6].
A. UAV VISUAL LOCALIZATION
Visual localization methods can be broadly categorized
into relative and absolute approaches [7], [8], [9]. The
relative localization, including VO, VIO, and visual SLAM,
estimate UAV trajectories by tracking features across several
image frames. These techniques are suited for short-term
motion estimation because they exploit temporal continuity
and operate at high frequency. A significant drawback,
however, is their susceptibility to the cumulative drifts,
which can lead to unbounded position errors over long-range
flights.

To mitigate this issue, absolute localization techniques aim
to register the UAV by matching its current view with a geo-

global data, so that map alignment can provide intermittent
corrections to the bound drift. Conte et al. [10] fused
VIO with satellite imagery to for drift correction, and pre-

estimation. In [11], Mao et al. developed a visual-inertial
system to register VIO outputs with a 3D pointcloud via
a pose graph network, effectively suppressing drift through
global constraints. Other approaches leverage map features
to reduce the accumulated error, including Hao et al. [12]
building on VINS-Mono [13], and RVIO [14]. Beyond
visual cues, non-vision sensors have also been integrated
to constrain drift. Delama et al. proposed UVIO, which
incorporated Ultra-Wideband (UWB) anchors to bound VIO
drift [15].
While effective, these fusion-based systems can be
complex in both implementation and deployments. Their
performance often depends on the quality and coverage of
available maps, as well as the reliabilities of the underlying
odometry between global corrections. Moreover, achieving
robust performance typically requires careful system design,
including consistent reference frames and stable estimation
in various operating conditions.
B. DEEP LEARNING FOR CROSS-VIEW LOCALIZATION
More recently, deep learning-based techniques have become
the paradigm for cross-view localization, directly addressing
the challenges of large viewpoint differences and appear-
ance variations. Many of these approaches learn compact
descriptors and formulate localization as retrieval in a
shared embedding space, where the goal is to maximize
similarity between matched cross-view pairs while separating
mismatched pairs. Compared with hand-crafted descriptors,
learned representations can capture higher-level cues, and
achieve stronger invariance to nuisance factors that frequently
occur in outdoor environments.
Early deep neural networks commonly combined learned
features with geometric or semantic cues to reduce ambiguity.
Tian et al. [16] introduced a framework using Faster R-CNN
to detect buildings as semantic features, and estimated
the location by matching the detected structures with a
geo-referenced database. This line of work leverages the
intuition that semantic entities can be more stable across
viewpoints than low-level textures, especially under the
local appearance changes. In a different approach which
combines geometry with classic features, Viswanathan et al.
proposed warping ground-level panoramic images into a
top-down perspective and matching with satellite imagery
using SIFT descriptors [17]. Such methods highlight the
value of injecting geometric structure to narrow the cross-
view gap, although the robustness still depends on the
warping quality, landmark visibility, and the reliability of
matching.
Later deep networks shifted toward end-to-end learning
of discriminative embeddings that support efficient retrieval.
A notable example from Zhou et al. linked ground-level
images to OpenStreetMap tiles by learning shared embed-
ding spaces [18]. This learned representation proved more
effective than binary semantic descriptors, enabling faster
and more accurate localization. In general, embedding-based
retrieval offers favorable scalability, since candidate regions
can be indexed and compared efficiently. Nevertheless,
retrieval alone might be insufficient when multiple satellite
regions exhibit similar appearance, motivating complemen-
tary mechanisms such as local verification, re-ranking,
or geometry-aware refinement.
The evolution of network architectures has advanced per-
formance by improving how spatial contexts and cross-view
correspondence are modeled. To better capture spatial layout,

C. FEATURE MATCHING AND HOMOGRAPHY ESTIMATION
The classical feature-based pipelines, such as SIFT and
ORB, estimate the image-to-image homography from sparse
correspondences. If there exist sufficient repeatable key-
points, the homography estimation provides a principled way
to enforce geometric consistency and supports downstream
tasks such as alignment, verification, or orientation inference.
However, reliability often degrades under cross-view settings
and large appearance gaps, where the local descriptors
might produce ambiguous or incorrect matches, and visible
overlap between views can be limited. These issues motivate
learning-based matching, which is more tolerant to viewpoint
and radiometric changes.
Mughal et al. [22] projected the UAV image onto a
satellite map to infer both position and orientation from map
coordinates, illustrating how the geometric alignments can
directly yield the navigation output. Cao et al. introduced the
Iterative Homography Network (IHN) [23], that employed a
trainable iterative refinement scheme. It was later extended it
to the Recursive Homography Warping Framework (RHWF)
[24] using hierarchical attention for improving accuracy
and parameter efficiency compared with LocalTrans [25].
For the detector-free matching, GeoFormer [26] built on
LoFTR [27] by incorporating RANSAC geometric cues to
focus attention. Beyond single-modality image matching,
specialized cross-view settings were explored, including
thermal-visual UAV-satellite matching via image retrieval
and domain adaptation, and BEV (bird-eye view) based
alignment for the geometric consistency improvement.
D. CLIP-BASED METHODOLOGY
In recent works, Radford et al. [28] introduced CLIP, which
aligns image and text representations via contrastive learning
on large-scale data. This representation is transferable across
tasks since the contrastive objective encourages the semantic
grouping and robust features beyond narrow domain-specific
supervision. Follow-up studies, such as OpenCLIP [29] and
MetaCLIP [30], further highlighted the importance of data
quality and scale for representation learning. It indicates that
the effectiveness of CLIP-style models is closely tied to the
breadth and cleanliness of the pretraining corpus. Beyond the
general vision-language tasks, CLIP contrastive pre-training
has been adopted in remote sensing and geo-localization to
align the visual observation with geographic signal, enabling
retrieval and localization through a shared embedding space.

III. METHODOLOGY
A. SYSTEM OVERVIEW
We propose an UAV-satellite visual navigation approach
that leverages wide-area satellite imagery with aerial images
captured by an UAV. Inspired by GeoCLIP [3], its core
contains a localization module which integrates an image
encoder with the location encoder. The image features
and geo-coordinate embeddings are then projected to a
common latent space for retrieval-based geo-localization.
Finally, MapGlue [2] is used to establish the image-level
correspondences and estimate the UAV heading angle from
the geometric alignment.
The training and inference pipelines are shown in Figures 1
and 2, respectively. During the training phase, the localization
module is optimized using the paired drone and satellite
images. An image encoder first extracts visual features from
both images. Concurrently, a location encoder generates
positional embeddings from their corresponding geographic
coordinates. These feature embeddings are then projected
into a shared latent space and aligned using contrastive
learning. The process is to maximize the feature similarity
for drone-satellite pairs while minimize for non-matching
pairs. At the inference stage, the proposed NavCLIP takes a
single, query drone image as input. It first predicts the UAV
geographic coordinates and retrieves the most likely satellite
image from a database. The aerial and satellite images are
then fed into MapGlue to find the heading angle.
B. IMAGE ENCODER
The image encoder serves as a foundational component of
NavCLIP, extracting robust and discriminative feature repre-
sentations from both UAV and satellite imagery. To leverage
general-purpose knowledge from visual cues, we employ the
pre-trained Vision Transformer (ViT-L/14) as our backbone
for feature extraction. This network is based on CLIP and
has exceptional ability to learn semantically rich embeddings
from vast, diverse image-text corpora. It is highly beneficial
for bridging the significant domain gap between aerial and
satellite views.

B. UAV POSE ESTIMATION

size of 512. Several core modifications are introduced to the
configuration to enhance the stability and performance. First,
we set the initial learning rate to 0.0016 and use the AdamW
optimizer. Unlike the conventional optimizer, which couples
the L2 regularization with the adaptive learning rate, AdamW
decouples weight decay from the gradient update. It prevents
the regularization from being influenced by adaptive learning
rates, leading to more stable and effective control over model
complexity. This approach is able to improve the robustness

For orientation estimation, we define the north-up satellite
map as reference. The yaw angle θyaw ∈(+180◦, −180◦] of
the image simulates the UAV’s heading. Following successful
localization, the yaw angle is estimated by first establishing
keypoint correspondences between the query image Iuav and
the retrieved satellite patch Isat. The homography matrix is
then computed from the matches to extract the yaw angle.
As shown in Table 2, our method achieves a mean error of
0.2 degrees, which indicates that it can estimate the yaw angle
in feature-rich environments accurately.

images. As presented in Table 3, the model maintains robust
performance under geometric affine transformations, with

MAE of 0.13◦. This indicates that the model exhibits
high robustness in both the localization and heading angle
estimation. From the Table 3, we can observe that the RMSE

levels, but a few outliers with large errors increase the

catastrophic matching failures. Moreover, the low yaw angle

D. ABLATION STUDY
Ablation experiments are conducted to validate the effective-
ness of the proposed localization method. First, the inference
is performed using only the GeoCLIP pretrained model. It is
followed by testing on different backbone models, including
SigLIP2 [32]. We then investigate the impact of architectural
variations, including using only self-attention (SA) in the
image encoder, and replacing the ReLU activation with
GELU or Swish in the image and location encoders. The
experiment results are tabulated in Table 5.
For the ablation study on heading angle estimation, several
networks with cross-view image inputs are compared, and
the best one is employed as our heading angle prediction
model. The dataset used in this study is collected from the
DJI Drone Route 1, where each input contains an UAV
aerial image and a corresponding satellite image retrieved
from the predicted location. Both images are resized to
224×224 to match the input requirements of our localization
module. As the results tabulated in Table 6, the methods such
as SuperGlue [33] and LightGlue [34] fail to estimate the
heading angle under cross-view settings. The experiments
indicate that those matching-based approaches can only
infer meaningful rotation angles when the true rotation is
within ±10◦.
E. FAILURE CASES AND LIMITATIONS
The failure cases are mainly observed in visually ambiguous
regions, such as intersections partially occluded by the trees,
areas with repetitive building patterns, and scenes with lim-
ited distinctive structures. In these cases, the retrieval stage
may return a nearby but incorrect satellite tile, which further
affects the subsequent homography estimation for heading
prediction. Dynamic scene changes, lighting differences, and
seasonal appearance variations might also reduce cross-view
consistency between the UAV image and satellite references.
Some possible mitigation strategies include stronger domain
adaptation, model training with more and diverse real-flight
imagery, multi-scale retrieval refinement, and incorporating
temporal consistency across consecutive UAV frames.
V. CONCLUSION
This paper addresses UAV navigation in GNSS-denied
environments by presenting a deep neural network for
localization and heading angle estimation. Our proposed
end-to-end NavCLIP architecture takes aerial images and
satellite maps as input for feature matching and UAV’s pose
estimation. The localization module utilizes a CLIP-based
image encoder and a multi-scale coordinate encoder for
constructing a similarity matrix for robust feature matching.
To validate the feasibility and effectiveness of our approach,
we conduct experiments on controlled and real-world
datasets, with the tests across multiple geographic regions and
time periods to assess spatial and temporal generalization.
The experimental results demonstrate strong localization and
heading estimation performance on the controlled Google
Earth dataset. In the real-world UAV flight experiments,
the localization error is notably larger, indicating that the
domain discrepancy between UAV imagery and satellite
maps remains a major challenge for practical deployment.
Nevertheless, the proposed method consistently improves
over the GeoCLIP baseline in real-world evaluation.
