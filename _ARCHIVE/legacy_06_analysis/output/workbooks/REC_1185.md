A B S T R A C T
With the widespread use of Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) in urban environments, achieving high-precision 
localization, particularly in Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS)-denied environments, presents a signif­
icant challenge. Existing localization methods largely rely on centralized data processing, which often leads to 
issues such as information redundancy, high computational complexity, and limited real-time responsiveness. 
This paper proposes a UAV swarm collaborative localization strategy, termed I-ACPM, based on Imperialist 
Competitive Mechanism-based Clustering (ICMC) and the Ant Colony Pheromone Mechanism (ACPM). The ICMC 
is employed for multi-stage clustering of the UAV swarm, enhancing communication efficiency and reducing 
information redundancy. Meanwhile, the ACPM is used to fuse localization information both within and between 
clusters, improving system robustness and localization accuracy. Simulation results demonstrate that I-ACPM 
significantly enhances localization accuracy, particularly in low-noise environments, with performance 
approaching the Cram´er-Rao Lower Bound (CRLB). Compared to the traditional Maximum Likelihood Estimation 
(MLE) algorithm, I-ACPM offers substantial improvements in both accuracy and interference resistance.
1. Introduction
In the past decade, the development and application of Unmanned 
Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) have expanded from traditional military do­
mains to a wide range of civil sectors, including aerial photography, 
communication, and disaster relief. UAVs, functioning as flying base 
stations, offer distinct advantages in these fields [1]. With urbanization 
accelerating, urban environments are increasingly characterized by 
towering buildings, dense transportation networks, and intricate to­
pographies. These features present significant challenges to UAV oper­
ations. Despite these difficulties, UAVs continue to provide flexible 
perspectives and rapid response capabilities in urban environments, 
facilitating tasks such as logistics delivery, service inspection, and 
emergency response [2]. In particular, in the commercial delivery 
sector, UAVs have shown considerable potential, enabling direct de­
liveries from sorting centers to end users. Moreover, in the postal service 
sector, UAVs have been explored for delivering essential goods, such as 
medicine, to remote areas, demonstrating their substantial advantages 
in enhancing delivery efficiency and expanding coverage [3]. However, 
the complexity of urban structures imposes higher demands on the 
positioning accuracy and reliability of UAVs. In such environments, 
relying on Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) solutions for 
precise navigation becomes a major challenge due to issues like signal 
attenuation, reflection from buildings, and electromagnetic interfer­
ence, all of which degrade positioning accuracy [4]. These factors 
significantly hinder the effective deployment of UAVs in urban envi­
ronments. As a result, ensuring accurate and low-latency positioning 
information for each UAV is essential for the successful implementation 
of UAV-based applications.
Currently, extensive research has been conducted on UAV localiza­
tion methods. Murata et al. in [5] proposed a single-UAV wave source 
localization scheme that estimates the Direction of Arrival (DOA) of the 
signal and uses Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) to achieve ac­
curate wave source localization in Non-Line-of-Sight (NLOS)

efficiently.
Despite significant progress in distributed localization, machine 
learning, and swarm intelligence algorithms, traditional methods still 
face challenges in maintaining high-precision positioning, particularly 
in NLOS and GNSS-denied environments. These issues arise due to fac­
tors like dense buildings, strong electromagnetic interference, and se­
vere signal attenuation in complex urban settings [31]. Additionally, 
many existing strategies demand substantial computational resources 
and real-time communication, which limits their applicability under 
communication and observation range constraints. To overcome these 
challenges, this paper proposes a cooperative localization strategy for 
UAV swarms, termed I-ACPM, based on Imperialist Competitive 
Mechanism-based Clustering (ICMC) and the Ant Colony Pheromone 
Mechanism (ACPM). This approach leverages the AOA and TDOA in­
formation received by UAVs and uses effective clustering to achieve 
precise relative positioning, even with limited communication and 
observation range.

3. UAV swarm cooperative localization method
3.1. Multi-stage self-organizing clustering design based on the imperialist 
competitive mechanism
This paper proposes a multi-stage self-organizing clustering method 
based on the Imperialist Competitive Mechanism, which models the 
clustering process of UAV communication networks as an expansion and 
competition among empires, as shown in Table 1. In this model, the UAV 
communication network is abstracted as a world composed of multiple 
nations, where some nations succeed in the competition and become 
empires, while others are reduced to colonies. Through this clustering 
approach, the communication efficiency within the UAV network is 
optimized, and the amount of information processing is significantly 
reduced.
This paper presents a multi-stage self-organizing clustering process 
based on the ICMC, comprising four distinct stages. The roles and 
functionalities of the nodes within the resulting backbone structure are 
depicted in Fig. 3.

enable all UAVs to compete for the role of cluster head, simulating the

with a larger number of neighboring UAVs to become cluster heads.

3.3. Complexity analysis
The computational complexity of the proposed I-ACPM is analyzed 
as follows. For the ICMC algorithm, the dominant cost lies in the cluster 
head selection stage, which requires sorting the neighbor sets and 
evaluating the cluster utility function VCO for all candidate strategies. 
This results in an overall complexity of O(N2), where N is the number of 
UAVs. The subsequent stages, including cluster member assignment, 
bridge node selection, and cluster extension, each contribute O(N2) or 
lower, and do not increase the overall order.
For the ACPM within each cluster of Nz RSUs, the initial PLE estimate 
requires O(Nz) operations. For RSUs under NLOS conditions, the SDP- 
based position correction employs an interior-point method with per- 
cluster complexity O(TSDP ⋅ Nz
3.5),where TSDP is the number of barrier 
iterations. For RSUs under LOS conditions, the MLE gradient descent 
requires O(Titer ⋅ Nz) operations. The pheromone propagation stage in­
volves pairwise checks among UAVs; under the limited communication 
range rc, the effective complexity reduces to O(N ⋅ |ρ|), where |ρ| is the 
average neighborhood size. Since each cluster independently processes 
only its local subset of Nz RSUs rather than the full network of N nodes, 
the per-cluster complexity of O(TSDP ⋅ Nz
3.5) ensures that the algorithm 
remains computationally tractable as the swarm scales.

at all time instants and exceeding SMDS-CFG at the majority of the 
evaluated instants. As shown in Fig. 6(c) and 6(f), SMDS-CFG forms 
disjoint clusters with no shared members, requiring all inter-cluster 
communication to be routed through cluster heads to the ground sta­
tion; this centralized relay topology, combined with the uneven cluster 
sizes observed in Fig. 7(b), creates communication bottlenecks. In 
contrast, ICMC eliminates the dependence on ground infrastructure 
through bridge nodes, enabling direct inter-cluster communication.
4.3. Localization performance analysis
To address the concern of whether the communication-efficient 
clustering also supports favorable localization geometry, Fig. 9 pre­
sents the log
10-transformed geometric dilution of precision (GDOP) of 
the RSU set within the GDU's cluster for ICMC, HEED, and SMDS-CFG at 
15 sampling time instants from 2 s to 30 s. For bearing-only localization, 
GDOP quantifies the geometric diversity of RSU angular observations 
around the GDU, where a lower value indicates more uniformly 
distributed RSUs and better-conditioned position estimation [41,42]. 
The ICMC design promotes favorable localization geometry through 
three structural properties. Specifically, Stage 1 selects UAVs with the 
largest neighbor counts as cluster heads, which tend to occupy spatially 
central positions and attract members from diverse directions. The 
CNLOS(m) penalty further excludes NLOS-affected nodes from cluster 
head roles, favoring LOS-dominant configurations. Stage 4 prevents 
node isolation, ensuring that the GDU's cluster consistently maintains 
sufficient RSUs for well-conditioned localization. As shown in Fig. 9, 
ICMC achieves the lowest GDOP at all evaluated time instants, with a 
geometric mean of 239 m/rad, compared to 355 m/rad for HEED (49% 
higher) and 454 m/rad for SMDS-CFG (90% higher). These results 
confirm that the ICMC clustering structure simultaneously achieves su­
perior communication efficiency and supports well-conditioned bear­
ing-only localization geometry.

4.4. Computational complexity validation
Fig. 14 illustrates the average computation time per localization step 
for PLE, WPLE, MLE, I-ACPM, and SMDS-CFG as a function of the 
number of RSUs, ranging from 4 to 20. PLE achieves the lowest 
computation time across all evaluated configurations, with an average of 
0.017 ms, owing to its non-iterative closed-form structure. WPLE also 
exhibits low overhead, with an average computation time of 0.024 ms. 
The computation time of MLE ranges from 0.33 ms to 0.45 ms, with an 
average of 0.396 ms, consistent with its iterative gradient descent pro­
cedure. I-ACPM requires 0.46–0.53 ms, with an average of 0.506 ms, 
reflecting the additional NLOS detection, correction, and ACPM-based 
fusion steps. The SMDS-CFG baseline shows a comparable average 
computation time of 0.515 ms, mainly due to distance-matrix recon­
struction and range-based refinement. Although I-ACPM incurs slightly 
higher overhead than MLE, its computation time remains close to that of 
SMDS-CFG while achieving better localization accuracy. All methods 
operate within the sub-millisecond range, confirming the computational 
tractability of I-ACPM for real-time UAV swarm cooperative 
localization.
5. Conclusion
This paper proposes a UAV swarm cooperative localization strategy 
based on ICMC and ACPM, termed I-ACPM. By establishing clustering 
formation principles and an efficiency function, ICMC achieves stable 
clustering structures that outperform traditional HEED and SMDS-CFG 
algorithms, and effectively addresses edge node assignment issues 
through the introduction of bridging nodes, resulting in higher network 
communication efficiency. Based on this clustering framework, ACPM 
implements localization information fusion both within and between 
clusters. Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed I-ACPM 
effectively solves the multi-UAV localization problem in GNSS-denied 
urban environments, significantly outperforming traditional algo­
rithms such as PLE, WPLE, and MLE in terms of localization accuracy, 
especially under low-noise conditions, where the localization error ap­
proaches the theoretical CRLB. As the number of UAVs increases, I- 
ACPM's performance advantage becomes more pronounced. The key 
advantage of I-ACPM lies in its ability to rely on local observation in­
